This chapter is for you if you have built backend or distributed systems and want a Senior or Staff role
serving models, scheduling GPUs or running an ML platform. You get the loop shape, 13 ranked themes with what a
strong answer covers, a back-of-envelope sheet with worked numbers, Senior vs Staff signals and a lab-based
prep path. It is built from the 156 questions in the bank
tagged AI Infra or both tracks (as of 2026-10-11). The bank leans on two companies: Anthropic appears in
20% of these rows and NVIDIA in 13%, much of it from PracHub listings. Most rows rest on one anonymous
account or a prep site, so treat every company claim below as reported, not as policy.

## The role at Senior and Staff

AI Infra Engineers keep models running: inference serving, batching and scheduling, GPU fleets, weight
distribution, training infrastructure and the platform around them. The research's main finding is that
these loops mostly ask distributed-systems and concurrency questions in inference form: batch requests onto
a GPU, distribute a large file to thousands of machines, recover when a node dies mid-job. Anthropic's
careers page says "about half our technical staff had no prior ML experience" (<Cite id="s-131d7342e9" />).

Postings show the top-end depth. A Google DeepMind Staff inference posting asks for modifying serving
codebases, vLLM, TensorRT-LLM, SGLang or Dynamo, and modelling how batch size and utilization trade latency
against cost (<Cite id="s-8e2b61a5c2" />). An NVIDIA inference posting asks for CUDA or Triton, Nsight and
vLLM, SGLang or TensorRT-LLM contributions (<Cite id="s-98e1e160c1" />).

How much CUDA you need is disputed. One prep guide says Senior+ candidates should read or write Triton and
CUDA (<Cite id="s-f07fcd5321" />); another says you only need to know where Python ends and C++/CUDA begins
(<Cite id="s-f440daaea4" />). It likely depends on whether the role is kernel work or platform work. The
research found no first-hand report of a live CUDA or Triton kernel-writing round.

## Ranked themes

Themes are listed in the site's rank order, which comes from how many sourced rows touched each theme in

### 1. Batching and inference scheduling (12)

**What gets asked.** Design a batching layer for one GPU that takes up to 100 inputs per batch while each
caller waits for its result; follow-ups on how to return each response, when to flush a batch, failure points
and how to choose among several GPUs (Anthropic, high confidence, <Cite id="s-2348518c66" />,
<Cite id="s-51ab9fad72" />). Design ChatGPT-style serving with separate budgets for time to first token
(TTFT) and inter-token latency, a client that disconnects mid-reply, and a long shared system prompt
(OpenAI, aggregator, <Cite id="s-86dff7d564" />). Code a continuous-batching decode loop over a mock model
(xAI, aggregator, <Cite id="s-8d74591878" />).

**A strong answer** flushes on whichever comes first, a full batch or a wait deadline. It bounds the queue,
returns each result through a per-request future, handles timeouts and cancellation, and pushes back with an
explicit rejection when full. For generation it batches at the token level, refilling slots as sequences
finish, and treats KV-cache memory as the real batch limit (see the headroom formula below). A Baseten guide
adds: autoscale on queue depth or TTFT, not GPU utilization, and raise cold starts unprompted
(<Cite id="s-f440daaea4" />).

**Senior vs Staff.** The Staff inference candidate in one report believes pushing back on the interviewer's
"unrealistic assumptions" sank them; the interviewer wanted the model treated as a black box
(<Cite id="s-4e04a1689c" />). Accept the framing, then add depth on scheduling, queueing and failure.

**Labs.** <LabRef lab="llm-inference" inline /> has a real KV cache, but its batching is bookkeeping and it
does not stream tokens or measure TTFT. <LabRef lab="message-queue" inline /> and
<LabRef lab="rate-limiter" inline /> cover the queue and admission control in front of a batcher.
[Batching questions](/system-design-labs/interviews/questions/?track=ai-infra&theme=batching-scheduling).

### 2. Overload, failure and fault tolerance (5)

**What gets asked.** A training job on 500 nodes loses one: how fast do you detect it, do you restart or let
the cluster catch up, and do you even need 500 nodes (NVIDIA Senior, first-hand, <Cite id="s-79598dd069" />)?
Detect, drain and return GPUs with intermittent ECC errors across 5,000 GPUs (Fluidstack, prep site,
<Cite id="s-747377636a" />). Anthropic's design-doc review turned into failure handling and overload
protection (<Cite id="s-eab681003c" />).

**A strong answer** raises these before being asked: heartbeats and timeouts for detection, leases and
fencing so a stale worker cannot write, bounded retries with a budget, idempotent requests, load shedding by
priority, and how much work a checkpoint restart loses. One guide says reliability counts for more than
"exotic architecture" (<Cite id="s-5f44d8637a" />). Staff answers challenge the requirement itself, as the
500-node follow-up invites.

**Labs.** <LabRef lab="gossip" inline /> for failure detection, <LabRef lab="load-balancer" inline /> for health
checks, circuit breakers and retry budgets, <LabRef lab="dist-lock" inline /> for leases and fencing tokens.
[Overload questions](/system-design-labs/interviews/questions/?track=ai-infra&theme=overload-failure).

### 3. Practical concurrent coding (23)

**What gets asked.** Convert sampled stack traces into an execution trace, with a follow-up that removes one
constraint (Anthropic, high confidence, <Cite id="s-4e04a1689c" />, <Cite id="s-42f1c7f6dd" />).
Write a web crawler, then make it multithreaded; that candidate ran out of time (<Cite id="s-2348518c66" />).
Build a cache for container-image chunks that coalesces two simultaneous misses (Modal, prep site,
<Cite id="s-86890a1069" />). A thread-safe durable event writer shared by thousands of threads (Databricks,
aggregator, <Cite id="s-6db00549b3" />).

**A strong answer** gets a correct single-threaded version running fast, then adds concurrency deliberately:
what each lock protects, a bounded worker pool, single-flight coalescing, cancellation, and tests that build
their own fixtures. Rejected Anthropic candidates point to unfinished tasks and follow-ups, not wrong first answers. Yuan Meng
writes that without a quick insight "I never get a 'strong yes'", that needing hints can still reach a "weak
yes", and that panicking while debugging is "a strong signal for rejection" (<Cite id="s-598c128e3b" />).

**Labs.** <LabRef lab="wal" inline /> builds group commit, the trade-off behind the Databricks batch-write
round. <LabRef lab="cdn-edge" inline /> builds request coalescing and stale-if-error (its tests pass as of 2026-10-13).
<LabRef lab="io-model" inline /> covers blocking versus event-driven I/O.
[Concurrency questions](/system-design-labs/interviews/questions/?track=ai-infra&theme=concurrent-coding).

### 4. Parallelism, collectives and sharding math (9)

**What gets asked.** Estimate FFN compute, memory traffic and communication under three sharding schemes,
and explain why FFN-dimension sharding needs an output reduction (Anthropic, aggregator,
<Cite id="s-1ffbee39ea" />). Explain data, tensor and pipeline parallelism and why column- and row-parallel
layers alternate (Amazon, aggregator, <Cite id="s-9936fcb114" />). Serve a 70B model on 4×A100-80GB with
tensor parallelism of 2 or 4 (prep site, <Cite id="s-f07fcd5321" />).

**A strong answer** says what each GPU holds, which collective runs, and how many bytes it moves (the ring
formula below). For the 70B question: at TP=2 each GPU holds 140 ÷ 2 = 70 GB of BF16 weights, leaving about
10 GB of 80 for KV cache and activations. At TP=4 it is 35 GB per GPU, so more cache room and a shorter per-token
decode time (17.2 ms against 34.3 ms from the bandwidth bound below), paid for with an all-reduce per layer
across four GPUs. No lab covers parallelism yet
(planned as `training-parallelism`); <LabRef lab="transformer" inline /> gives you the layer shapes.
[Parallelism questions](/system-design-labs/interviews/questions/?track=ai-infra&theme=parallelism-sharding).

### 5. GPU fleet scheduling and fairness (12)

**What gets asked.** One 8-GPU node serves a large model that needs all 8 GPUs and a small one that needs 1:
design routing and placement, then handle one failed GPU in the group and small-model traffic starving the
large one (Anthropic, aggregator, <Cite id="s-62f6a22994" />). A text-to-video backend as a GPU job
scheduler with leases, fencing, bounded retries and a paid tier (OpenAI, aggregator, <Cite id="s-725e58e60a" />).
A GPU-aware pod scheduler: best-fit versus first-fit and fragmentation (Together, aggregator,
<Cite id="s-3a3d9c8dba" />).

**A strong answer** covers gang scheduling (a multi-GPU job gets all its GPUs or none), packing versus
spreading, fragmentation, topology (which GPUs share fast links), fair-share queues with priority and
preemption, and quotas. Staff answers price the trade-offs, such as when a customer with a tail-latency
requirement gets a dedicated endpoint instead of the shared pool (<Cite id="s-9597947584" />).

**Labs.** <LabRef lab="container-orchestrator" inline /> has a reconcile loop and first-fit scheduling on CPU
and memory, with no GPU resource type or gang scheduling; adding those is the exercise. GPU scheduling and
multi-LoRA serving are gaps (planned as `gpu-scheduler` and `multi-lora-serving`).
[Scheduling questions](/system-design-labs/interviews/questions/?track=ai-infra&theme=gpu-fleet-scheduling).

### 6. Weight distribution, cold start and model residency (6)

**What gets asked.** Distribute one large file to thousands of machines over bandwidth-limited links
(Anthropic Staff, first-hand, <Cite id="s-e9fee3985a" />). Design a chunk-based model download pipeline:
chunk size, pipeline versus tree fan-out, recovery with and without a coordinator (Blind, first-hand,
<Cite id="s-e98cbef9cd" />). Keep a warm pool and make the second cold start of a 40 GB model fast (Modal,
prep site, <Cite id="s-86890a1069" />).

**A strong answer** starts with numbers. With one source sending 140 GB of weights to 1,000 machines over an
assumed 10 Gbit/s (1.25 GB/s) link, the time is 1,000 × 140 ÷ 1.25 = 112,000 s, about 31 hours. If every
receiver forwards chunks onward, as in a pipelined chain or a peer-to-peer swarm, the total approaches one
copy's time, 140 ÷ 1.25 = 112 s, plus a per-hop term. Then add checksums per chunk, a local disk cache,
staged rollout and rollback.

**Labs.** <LabRef lab="gossip" inline />, <LabRef lab="cdn-edge" inline /> (coalescing and stale-while-revalidate),
and <LabRef lab="faas-runtime" inline />, whose cold start is a simulated sleep, so it teaches the warm-pool
logic, not load times. [Cold-start questions](/system-design-labs/interviews/questions/?track=ai-infra&theme=weights-coldstart).

### 7. Rate limits, credits and quotas (3)

All three rows are low-confidence snippets: a distributed rate limiter for an LLM API (OpenAI,
<Cite id="s-3b9dae2c4c" />), a GPU credit system (<Cite id="s-5fa409d1c2" />), compound limits on one request

front, so you reserve an estimate and reconcile when the request finishes. <LabRef lab="rate-limiter" inline />

### 8. Implement attention and the KV cache (11)

**What gets asked.** Find the bugs in a few hundred lines of transformer code, then add KV caching (OpenAI
Research Engineer, first-hand, <Cite id="s-7fa103b073" />). Implement multi-head attention, then a KV cache
and grouped-query attention in the same hour (Anthropic, one Blind comment, <Cite id="s-a129aa6451" />).
Masked multi-head self-attention from scratch (Meta and Apple, aggregator, <Cite id="s-be19e40ea6" />,
<Cite id="s-0630fd2aff" />).

**A strong answer** writes the shapes at every step, applies the causal mask, subtracts the row max before
softmax, appends to the cache along the sequence axis, and implements GQA by sharing each KV head across a
group of query heads. Test against a reference implementation. Labs: <LabRef lab="transformer" inline /> and
<LabRef lab="llm-inference" inline />.
[Attention questions](/system-design-labs/interviews/questions/?track=ai-infra&theme=attention-kv-impl).

### 9. Roofline and performance reasoning (7)

**What gets asked.** Performance-model a sharded matmul: FLOPs, bytes, network traffic, the ridge point, the
small-M decode case, and when K-sharding becomes network-bound (Anthropic, aggregator,
<Cite id="s-5198fc4992" />). Why is decode memory-bound and prefill compute-bound (prep site,
<Cite id="s-55d540eda9" />)? The sheet below works these through. No lab measures a GPU.
[Roofline questions](/system-design-labs/interviews/questions/?track=ai-infra&theme=roofline-perf).

### 11. Design-doc critique (2)

Review a design doc for a batch sampling service (Anthropic Staff, first-hand, <Cite id="s-eab681003c" />), or
critique a half-built inference design. The engineering-manager report says the emphasis was design
judgment, communication and review skills, not calculations (<Cite id="s-4e3dc29a95" />). Walk the request
path, find unbounded queues, missing back-pressure, retry storms and single points of failure, and rank what
you found by impact.

### 12. Quantization, speculative decoding and MoE (6)

Quantize an FP32 tensor to INT8 and back in NumPy, then add per-channel scales (NVIDIA, aggregator,
<Cite id="s-e6bac4f7b9" />). What does "671B total, 37B active" predict about serving cost
(<Cite id="s-9e6b455b13" />)? Why did throughput drop at high batch sizes after enabling speculative decoding
(<Cite id="s-4525fcd815" />)? Treat each as a trade-off with a measurable cost, not a buzzword
(<Cite id="s-f440daaea4" />). Labs: <LabRef lab="model-quantizer" inline /> and
<LabRef lab="speculative-decoding" inline />.

### 13. Training infrastructure and checkpointing (7)

Design a distributed training environment for a 1-trillion-parameter model; the candidate called it their
weakest round because the interviewer asked how they had benchmarked and debugged low GPU utilization and
which NVIDIA tools they had used (NVIDIA Senior, first-hand, <Cite id="s-7dd221b925" />). Move 200 TB of media
to a cloud GPU cluster in a few days (Apple, first-hand, <Cite id="s-8e08e84623" />): at 10 Gbit/s that is
200 × 10¹² × 8 ÷ 10¹⁰ = 160,000 s, about 1.85 days of line rate, so link speed decides between the network and
shipping disks. <LabRef lab="transformer" inline /> has training with checkpoints; sharded checkpoints are a gap
(planned as `sharded-checkpointing`).

## Back-of-envelope sheet

Estimation comes up mostly in the estimation-heavy PracHub items, such as Anthropic's sharded-matmul and FFN
questions. First-hand reports barely mention specific numbers, and the Anthropic engineering-manager report
said its design round was not about detailed calculations (<Cite id="s-4e3dc29a95" />). So learn to derive a
number out loud, state your units, and don't memorize multipliers. Every example below was recomputed for
this page; inputs marked "assumed" are round numbers chosen to make the arithmetic concrete.

**Constants**, fetched 2026-10-11:
- Llama-3.1-70B: 80 layers, 64 query heads, 8 KV heads, hidden size 8,192, so head_dim = 8,192 ÷ 64 = 128
  (<Cite id="s-6fdd575dc5" label="config.json" />).
- Llama-3.1-8B: 32 layers, 32 query heads, 8 KV heads, head_dim 128
  (<Cite id="s-20bffa9709" label="config.json" />).
- A100 80GB SXM: 2,039 GB/s of HBM bandwidth, 312 TFLOPS BF16 dense
  (<Cite id="s-8e443a8b66" />).
- GB = 10⁹ bytes, GiB = 2³⁰ bytes. Sources mix them; say which you mean.

| 1 | 134,217,728 | 134,250,496 | 1.0 | 0.43 µs | 65.8 µs | memory, by ~150× |

The weight matrix dominates the bytes, so for small M the intensity is roughly M itself. Batching pays until
M nears the ridge point; past it, extra tokens cost real compute.

**Latency targets.** The serving guide (<Cite id="s-75e82f8882" />) cites TTFT of "a few hundred ms" and inter-token latency of about

### Where sources disagree

1. **KV-cache form.** The KV-heads form is right for GQA models. The hidden-size form, used on a PracHub
   concept page (<Cite id="s-d97e9a72e2" />) and in the Baseten guide (<Cite id="s-f440daaea4" />), overstates Llama-3.1-70B by 8×. An LLM
   inference API guide uses the hidden-size form as well, then drops its own factor of 2 in its 70B example
   (<Cite id="s-c12521aa7d" />).
2. **Units.** The serving guide's "0.31 MB/token" for 70B (<Cite id="s-75e82f8882" />) is really 0.3125 MiB (320 KiB, 0.33 MB), and its
   "about 42 GB at 128K" is decimal GB (40 GiB).
3. **INT4 size.** "0.5 bytes per weight" leaves out the scales; with one FP16 scale per 32 weights, 70B is
   39.4 GB, not 35 GB.
4. **Continuous-batching gain.** Quoted as about 2× (<Cite id="s-f440daaea4" />), 3–5× (<Cite id="s-75e82f8882" />) and
   10–20× (<Cite id="s-c12521aa7d" />). Don't quote a multiplier. Derive it: batching amortizes the weight read
   until M nears the ridge point, so the gain depends on how far below the ridge you started and how uneven
   request lengths are.
5. **70B cold start.** "Tens of seconds to a couple of minutes" (<Cite id="s-75e82f8882" />) against "5–10
   minutes" (<Cite id="s-c12521aa7d" />). Load time = weight bytes ÷ effective read bandwidth: 140 GB at an assumed 2 GB/s from
   local NVMe is 70 s, at 1.25 GB/s over a 10 Gbit/s link 112 s, at 250 MB/s 560 s (9.3 minutes). Both quotes
   fit; ask where the weights live.

<AINative title="An agent can write the batcher; you still own its invariants">
Claude Opus 4 beat almost all applicants on Anthropic's performance take-home, where AI is allowed, and Opus
4.5 later matched the best human on the redesign, so Anthropic moved to puzzle-style problems
(<Cite id="s-a8c29f7dce" />). Generating a plausible batcher is cheap.
Knowing it is correct is not: every caller gets exactly its own result after a partial flush, the queue stays
bounded, a lone request still flushes by its deadline, cancellation frees the slot, and a dead GPU fails only
its own batch. Write those as tests and a load test before you prompt. The
[AI-native engineer](/system-design-labs/interviews/ai-native/) chapter argues that this verification is the
scarce skill.

This chapter is for you if you have spent years on backend or distributed systems and want a Senior or
Staff applied-AI role. You get the loop shape, ten ranked themes with what a strong answer covers, a section
each on evals and prompt injection, Senior vs Staff signals and a lab-based prep path. It is built from the 154
questions in the bank tagged AI Engineer or both tracks (as of 2026-10-11). Most rest on one anonymous account
or a prep site, so every claim below says where it comes from. "Reported" means a candidate or a prep site
said so, not that the company confirmed it.

## The role at Senior and Staff

An AI Engineer builds products on top of models they did not train: retrieval, agents, tool use, evals,
latency and cost. It is not ML research, and most loops do not test model math. The Forward Deployed
Engineer (FDE) title is a customer-facing version of the same job. By May 2026 the Pragmatic Engineer
described FDE work as roughly 25% coding, 50% integration and 25% customer-facing
(<Cite id="s-e4c26adb42" />).

The research behind this guide found one pattern that works in your favor: many AI Engineer questions are
distributed-systems questions with an LLM in the middle. An access-control-aware multi-tenant index, a
versioned prompt store, connector freshness after an outage, a batching scheduler. You already know how to
answer those. The two gaps the research points to are **evals** and **a clear account of a RAG or agent
system you built yourself**, with numbers.

At Senior and Staff, coding mostly filters and the design and behavioral rounds set the level. In a Blind
thread about a Meta E6 "AI native" loop (two AI-enabled coding rounds, two design rounds and a behavioral, per
the recruiter), commenters described coding as effectively pass or fail and said design and behavioral set the
level (<Cite id="s-2a15ccde37" />).

## Ranked themes

Themes are listed in the site's rank order, which comes from how many sourced rows touched each theme in
the first research pass. The number after each heading is how many rows the bank holds today for that
theme (AI Engineer plus both-track rows). The two orders differ in places: the second research pass added
many AI-assisted round reports (21 of the 26 are tagged both tracks), and practical-coding reports
outnumber security ones. Each theme ends with a link to its questions in the bank.

### 1. RAG and retrieval quality (21)

**What gets asked.** Design a multi-tenant enterprise RAG assistant with hybrid keyword and vector retrieval,
reranking, permission-aware retrieval and citations; PracHub's version adds how fast a deleted document
stops appearing in answers (<Cite id="s-f8053ff3a7" />). Harvey had a candidate build bare-bones embedding
RAG in Colab and then asked how to improve its evaluation with recall and hit rate (<Cite id="s-74265be7ec" />).
A Google "AI Infrastructure Engineer" round still opened with enterprise RAG design (<Cite id="s-3da5e3fd6a" />).

**A strong answer** separates indexing from serving, ties chunk size and `k` to the corpus and the questions
users ask, combines lexical and dense retrieval, reranks a shortlist, and states retrieval quality as a
number. It decides where the access check runs: filtering inside retrieval keeps forbidden documents out of
the candidate set; filtering after ranking is simpler but can empty the top-k and leak through timing. One
prep guide calls fixed 512-token chunks "the lazy default" because they cut sentences in half, and warns that
raising `k` buys recall at the cost of context, latency and distractors (<Cite id="s-7d03a572e0" />).

**Senior vs Staff.** Senior builds the pipeline and measures recall@k. Staff puts a number on deletion
latency, decides who owns the eval set, and prices re-indexing when the embedding model changes.

**Labs.** <LabRef lab="rag" inline /> builds dense, hybrid (BM25 plus dense with reciprocal rank fusion) and
reranked retrieval, measured on a public benchmark (see the prep path below). <LabRef lab="vector-index" inline /> covers HNSW, IVF and PQ, but not filtered search.
<LabRef lab="embedding-pipeline" inline /> covers batching, caching and model versioning. Gaps: chunking
beyond fixed-size windows, faithfulness scoring and ACL-filtered vector search have no lab yet.
[RAG questions](/system-design-labs/interviews/questions/?track=ai-engineer&theme=rag).

### 2. Agent design and reliability (20)

**What gets asked.** Design a support agent whose loop answers, calls a tool or hands off to a human on each
turn, and never gets stuck (Sierra, prep guide, <Cite id="s-9116046d24" />). Sierra's debugging round hands you a 4–5 file agent
codebase and a diagram of correct behavior and asks you to find about three bugs (<Cite id="s-6c08b2d69e" />).
A Snowflake ML engineer onsite asked for the common components of agent memory (<Cite id="s-5b59630431" />).

**A strong answer** keeps control flow in the orchestrator, not the prompt. It sets explicit stop conditions
(step budget, token budget, repeated-action detection), validates tool arguments against a schema, makes
tools idempotent so retries are safe, and traces every step. It hands off to a human when confidence is low;
one guide puts it as "a low-confidence escalation path matters more than a clever prompt"
(<Cite id="s-9116046d24" />).

**Senior vs Staff.** Senior designs the loop and its failure handling. Staff says where an agent is the wrong
tool (an Apple report asked when deterministic code is better, <Cite id="s-d3df675c97" />) and sets what the
agent may do without a human. The Harvey candidate thinks they overcomplicated their multi-agent
design (<Cite id="s-74265be7ec" />).

**Labs.** <LabRef lab="agent-framework" inline /> has a ReAct loop, function calling with schema validation, a
token-budget guard and a subprocess sandbox, all against a mock LLM. <LabRef lab="mcp-server" inline /> covers
tool and resource servers. [Agent questions](/system-design-labs/interviews/questions/?track=ai-engineer&theme=agents).

### 3. Evaluation (16)

**What gets asked.** A take-home with 50 markdown documents: build Q&A plus a 20-question eval set and report
recall@5 and faithfulness; a variant calibrates an LLM judge against human labels on 30 pairs
(<Cite id="s-7d03a572e0" />). A Sierra prep guide asks for eval sets and a regression process for prompt or
model swaps (<Cite id="s-9116046d24" />). An OpenAI FDE prep guide asks how you know an assistant is good
enough to roll out to the next team (<Cite id="s-26f0cae3fe" />). Evals get their own section below.
[Eval questions](/system-design-labs/interviews/questions/?track=ai-engineer&theme=evals).

### 4. Security, permissions and prompt injection (9)

**What gets asked.** Summarize product reviews with an LLM while defending against injection through the
review text, sanitizing input and handling PII (Google, first-hand, <Cite id="s-25809e059e" />). Design
per-tool permissions so injected text can never reach billing writes (Sierra, prep guide,
<Cite id="s-9116046d24" />). How would
you stop a coding agent leaking private source code (Meta, aggregator, <Cite id="s-be19e40ea6" />)? See the
section below. [Security questions](/system-design-labs/interviews/questions/?track=ai-engineer&theme=security-injection).

### 5. Practical LLM-plumbing code (17)

**What gets asked.** Call an endpoint that fails intermittently until it succeeds, then resolve products
through fallback IDs, with a new requirement in each follow-up (Sierra, <Cite id="s-6c08b2d69e" />). Page
through an API and compute over the JSON (Sierra Senior, rejected, <Cite id="s-aa5a42ddc5" />). Implement a
resilient pool of LLM providers with failover (Perplexity, <Cite id="s-a90c8401d4" />). A Cursor guide lists
parsing partial or malformed streamed tokens (<Cite id="s-c29d429c3c" />).

**A strong answer** is correct, readable code, finished fast. It puts a timeout on every call, separates
retryable errors (429, 5xx, timeouts) from the rest, uses exponential backoff with jitter and a retry budget,
opens a circuit per provider, and makes writes idempotent. A streaming parser buffers partial chunks instead
of assuming whole tokens.

**Senior vs Staff.** Both level-tagged Senior reports in the research (Sierra, Harvey) ended in rejection on
long practical problems; the advice is speed in your own language. A Staff OpenAI candidate called the coding
question "rather long" and suggested using the interviewer to focus on the parts that matter
(<Cite id="s-4d55d9b987" />).

**Labs.** <LabRef lab="load-balancer" inline /> has health checks, a circuit breaker and retry budgets.
<LabRef lab="rate-limiter" inline /> limits by request count, not tokens. <LabRef lab="tokenizer" inline />
covers BPE, which aggregators report at Perplexity and Glean. Gap: an LLM gateway with token budgets and
provider failover. [Plumbing questions](/system-design-labs/interviews/questions/?track=ai-engineer&theme=llm-plumbing-code).

### 6. Serving, latency and cost (10)

**What gets asked.** Design an inference service that batches waiting requests where each batch takes 100 ms
(Anthropic, aggregator, <Cite id="s-0606cb2a41" />). Diagnose high latency across an LLM pipeline and say
where batching and caching help (OpenAI, first-hand, <Cite id="s-812dbc9385" />). A LinkedIn project deep
dive asked how tokens were allocated and whether pricing was per query or per token (<Cite id="s-1980a339c0" />).

**A strong answer** argues in tokens first: prompt and output lengths, fan-out, cache-hit rate, then which
model tier each step needs. It treats time to first token and inter-token latency as separate budgets.
Hello Interview's OpenAI guide says the company name alone isn't a reason to prepare GPU infrastructure;
prepare for the team you're interviewing with (<Cite id="s-4d55d9b987" />). One unverified Blind comment says Staff loops expect you to go into KV cache,
batching and eval design (<Cite id="s-fe60d2bf1f" />). The [AI Infra back-of-envelope sheet](/system-design-labs/interviews/ai-infra/#back-of-envelope-sheet)
has the formulas. Lab: <LabRef lab="llm-inference" inline />.

### 7. AI-assisted coding rounds (26)

**What gets asked.** Meta's round is about 60 minutes in a multi-file CoderPad project: fix a bug, implement
the core logic, then optimize for larger inputs. The assistant answers in chat, you write the code, and the
grading covers problem solving, code quality, verification and communication, not prompting
(<Cite id="s-810bf5f1ff" />). A LinkedIn candidate finished 2 of 4 staged parts (LRU, then TTL, then thread
safety) and said fixing the AI's mistakes took most of the time (<Cite id="s-8e19cb9489" />).

**A strong answer** states the plan before prompting, asks for small pieces, runs the tests after each one,
reads every diff, and catches invented helpers; one Meta candidate reports the model inventing functions that
did not exist in the codebase (<Cite id="s-15a97c49b3" />). Miscalibration cuts both ways: a Meta MLE was told
"less manual work, more on AI" (<Cite id="s-443984bc07" />), while a rejected Stripe Staff candidate
believes letting the agent drive counted against them (<Cite id="s-8e0784406e" />).

**Senior vs Staff.** interviewing.io and DG Learning say Meta E7+ candidates get only the AI-assisted coding
round, so it carries the whole coding signal; neither cites a source (<Cite id="s-f5c1072815" />, <Cite id="s-feb4b11f82" />). Hello
Interview says the AI round runs up to E7 next to a classic round (<Cite id="s-810bf5f1ff" />), and nothing
official settles it. No lab targets this round; practice on a repo you didn't write, such as one of the Go
labs.
[AI-assisted questions](/system-design-labs/interviews/questions/?track=ai-engineer&theme=ai-assisted-coding).

<AINative title="What the AI-assisted round is really grading">
Most first-hand reports in the bank point the same way: the bottleneck is review, not generation. You lose time
fixing evicted keys left in a map or helpers that don't exist, and you lose points for accepting them. Drill
the habits that catch them: a plan in your own words, tests run after every change, and an explanation of
why a wrong suggestion is wrong before you fix it. The [AI-native engineer](/system-design-labs/interviews/ai-native/)
chapter makes the wider case that verification is the scarce skill.
</AINative>

### 8. Project deep dive (7)

**What gets asked.** Walk through a RAG project you built: sources, retrieval, evaluation, what broke, how
you catch answers drawn from stale documents (Amazon, aggregator, <Cite id="s-56187f9727" />). "Is there an
actual eval framework here, or is it vibes-based?" (OpenAI, snippet, <Cite id="s-9ffe6685c2" />). Why did you
use an LLM, and how did you know it fit (Anthropic, aggregator, <Cite id="s-cb39678bbd" />)?

**A strong answer** says "I", not "we", for the parts you built, and brings three numbers: retrieval or task
quality, cost per request, and latency. It names what broke and what you changed because of it. Staff
answers also defend what the project cost against what it returned.
[Deep-dive questions](/system-design-labs/interviews/questions/?track=ai-engineer&theme=project-deep-dive).

### 9. Customer discovery and scoping (4)

**What gets asked.** In an OpenAI FDE round, a candidate started on architecture and the interviewer stopped
them to ask what they would ask the customer first (<Cite id="s-812dbc9385" />). Cohere's FDE debugging round
says "requests are failing" and gives you only a diagram, no logs (<Cite id="s-88b41bdbd6" />).

**A strong answer** runs discovery before architecture: who the users are, whether the flow is interactive or
agentic, what the corpus looks like, and whether the system writes data
([Formation, secondary](https://formation.dev/blog/the-biggest-mistake-engineers-make-in-ai-system-design-interviews)).
No lab covers this.

### 10. Fine-tuning vs RAG vs prompting (6)

**What gets asked.** When would you fine-tune, use RAG, or just improve the prompt (OpenAI, first-hand,
<Cite id="s-812dbc9385" />)? An Amazon GenAI-team phone screen mixed fine-tuning, embeddings, the KV cache
and RAG evaluation (snippet, <Cite id="s-760e5688f9" />).

**A strong answer** starts with the prompt, uses retrieval for knowledge that changes or needs citations, and
uses fine-tuning for format, style or moving a task to a smaller model. The eval set decides, not taste.
Lab: <LabRef lab="lora-finetune" inline />.

### Also in most loops: classic DSA (7) and behavioral (10)

Classic coding hasn't gone away; see the table above. Behavioral rounds increasingly ask about AI use itself:
"tell me about a time AI gave you wrong or harmful output" (Amazon, aggregator, <Cite id="s-56187f9727" />) and
"what is your view of AI?" (Microsoft, aggregator, <Cite id="s-d302bba651" />).

## Evals: the differentiator

The research names evals as the clearest differentiator for applied candidates. Eugene Yan and Jason Liu, writing as hiring
managers in July 2024, said even engineers who only call APIs should build eval harnesses, track performance
over time and add guardrails ([Eugene Yan, secondary](https://eugeneyan.com/writing/how-to-interview/)). A
paid course calls evals the "weed out" part of interviews; that is a vendor claim
([Hamel Husain](https://maven.com/p/07e7c7/cracking-the-ai-evals-interview)). The research found no company
that officially runs a standalone eval round. Evals come up inside design rounds, take-homes and project deep
dives, usually as "how do you know it works?"

### Build the eval set from real failures

"A customer says your bot feels dumb. What do you change first?" (<Cite id="s-7d03a572e0" />). Start by turning
the complaint into labeled cases you can count. Pull them from production logs, thumbs-down events, support
tickets and escalations. Tag each with a query type, so you can see which slice regressed. Every incident
becomes a test. Keep a held-out slice you never tune prompts against.

Then check the set is big enough to detect the change you care about:

A 20-question set, the size one take-home asks for, can't tell a 5-point regression from noise. Say so in the
interview, and say what size you would grow it to. A paired test helps but doesn't remove the need for size:
the llm-eval lab's simulation found that with 500 items, the smallest true gap its paired bootstrap catches
80% of the time is <LabNumber lab="llm-eval" metric="Smallest true gap with 80% power" />, when the two versions
disagree on 20% or 40% of items.

### Measure retrieval and generation separately

If you only score the final answer, you can't tell a retrieval miss from a generation error. Measure
retrieval with recall@k or hit rate (did a gold passage reach the top k?) and MRR or NDCG when rank order
matters, the split one prep guide uses (<Cite id="s-7d03a572e0" />). Measure generation with
faithfulness (is every claim supported by the retrieved context?), answer relevance, and citation
correctness. Harvey's follow-up on recall and hit rate (<Cite id="s-74265be7ec" />) and the OpenAI FDE question
on how specific results should be (<Cite id="s-05342b6eb9" />) are both versions of this.

### Calibrate the judge against human labels

An LLM judge is a model too. One prep guide counts trusting a judge you haven't checked against human labels
as a weak answer (<Cite id="s-7d03a572e0" />), and an Apple question asks when you would trust an LLM judge rather than human
judges (<Cite id="s-0630fd2aff" />). Label a sample by hand, run the judge on it, and report agreement corrected
for chance:

<Formula
  title="Judge agreement corrected for chance (Cohen's kappa)"
  expr="κ = (p_o − p_e) ÷ (1 − p_e)      p_e = P(judge yes) × P(human yes) + P(judge no) × P(human no)"
  vars={[
    { sym: 'p_o', meaning: 'fraction of items where judge and human agree' },
    { sym: 'p_e', meaning: 'agreement you would get by chance from each side’s base rates' },
    { sym: 'κ', meaning: '1 = perfect agreement, 0 = no better than chance' },
  ]}
  example={[
    '30 labeled pairs; judge and human agree on 26: p_o = 26 ÷ 30 = 0.867.',
    'Both say "faithful" 70% of the time: p_e = 0.7 × 0.7 + 0.3 × 0.3 = 0.58.',
    'κ = (0.867 − 0.58) ÷ (1 − 0.58) = 0.287 ÷ 0.42 = 0.68.',

  ]}
  note="Illustrative inputs. Raw agreement looks good whenever one label dominates; kappa shows how much of it is real."

Also check the known judge biases (position, length, preferring its own model's output), pin the judge model
and prompt version, and re-run the calibration whenever either changes.

### Gate every prompt and model swap

Treat a prompt change like a code change. Version prompts immutably (Anthropic's prompt-playground question
asks for exactly this, <Cite id="s-f40d0eed6e" />). Run the offline eval in CI on every prompt, model or
retrieval change, compare against the current version on the same items with a paired test, and block on
per-slice thresholds, not just the average. Then canary the change with online metrics; for a support agent,
that means containment and escalation rates (<Cite id="s-9116046d24" />). Keep the rollback one flag flip
away.

**Labs.** <LabRef lab="llm-eval" role="proof" note="Few-shot and log-likelihood scoring, contamination checks, bootstrap confidence intervals and a paired bootstrap test." />
<LabRef lab="feature-flags" role="proof" note="Percentage rollout and targeting: the canary half of a regression gate." />

LLM-as-judge and faithfulness scoring are a gap: no lab builds them yet (planned as `llm-judge-evals`).

## Prompt injection and permissions

**What gets asked**, beyond the theme examples above: design MCP servers and tool permissions so analysts can
query five siloed systems through Claude, with explicit trust boundaries (<Cite id="s-a982325743" />); build a
sales-insights agent that refuses requests for personal data (Cohere take-home, <Cite id="s-1946467fb3" />);
what happens downstream when a party's data access is revoked (Cohere design follow-up,
<Cite id="s-d5d3016775" />).

**What a strong answer covers:**

1. **All retrieved and tool-returned text is untrusted data.** Injection arrives through review text, web
   pages, emails, documents and tool output, not only the user's message.
2. **Permissions live outside the model.** Authorize every tool call against the end user's identity and
   scopes, not the agent's service account. Deny by default. Split read tools from write tools. The model
   cannot grant itself access, whatever the text says.
3. **Irreversible actions need a gate.** Human approval, confirmation, amount and rate limits. One reported
   take-home asks for an inbox agent that waits for approval before it acts (<Cite id="s-1946467fb3" />).
4. **Close the exfiltration paths.** Don't auto-render links or images to arbitrary domains, allowlist tool
   egress, and redact PII before it reaches the model or the logs.
5. **Access control inside retrieval.** The RAG theme again: a document the user can't read should never
   reach the context window, and revocation has a stated propagation time.
6. **Test it.** Injection cases go into the eval set and the regression gate. Log and audit every tool call.

No prompt makes injection impossible, so design for a bounded blast radius when the model is confidently
wrong. Staff answers say who owns the policy across systems and how an audit would reconstruct what the agent
did.

**Labs.** <LabRef lab="mcp-server" inline /> (JSON-RPC tools, resources and middleware, where a permission
check would sit), <LabRef lab="agent-framework" inline /> (schema validation and a sandbox), and
<LabRef lab="oauth-provider" inline /> for the token flows that carry the user's identity to a tool.
Guardrails and injection defense are a gap (planned as `guardrails`).

## Why candidates get rejected

Each of these is reported, mostly by one candidate or one guide.

- **Architecture before discovery.** The OpenAI FDE redirect (<Cite id="s-812dbc9385" />); a rejected Cohere
  candidate said they went deep too early instead of laying out the components first (<Cite id="s-d5d3016775" />).
- **Hand-waved evaluation**: a prep guide's list of weak answers includes an unchecked judge and default 512-token chunks (<Cite id="s-7d03a572e0" />).
- **Too slow on long practical coding**: first-hand Sierra and Harvey Senior rejections
  (<Cite id="s-aa5a42ddc5" />, <Cite id="s-956f0bbc95" />).
- **Miscalibrated AI use, either way**: under-using it at Meta (<Cite id="s-443984bc07" />), over-delegating at
  Stripe (<Cite id="s-8e0784406e" />). A Cursor guide calls pasting the model's output, pasting back the error and

## A prep path through the labs

Build these in order. Each step says what it lets you claim in an interview. Many lab benchmark tables are
still estimates. The rag, embedding-pipeline and llm-eval numbers quoted here were measured on 2026-10-11;
quote them with the machine they ran on, or better, your own measurement on your own corpus.

1. **Retrieval, end to end.** "I built dense, then hybrid, then reranked retrieval, and here is recall@k on
   my corpus at each stage." On 300 BEIR SciFact queries the lab measured hit@3 of
   <LabNumber lab="rag" metric="hit@3 on BEIR SciFact" /> for the three stages. Hybrid gained nothing with the
   lab's whitespace BM25 tokenizer; a `\w+` tokenizer, tried in the benchmark script, lifted it to
   <LabNumber lab="rag" metric="BM25 tokenizer" />,
   as good as reranking without the reranker's
   <LabNumber lab="rag" metric="Cross-encoder rerank" /> per query (CPU / Apple GPU). Fixing the cheap stage before adding the
   expensive one is a strong interview answer.
   <LabRef lab="rag" role="proof" note="Dense, hybrid (BM25 + dense with RRF) and cross-encoder reranked retrieval, with a recall@k eval." />

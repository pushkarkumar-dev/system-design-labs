## The prompt

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "I'd split the documents into small pieces along their headings and paragraphs, and give each piece a stable
> ID. I'd turn each piece into a vector that captures its meaning, and for a question I'd find the pieces whose
> vectors are closest. I'd add a keyword search for exact terms like error codes, and merge the two ranked
> lists. The model answers only from those pieces and cites them, and my code checks the citations. Then I'd
> prove it works: 20 to 50 questions where I know the right documents, a hit rate and a recall at the number of
> pieces I actually send, with an error bar, next to keyword-only and vector-only baselines."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer.

In a notebook, a 60-minute round or a take-home, you build a small version of the system behind "chat with your
documents". You split a small set of documents into pieces, make them searchable by meaning and by keyword,
and have a model answer a question from the best pieces, citing them. Then you build a small set of questions
with known answers and report how well the search works. The follow-ups ask what changes at 100× the data,
under a provider's rate limits, and for searches that need exact rather than fuzzy matches.

### What makes this different from search you have built before

You have probably built search on a database: `WHERE title ILIKE '%refund%'`, or a keyword query against a
search engine. That kind of search has a clear contract. A row matches or it does not, and a unit test can
check it.

This exercise is different in three ways, and a good answer is built around them:

1. **It searches by meaning, not by matching words.** "Can I get my money back?" should find a paragraph
   titled "Refunds", even though the two share no words. So the search never says "no match". It always
   returns a ranked list of the closest pieces, and the closest piece can still be the wrong one.
2. **A language model writes the final answer.** It reads the pieces you retrieved and writes a fluent reply.
   If retrieval returned the wrong pieces, the reply is fluent and wrong, and nothing crashes.
3. **"Correct" is a judgement, so you prove it with a measurement, not a unit test.** You write questions
   whose right documents you know, run the pipeline, count how often the right document comes back, and say
   how sure you are of that count. It is closer to an A/B test than to a test suite.

The code is a few hundred lines. The round is usually decided by the third point.

### Words you need first

- **LLM** (large language model): the model that writes the answer, called through an API such as OpenAI's or
  Anthropic's. It reads and writes text in **tokens**, pieces of words of roughly ¾ of a word each. Prices and
  rate limits are counted in tokens.
- **RAG** (retrieval-augmented generation): first retrieve a few relevant pieces of your documents, then paste
  them into the LLM's prompt and ask it to answer from them. The retrieval half is ordinary code you write.
  The generation half is one API call.
- **Chunk**: one of those pieces, usually a paragraph or a short section. You search chunks rather than whole
  documents because a prompt can hold only so much text, and a small piece is a sharper match.
- **Embedding**: a list of numbers (a vector, for example 384 numbers long) that a small model computes from a
  piece of text. Texts with similar meaning get vectors that point in similar directions. The model that
  computes them is the **embedding model**. It is a different, much smaller model than the LLM.

Other terms come up later, and each is defined where it first appears.

## What is being tested

The reports agree on four things. The fourth is where these rounds tend to be decided.

1. **The core math, by hand, without bugs.** A similarity score between two vectors, picking the best few
   results, a keyword score, and a rule that merges two rankings. Each is a few lines, and each is explained
   in the stages below. The interviewer wants them correct on the first run, with every row of numbers still
   matched to the right chunk ID. PromptLayer's advice to interviewers for these exercises: "Focus on clean
   code, error handling, and understanding trade-offs" (<Cite id="s-1205b0cc50" />).
2. **Structure.** Scale AI ran this as an object-oriented design round (<Cite id="s-90185f6ee1" />). A search
   component you can swap, an embedding model behind an interface, and a test that uses a fake embedding model
   show structure better than a long notebook cell.
3. **Speed.** A Harvey Senior candidate's advice after a rejection: "You need to be really fast at coding"
   (<Cite id="s-956f0bbc95" />). Have the skeleton in your fingers before the round.
4. **A number for "it works".** The prep guide puts it bluntly: "Retrieval quality gets scored with recall@k and
   precision@k, and ranking quality with MRR or NDCG when the order of results matters." Those are scores on a
   labeled question set, and stage 5 defines each one. Without such a number, "the interviewer assumes you tuned
   yours by feel" (<Cite id="s-7d03a572e0" />). Harvey's final question was about exactly this
   (<Cite id="s-74265be7ec" />), and both OpenAI take-homes were defended live, choice by choice
   (<Cite id="s-05342b6eb9" />, <Cite id="s-812dbc9385" />).

My reading: a candidate who builds a clean pipeline but answers the metric question with "I'd look at some
outputs" leaves the round with nothing to show. A candidate who has 30 labeled questions, a hit rate (the share of
questions whose right document came back) with an error bar and two failure examples leaves with a defensible result, even if the code is plain.

## Clarify and scope

Ask these before writing code. When the interviewer has no answer, write your assumption down and move on.

- **Corpus.** How many documents, what format, how long? *Assume 50 markdown files of 1,000–3,000 words.*
- **What counts as a good answer?** One supporting passage, or several? Must every sentence cite a source?
  *Assume answers cite chunk IDs and the system may say "I don't know".*
- **Labels.** Is there a set of questions with known answers, or do you write one? *Assume you write 20–50
  questions with the relevant document or chunk IDs, including a few the corpus cannot answer.*
- **Allowed tools.** Hosted APIs (OpenAI's take-home was built on OpenAI's APIs), a local model, a keyword-search
  library, a vector database? *Assume NumPy for vectors and a hand-written keyword score unless told otherwise.*
- **Query mix.** Exact identifiers (error codes, product codes, names) or vague intent? This decides how much the
  keyword side matters. It is also the OpenAI "how specific" follow-up in disguise.
- **Latency and scale.** Interactive or batch? How big will it get? This sets up the 100× follow-up.

One more point is worth saying aloud. The most text a model can read in one prompt is its **context window**,
and 50 documents may fit inside it. Anthropic's contextual-retrieval post suggests that for a knowledge base under
200,000 tokens you can "include the entire knowledge base in the prompt"
([Anthropic, 2024-09-19](https://www.anthropic.com/news/contextual-retrieval)). Build retrieval anyway, because
the exercise asks for it. But name the long-context version as the baseline your pipeline has to beat on cost or
accuracy. Say: "For 50 documents I could paste everything into the prompt. I'll build retrieval because it's the
exercise, and I'll treat paste-everything as the baseline to beat."

Out of scope unless asked: who may see which document (access control), connectors and incremental sync,
multiple customers in one index, and training your own embedding model.

## Plan before code

### The pipeline as a picture

Say the plan in one breath, then build it so that you always have something that runs.

```
50 markdown files
   │  1. split on headings and paragraphs
   ▼
Chunker ── chunks with stable IDs like "billing.md#2"
   │                              │
   │ 2a. embed each chunk         │ 2b. count the words in each chunk
   ▼                              ▼
Dense index                    BM25 index
(vectors, cosine score)        (keyword score)
   │                              │
   └───────────────┬──────────────┘
                   ▼  3. merge the two ranked lists (RRF)
           Fused ranking ── optional reranker re-scores the top 20
                   │  4. paste the top 5 into a prompt that demands citations
                   ▼
              LLM answer ── citation checker: every sentence cites a chunk it was given

Labeled questions ──▶ 5. metrics on the ranked IDs: hit@k, recall@k, MRR, nDCG
```

**Chunker.** It splits each document into chunks and gives each one an ID such as `billing.md#2` (the second
chunk of `billing.md`). The ID must come out the same on every run, because your labels and the model's
citations point at it.

**Dense index.** It holds one embedding per chunk, as rows of a matrix. Each row is scaled to length 1 once,
when it is stored; this is called **normalizing**. A question is embedded the same way, and
each chunk is scored by how closely its vector points in the question's direction. This score is called
**cosine similarity**. "Dense" refers to the vectors: every position holds a number.

**BM25 index.** BM25 is the standard keyword-ranking formula in search engines. It scores a chunk higher when it
contains the question's words, especially rare ones. It catches what embeddings blur: an error code, a product
code, a person's name.

**Merge (RRF).** The two indexes give two ranked lists. **Reciprocal rank fusion** (RRF) merges them using only
each chunk's position in each list, so you never have to compare a cosine score with a keyword score.

**Reranker (optional).** A slower, more accurate model that re-scores only the top 20 or so. You keep it only if
the measurement says it helps.

**LLM answer and citation checker.** The top few chunks go into a prompt that says "answer only from these, cite
each sentence, or say I don't know". Plain code then checks that every citation points at a chunk the model was
actually given.

**Metrics.** A small set of questions with known relevant chunks scores the ranked list before the LLM sees it.
This is the number the round is decided on.

### Follow one question through it

Here is one question going through the pipeline. Assume the corpus is a software product's help center (an
assumption for illustration). A user asks *"Can I get a refund on an annual plan after 30 days?"* Two chunks
answer it: `billing.md#2` (heading "Billing > Refunds") and `plans.md#3` (heading "Plans > Annual billing").
Call them A and B. A says what the refund window is, so it matters most. B adds the annual-plan detail. All values
are illustrative.

| Step | What happens | What to say in the interview |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Chunk | The 50 files become about 500 chunks, each with an ID and its heading path. | "IDs are stable, so labels and citations survive a re-run." |
| 2a. Dense search | The question becomes 384 numbers. The top 5 by cosine are `faq.md#7` (C), A, `account.md#1` (D), B, `billing.md#5` (E). | "Normalize once at index time, and search is one matrix multiply." |
| 2b. Keyword search | BM25 ranks chunks containing "refund" and "annual" highest, so A and B both appear near the top. | "Keyword search catches exact words that embeddings blur." |
| 3. Merge | RRF rewards chunks that both lists place high, so A and B move up. | "Fusion uses ranks, not scores, so I never put two scales on one axis." |
| 4. Answer | The top 5 go into the prompt. The model answers with "[1]" and "[2]" citations, and the checker confirms both point at chunks it was given. | "An uncited sentence or a citation to a missing source fails without any judge." |
| 5. Measure | Score the dense list [C, A, D, B, E] against the label \{A, B\}. | "A is at position 2, B at position 4. Top 3 finds one of two, top 5 finds both." |

Step 5 is where the numbers come from. On this one question the dense list has a hit in the top 3 (hit@3 = 1),
finds half the relevant chunks in the top 3 (recall@3 = 0.5) and all of them in the top 5 (recall@5 = 1.0).
Stage 5 defines these metrics properly and computes the rest for this same list. Averaged over 20–50 such
questions, they are the result you report.

### The build order

| Stage | What you write | Why this order |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | `cosine`, `normalize_rows`, `top_k` | Everything else calls them, and the Amazon variant is only this |
| 1 | Markdown chunker with stable chunk IDs and heading paths | Labels and citations need IDs that survive a re-run |
| 2 | `Embedder` interface, a dense index | Swapping the model or using a fake in tests is one line |
| 3 | BM25, reciprocal rank fusion | Exact terms and identifiers that embeddings blur |
| 4 | Cited prompt, citation checker, abstention | The answer step, plus checks that need no judge |
| 5 | hit@k, recall@k, MRR, nDCG@k over a labeled set | The number the round is decided on |

In a 60-minute round, a workable split is about 10 minutes for stage 0, 15 for chunking and embedding, 10 for
BM25 and fusion, 10 for the answer step and 15 for the evaluation. In a take-home, add a README that leads with
the numbers, two failure examples and a "what I'd change at 100×" section. Reviewers read the README before the
code.

Write the labeled questions **before** tuning anything. Mix single-fact questions, questions that need two
chunks (like the refund example), keyword-heavy ones (codes, names), paraphrases that share no words with the
source, and three or four the corpus cannot answer. If you tune chunk size on the same 20 questions you report,
say so: it is a tuning set, not a test set.

## Solution in stages

The code below is one file plus a test file, written for this walkthrough. Its 18 tests and an end-to-end run
on a public dataset were run on 2026-10-12 (Python 3.13.7, NumPy, sentence-transformers 6.1, Apple M1 Ultra).
The results are under Verification.

### Stage 0: cosine similarity and top-k

Cosine similarity measures the angle between two vectors. It is 1 when they point the same way (same meaning),
about 0 when they are unrelated, and −1 when they point in opposite directions. It ignores length, so a chunk is
not favoured just because its numbers are bigger. The formula is the dot product divided by the two lengths.
**Top-k** means the k chunks with the highest scores. This stage is the whole Amazon variant, and every later
stage depends on it.

```python
import numpy as np

def cosine(a, b, eps=1e-12) -> float:
    a = np.asarray(a, dtype=np.float32); b = np.asarray(b, dtype=np.float32)
    denom = np.linalg.norm(a) * np.linalg.norm(b)
    return float(a @ b / denom) if denom > eps else 0.0      # zero vector: 0, not NaN

def normalize_rows(m, eps=1e-12):
    m = np.asarray(m, dtype=np.float32)
    return m / np.maximum(np.linalg.norm(m, axis=1, keepdims=True), eps)   # axis=1: rows

def cosine_matrix(q, d):                                       # (n_q, dim) x (n_d, dim)
    return normalize_rows(q) @ normalize_rows(d).T

def top_k(scores, k):
    k = min(k, scores.shape[-1])
    part = np.argpartition(-scores, k - 1)[:k]                 # O(n), unordered
    return part[np.argsort(-scores[part], kind="stable")]      # sort only the k winners
```

Say three things while you write it:

- "I normalize once at index time. For vectors of length 1, cosine equals the dot product, so search is a single
  matrix-vector product."
- "I decide what a zero vector scores. `0 / 0` is NaN, and NaN sorts unpredictably, so it scores 0."
- "`argpartition` finds the k winners in linear time; I only sort those k, not the whole corpus."

### Stage 1: chunk markdown by structure

The chunker decides what the search can find. A chunk that cuts a sentence in half, or loses the heading that
says what the paragraph is about, is a worse match for every question.

```python
@dataclass(frozen=True)

    chunk_id: str   # "billing.md#2": stable, so labels and citations survive re-runs
    doc_id: str
    heading: str    # "Billing > Refunds", kept for citations and prepended when embedding
    text: str

    # 2. within each section, pack whole paragraphs until the next one would exceed max_tokens

    # 4. carry the last paragraph into the next chunk as overlap
    ...
```

The full function is about 30 lines. The design choice to defend is splitting on headings and paragraphs rather
than fixed windows of N tokens. The prep guide calls fixed 512-token windows "the lazy default" because "they cut
sentences in half" (<Cite id="s-7d03a572e0" />). Adding the heading path ("Billing > Refunds") to the front of each
chunk gives both searches context that a bare paragraph lacks.

Size chunks to the embedding model, not to a round number. Every embedding model reads at most a fixed number of
its own tokens (for this model they are called word pieces) and silently ignores the rest.
`all-MiniLM-L6-v2`, the small model used here, embeds at most 256 word pieces
([its sentence-transformers config](https://huggingface.co/sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2/blob/main/sentence_bert_config.json)).
In the rag lab, about two thirds of the 512-approximate-token chunks were longer than that window, so their tails
were never embedded. Even the 150-word chunks used in this walkthrough's run left about a quarter of chunks over
the window. Say: "I measure how many chunks are longer than the embedder's window, because the extra text is cut
off without an error."

### Stage 2: embed behind an interface

The embedding model is an outside dependency, like a database driver. Put it behind a small interface (in Python,
a `Protocol`) so you can swap a local model for a hosted API, or use a fake in tests, without touching the
search code.

```python
class Embedder(Protocol):
    def embed(self, texts: Sequence[str]) -> np.ndarray: ...   # (n, dim) float32, unit rows

class SentenceTransformerEmbedder:
    def __init__(self, name="sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2", batch_size=64):
        self.model = SentenceTransformer(name)
        self.batch_size = batch_size
    def embed(self, texts):
        return self.model.encode(list(texts), batch_size=self.batch_size,
                                 normalize_embeddings=True).astype(np.float32)

class DenseIndex:
    def __init__(self, embedder: Embedder):
        self.embedder, self.chunks, self.matrix = embedder, [], None
    def add(self, chunks):
        vecs = self.embedder.embed([f"{c.heading}\n{c.text}" for c in chunks])
        assert len(vecs) == len(chunks), "embedder dropped or added rows"
        self.chunks += chunks
        self.matrix = vecs if self.matrix is None else np.vstack([self.matrix, vecs])
    def search(self, query, k):
        scores = self.matrix @ self.embedder.embed([query])[0]
        return [(self.chunks[i], float(scores[i])) for i in top_k(scores, k)]
```

In the OpenAI take-home you would put a hosted embedding call behind the same interface. The assertion on row
count is cheap, and it catches a provider that returns a short batch. Row `i` of the matrix must always be chunk
`i`. If they drift apart, every result is wrong and nothing crashes.

### Stage 3: BM25 and reciprocal rank fusion

Embeddings capture meaning, but they blur exact tokens: an error code, a product code, a person's name. Two
error codes that differ by one digit get almost the same vector. Keyword scoring does not have this problem.

BM25 combines three ideas. A chunk scores higher when it contains a query word more often, with diminishing
returns (the knob `k1` sets how fast the returns diminish). Rare words count more than common ones; this weight
is the **idf** (inverse document frequency). And long chunks are not rewarded just for being long (the knob `b`
sets how much length is penalized).

```python
def tokenize(text):
    return re.findall(r"\w+", text.lower())   # whitespace split keeps "limit," != "limit"

class BM25Index:
    def __init__(self, k1=1.2, b=0.75): ...
    def search(self, query, k):
        n, avgdl = len(self.chunks), sum(self.lengths) / len(self.lengths)
        scores = np.zeros(n, dtype=np.float32)
        for term in set(tokenize(query)):
            df = self.df.get(term, 0)
            if not df:
                continue
            idf = math.log(1 + (n - df + 0.5) / (df + 0.5))
            for i, tf in enumerate(self.tf):            # a real engine walks a posting list
                if f := tf.get(term, 0):
                    norm = f + self.k1 * (1 - self.b + self.b * self.lengths[i] / avgdl)
                    scores[i] += idf * f * (self.k1 + 1) / norm
        return [(self.chunks[i], float(scores[i])) for i in top_k(scores, k) if scores[i] > 0]

def rrf(rankings, k=60):
    fused = {}
    for ranking in rankings:
        for rank, cid in enumerate(ranking, start=1):
            fused[cid] = fused.get(cid, 0.0) + 1.0 / (k + rank)
    return sorted(fused.items(), key=lambda kv: -kv[1])
```

The tokenizer line matters more than it looks. Splitting on whitespace keeps punctuation attached, so "limit,"
and "limit" count as different words. The rag lab shows the cost. It was measured on BEIR SciFact, a public
benchmark of science claims checked against paper abstracts, using **hit@3**: the share of questions whose right document
was in the top 3 results (stage 5 defines it properly). The lab's BM25 split on whitespace, and its three stages
scored <LabNumber lab="rag" metric="hit@3 on BEIR SciFact" /> (dense, hybrid, rerank), so adding BM25 (the
"hybrid") added nothing over dense. Changing only the tokenizer to `\w+` raised hybrid to
<LabNumber lab="rag" metric="BM25 tokenizer (SciFact)" />.

Now the merge. Reciprocal rank fusion
([Cormack, Clarke and Büttcher, SIGIR 2009](https://plg.uwaterloo.ca/~gvcormac/cormacksigir09-rrf.pdf)) gives each
chunk 1 ÷ (60 + its position) from each list and adds them up. It uses positions, not scores, so you never have to
put a cosine of 0.4 and a BM25 score of 17 on one scale. A chunk that both lists place fairly high beats a chunk
that only one list places first.

A **reranker** is the optional last step. The embedding search compares two vectors that were computed
separately. A reranker instead reads the question and one chunk together and outputs one relevance score. This
kind of model is called a **cross-encoder**. It is more accurate and much slower, because it must run once per
(question, chunk) pair at query time. The rag lab measured <LabNumber lab="rag" metric="Cross-encoder rerank, 20 candidates" />
(CPU / Apple GPU) for 20 candidates. Say: "I rerank a short list, never the corpus."

### Stage 4: answer with citations, or abstain

The prompt gives the model numbered sources and three rules: answer only from the sources, cite every sentence
with `[n]`, and say "I don't know" when the sources do not contain the answer. Saying "I don't know" is called
**abstention**. Then plain code checks the reply.

```python
def build_prompt(question, chunks):
    sources = "\n\n".join(f"[{i}] ({c.doc_id} - {c.heading})\n{c.text}"
                          for i, c in enumerate(chunks, 1))
    return ("Answer using only the sources below. Cite every sentence with [n]. "
            "If the sources do not contain the answer, reply exactly: I don't know.\n\n"
            f"Sources:\n{sources}\n\nQuestion: {question}\nAnswer:")

def check_citations(text, chunks) -> Answer:
    if text.strip().startswith("I don't know"):
        return Answer(text, abstained=True)
    problems, cited = [], []
    for sent in re.split(r"(?<=[.!?])\s+", text.strip()):
        refs = [int(n) for n in re.findall(r"\[(\d+)\]", sent)]
        if not refs:
            problems.append(f"uncited: {sent[:60]!r}")
        for n in refs:
            (cited.append(chunks[n - 1]) if 1 <= n <= len(chunks)
             else problems.append(f"cites missing source [{n}]"))
    return Answer(text, citations=list(dict.fromkeys(cited)), problems=problems)
```

The model call is an injected function, `llm(prompt) -> str`, so tests use a stub. In this walkthrough's run the
generation stage was exercised only with a stub; no hosted model was called.

An answer is **faithful** when every claim in it is supported by the chunks it was given. The citation checker
is the first, free layer of faithfulness. An answer that cites a source it was never given, or makes an uncited
claim, fails without any extra model. Say: "These checks cost nothing and catch the most embarrassing failures
before I pay for a judge."

### Stage 5: measure

The **labeled set** (or eval set) is your list of questions, each with the IDs of the chunks or documents that
answer it. The metrics below all compare one ranked list of IDs with one question's label, and you average them
over the set.

- **hit@k** (hit rate): 1 if at least one relevant item is in the top k, else 0.
- **recall@k**: the share of *all* relevant items that made the top k. It is lower than hit@k whenever an answer
  needs two chunks and you found one.
- **precision@k**: the share of the top k that is relevant.
- **Reciprocal rank**: 1 ÷ the position of the first relevant item. Its average over questions is **MRR** (mean
  reciprocal rank). It rewards putting the right item first.
- **nDCG@k** (normalized discounted cumulative gain): gives each relevant item a gain (2 for "essential", 1 for
  "useful"), divides it by a penalty that grows with position, adds these up, and divides by the best possible
  total. It is 1.0 for the perfect order.

```python
def hit_at_k(ranked, relevant, k):    return float(any(r in relevant for r in ranked[:k]))
def recall_at_k(ranked, relevant, k): return len(set(ranked[:k]) & relevant) / len(relevant)
def reciprocal_rank(ranked, relevant, k=None):
    for i, r in enumerate(ranked[:k] if k else ranked, start=1):
        if r in relevant:
            return 1.0 / i
    return 0.0
def ndcg_at_k(ranked, gains, k):       # linear gain, log2 discount (trec_eval / BEIR convention)
    dcg = sum(gains.get(r, 0) / math.log2(i + 1) for i, r in enumerate(ranked[:k], 1))
    ideal = sorted(gains.values(), reverse=True)[:k]
    idcg = sum(g / math.log2(i + 1) for i, g in enumerate(ideal, 1))
    return dcg / idcg if idcg else 0.0

def dedupe_docs(chunk_ids):            # chunk ranking -> document ranking, first hit wins
    return list(dict.fromkeys(cid.split("#")[0] for cid in chunk_ids))
```

Back to the refund question. The dense ranking was [C, A, D, B, E], and A (gain 2) and B (gain 1) are relevant:

| Metric | Value | Why |
|---|---|---|
| hit@5 | 1 | At least one relevant chunk is in the top 5 |
| recall@3 | 0.5 | Only A is in the top 3; B is at position 4 |
| recall@5 | 1.0 | Both are in the top 5 |
| precision@5 | 0.4 | 2 of the 5 results are relevant |
| Reciprocal rank | 0.5 | The first relevant chunk is at position 2 |
| nDCG@5 | 0.6433 | Both found, but lower than the ideal order [A, B, …] |

These are the exact values the test suite checks by hand arithmetic (see Verification).

Know the difference between the two words Harvey's interviewer used. Hit rate and recall@k are different
numbers. Many codebases blur them: the rag lab's own `Recall@k` is a hit rate, and its bench notes say so. Say:
"I report which one I computed, at the k I actually pass to the model." The family walkthrough on
[measuring retrieval](/system-design-labs/interviews/walkthroughs/rag-quality-debugging/) covers MRR and nDCG and
when each one fits.

Faithfulness needs a judge, usually another LLM call that grades the answer. This is called **LLM-as-judge**.
Split the answer into claims and ask, per claim, whether the cited chunk supports it, with a yes/no verdict.
Before you report a judge's number, label 20–30 answers yourself and check how often the judge agrees with you.
The prep guide asks whether you would trust the judge before testing it against humans, and answers "The right
answer is no" (<Cite id="s-7d03a572e0" />).

### Debugging embedding code (the PromptLayer variant)

The reported prompt is one line, so this walkthrough assumes the usual shape: a short function that stores,
normalizes and searches embeddings, runs without errors, and returns plausible but wrong results. This planted
version has five bugs. One term first: **fp16** is a 16-bit floating-point number, half the size of a normal
32-bit float, whose largest value is 65,504.

```python
def search_buggy(query_vec, doc_vecs, doc_ids, k=3):
    docs = np.array(doc_vecs, dtype=np.float16)          # 1: fp16 overflows when squaring large values
    docs = docs / np.linalg.norm(docs, axis=0)            # 2: normalizes columns, not rows
    q = np.asarray(query_vec, dtype=np.float32)           # 3: query never normalized
    scores = docs @ q
    order = np.argsort(scores)[:k]                        # 4: ascending, so least similar first
    ids = sorted(set(doc_ids))                            # 5: reorders ids away from their rows
    return [(ids[i], float(scores[i])) for i in order]
```

The fix casts to float32, normalizes with `axis=1, keepdims=True` and an epsilon, normalizes the query, sorts
descending, keeps `doc_ids` in row order, and raises when the query and document dimensions differ (for example
384 against 1,536 means two different models).

A sixth bug lives in batching code. It dedupes the texts with `set()`, embeds the unique texts, then `zip`s the
results back onto the original list. A set has no order, so rows land on the wrong text and nothing crashes.
Dedupe with `dict.fromkeys` to keep order, and map results back by key.

Find these by testing, not by reading. Say: "Let me build a three-document corpus where I know the right
answer." That one test exposes bugs 2–5 at once.

### An end-to-end run on a public dataset

Unit tests prove the parts. To check the whole pipeline, the same code was run on 2026-10-12 against BEIR
SciFact's test split ([Thakur et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08663)): 300 queries with document-level
labels, 5,183 abstracts cut into 9,377 chunks of at most 150 words, MiniLM-L6 on the Apple GPU. This is a scratch
run of this page's code, not one of the lab's recorded benchmarks. MRR@10 and nDCG@10 look only at the top 10.

| System | hit@5 | recall@5 | MRR@10 | nDCG@10 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dense only | 0.753 | 0.737 | 0.612 | 0.655 |
| BM25 only (`\w+` tokens) | 0.747 | 0.724 | 0.616 | 0.649 |
| Hybrid (RRF) | 0.790 | 0.767 | 0.659 | 0.696 |
| Hybrid + cross-encoder | 0.790 | 0.770 | 0.669 | 0.704 |

A gap of a few points on 300 questions could be luck, so each comparison comes with an interval. The method is a
**paired bootstrap**: resample the 300 questions with replacement many times, recompute the difference between
the two systems on the same resampled questions each time, and keep the middle 95% of those differences.
"Paired" means both systems are scored on the same questions, which removes the noise that comes from some
questions being harder than others. If the interval excludes zero, the gain is real.

Hybrid beat dense by +3.7 points of hit@5 [+0.3, +7.0] and +4.1 points of nDCG@10 [+1.4, +6.9]. The reranker on
top of that hybrid added +0.0 hit@5 [−3.7, +3.7] and +0.8 nDCG@10 [−2.2, +3.7]: nothing measurable.

That differs from the rag lab, where reranking was the only one of the three lab stages that cleared its interval
on SciFact. The lab's hybrid used the whitespace tokenizer, so its reranker was fixing what a better BM25 also
fixes. The lab's own ablation (an experiment that changes one thing at a time) found the same: hybrid with `\w+` matched reranking without the reranker's latency.
The lesson for an interview: whether a stage helps depends on the baseline under it. Say: "I measure each change
against the configuration I would actually ship."

The dense nDCG@10 is also a check on the metric code. MTEB, the public embedding benchmark, publishes a SciFact
score for the same model; compare against it. A wrong discount or a chunk-versus-document mix-up usually lands
far from it.

### What to check live, in the round

- Print the top 3 chunks for three questions and read them. It takes a minute and catches most bugs.
- Assert row counts after every embed call, and check that one known-answer question comes back first.
- Run BM25 alone and dense alone. If the hybrid is not better than both, look for a bug before you report it.
- Put an error bar on the number. With 20 questions and a hit rate of 0.8, the 95% interval is about **±17.5
  points**; with 200 questions it is about **±5.5**. Say: "Twenty questions can show the system works at all.
  They cannot show that hybrid beat dense by 3 points."

## Complexity and performance

The simplest vector search compares the question with every chunk. This is called **flat** (or brute-force)
search. Its cost grows with the number of chunks times the vector length, and it is exact: it always finds the
true closest chunks.

Measured on 2026-10-12 on the same machine under other load (load average 5–8), one 384-dimension query against
random unit vectors took about **0.4 ms at 10,000 vectors, 4 ms at 100,000 and 44 ms at 1,000,000**. Time grows
linearly with N, as the formula says. Flat search stays fine well past the 100× follow-up. Because it is exact, it
is also the reference you measure a faster, approximate index against.

The hand-written BM25 is the slow part. It loops over every chunk for every query word, so it costs
O(N × query words) in Python. In the SciFact run, dense search took about 7 ms per query and the hybrid about
43 ms. The rag lab's hybrid `retrieve()` took <LabNumber lab="rag" metric="Hybrid retrieve() v1" /> on 5,852 chunks
(CPU / Apple GPU). A real search engine keeps an **inverted index**: for each word, a posting list of the chunks
that contain it. It then touches only those chunks. The search-engine lab builds one.

Embedding is the cost of loading documents in, called **ingestion**. The embedding-pipeline lab measured
<LabNumber lab="embedding-pipeline" metric="1 vs 32 chunk-length texts per call (CPU)" /> for 256-token texts,
one at a time versus batches of 32. At the batched rate, 50,000 chunks take about **450 s** on one CPU process
(derived: 50,000 ÷ 112 ≈ 450).

### What changes at 100× the data, and under rate limits

This is OpenAI's reported follow-up (<Cite id="s-812dbc9385" />). Going from 50 documents to 5,000 changes less
than candidates expect:

- **Search.** Flat NumPy still works at about 50,000 chunks. Move to an **approximate nearest-neighbour index**
  (HNSW and IVF are the common kinds: structures that find almost the closest vectors without comparing against
  all of them) or a vector database at millions of vectors, or when you need filters and live updates. When you
  do, measure how many of flat search's true top results the approximate index still finds.
- **BM25.** Replace the loop with a real inverted index: Postgres full-text search, OpenSearch, or whatever the
  company already runs.
- **Ingestion becomes a job.** Hash each chunk (its text plus the embedding model's name) and skip unchanged
  hashes, so a re-run or a retry never re-embeds or duplicates. Checkpoint progress. Re-embed everything when the
  model changes, because vectors from two models aren't comparable.
- **Rate limits.** Providers cap how many tokens you may send per minute (**TPM**, tokens per minute) and reply
  with HTTP **429** ("Too Many Requests") when you go over. Batch many inputs per request, cap concurrency on
  your side, and on a 429 back off with jitter and honour `Retry-After`. Cap total attempts, because retries
  multiply load: three attempts with no budget can triple traffic during an incident.
- **The eval set grows with the corpus.** 20 questions over 5,000 documents measure almost nothing.

The rate limit also sets a floor on how long ingestion takes, however many workers you run. With assumed numbers
(5,000 documents of 2,000 tokens each, and a 1M TPM limit), ingestion takes **at least 10 minutes**. Say: "Adding
workers doesn't beat the TPM limit; I'd tell the user when it will finish."

### "How specific do you want to be?" (the product-search variant)

For product search, the follow-up is about precision versus recall per kind of query (<Cite id="s-05342b6eb9" />).
Product queries come in two kinds.

"iPhone 15 Pro case MagSafe" has an exact intent. Return few results, all of which match brand, model and
attribute, and return nothing rather than a near miss. "Gift for a runner" is broad. Return a varied set.

A design that handles both works in four steps. It extracts structured filters from the query (brand, category,
price, size). It applies them as hard filters. It runs hybrid retrieval inside them. And it uses a score
threshold to return fewer results when the top matches are weak. Evaluate the two kinds separately: precision@5
on exact-intent queries, recall and nDCG on broad ones.

Amazon's public Shopping Queries Dataset labels each query-product pair Exact, Substitute, Complement or
Irrelevant ([ESCI, Reddy et al., 2022](https://github.com/amazon-science/esci-data)), which maps directly onto
graded nDCG gains. Mapping them to 3/2/1/0 is one reasonable choice, not the dataset's rule. ChatGPT calls this
search as a tool, so it should return structured fields (title, price, ID, why it matched), not prose.

## Small-team version

At a 50–500 person company this exercise *is* the first version of the product. One engineer writes a few hundred
lines, and then has to show the CEO a number.

### Build

- **The pipeline itself.** It is the code above. Plain API calls rather than a framework is Anthropic's advice
  too: "start by using LLM APIs directly"
  ([Building effective agents, 2024-12-19](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents)).
- **The labeled set and the eval script.** 50 questions labeled by someone who knows the domain, a script that
  prints hit@5, recall@5, MRR and nDCG@10 with intervals, and a check that runs on every change to chunking,
  prompts or models.
- **Citation checks and abstention.** They are free, and they keep a wrong answer from looking authoritative.

### Buy or call as a service

- **Embeddings and generation from a hosted API.** OpenAI's text-embedding-3-small lists at $0.02 per million
  tokens ([OpenAI pricing](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/pricing), fetched 2026-10-11), so embedding a
  5,000-document corpus of 10M tokens costs about **$0.20**. Embedding cost never decides this.
- **Vector storage where your data already lives.** In-process NumPy up to about a million vectors; pgvector (a
  Postgres extension that stores and searches vectors) if you run Postgres. A practitioner who ran pgvector in
  production warns that HNSW builds on a few million vectors can need 10+ GB of RAM on the primary database, and
  recommends a dedicated store past that unless you have strong Postgres expertise
  ([Alex Jacobs, 2025-10-29](https://alex-jacobs.com/posts/the-case-against-pgvector/)).
- **Tracing.** Log every query's retrieved IDs, scores, prompt and answer, with an open-source tracer such as
  Langfuse or a vendor. You will need those traces the first time someone reports a wrong answer.

### Skip, until a number says otherwise

- **A reranker.** The evidence is mixed. It was the only lab stage that cleared its interval on the lab's SciFact
  run, and it added nothing on top of a well-tokenized hybrid in this page's run. Add it when your own eval shows
  a gain worth its latency.
- **Fine-tuning the embedding model, a dedicated vector database on day one, and an agent framework.**

### A realistic delivery plan

1. **Days 1–2.** Pipeline, 20 questions, a baseline number, and a README listing what fails.
2. **Week 1.** Grow the set to 50 (paraphrases, identifiers, and questions the corpus can't answer). Compare
   dense, BM25 and hybrid with paired intervals, and pick the simplest configuration that wins.
3. **Week 2.** Ship behind a feedback button, with tracing on. Read 20–50 real traces a week and turn each new
   failure into a labeled question.
4. **Ongoing.** Every chunking, prompt or model change runs the eval before merge.

### What you verify yourself

- The metric code, against a hand-worked example like the refund question.
- That IDs stay aligned through dedupe and batching.
- That eval questions weren't generated from the same chunks they are scored on.
- How many chunks the embedder truncates.
- A sample of the judge's verdicts, against your own.

### What small-company interviewers probe

## Common mistakes

- **Tuning by feel.** With no labeled set, "it works" is an anecdote; in the prep guide's words, "the
  interviewer assumes you tuned yours by feel" (<Cite id="s-7d03a572e0" />).
- **Calling a hit rate "recall".** They are different numbers, so say which one you computed and at what k.
- **Declaring a winner on 20 questions.** The ±17.5-point interval swamps most differences, so use a paired
  comparison and more questions.
- **Generating the eval questions from the chunks you are retrieving.** The question shares the chunk's words,
  so BM25 and dense search both look better than they are.
- **Silent truncation.** Chunks longer than the embedder's window lose their tails, so measure how many do.
- **Row and ID misalignment.** Dedupe, sorting, batching or normalizing along the wrong axis can attach scores
  to the wrong chunks without any error.
- **Fixed 512-token windows.** They cut sentences in half, which is why the prep guide calls them "the lazy
  default".
- **No abstention path.** A question the corpus can't answer still gets a fluent, confident answer.

## The prompt

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "An LLM judge is a classifier, so I won't trust it until I've measured it. I'll write one pass/fail judge per
> failure mode, have a person label a set of examples, and compare. I'll report how many real failures the judge
> catches and how many good answers it wrongly fails, with intervals, plus an agreement score that corrects for
> luck. The bar for letting it replace people is that it agrees with people about as well as two people agree
> with each other, on examples I never tuned it on. And I'll re-check it whenever the model, the prompt or the
> traffic changes."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer, so you can say it with understanding.

### Words you need first

A **large language model (LLM)** is a model that reads text and writes text back. The text you send it is the
**prompt**. Many products first look up documents that might help (for example, the three most relevant help
articles) and paste them into the prompt. Those pasted documents are the **retrieved context**, and this
look-up-then-answer pattern is called **RAG** (retrieval-augmented generation).

An **LLM judge** (the interview phrase is **LLM-as-judge**) is a second LLM call whose only job is to grade the
first one's output. You give it the answer, whatever it needs to check that answer, and a written set of rules,
the **rubric**. It returns a grade, ideally just "pass" or "fail" (a **binary** grade). Each judge should check one **failure
mode**: one specific way an answer can go wrong, such as making up facts or ignoring the question.

A **human label** is the grade a person gives the same output. The person is an **annotator** (or labeller).

### What makes this different from testing normal code

You already know how to test a backend. A unit test has an exact expected value: the function returns 42 or it
doesn't. The test itself is trusted, because it is a few lines of code anyone can read.

LLM output breaks both halves of that. First, there is no single right answer. "Your refund window is 14 days"
and "You can get a refund within two weeks of purchase" are both correct. An exact string
comparison would accept at most one of them. Second, the thing doing the checking is itself an LLM. It is fast and cheap, but it makes mistakes,
and its mistakes are not random: it has habits, such as liking longer answers.

So the job in this family is not "write a judge". It is **measure the judge**, the way you would measure a
flaky health check before letting it page someone. You compare its grades with people's grades, you count the
kinds of mistakes it makes, and you decide what it is allowed to decide.

Two more terms come up in every version of the question:

- **Faithfulness**: does the answer stick to the retrieved context, or does it claim things the context doesn't
  support? An unfaithful answer is what people call a hallucination.
- **Relevance**: does the answer actually address the question that was asked?

### The four questions in the bank

All four ask the same thing from different angles: when can an automated grader be trusted, and what do you do
where it can't?

- **The take-home.** You get a prompt and about 30 input/output pairs with human labels. You must build an
  automatic faithfulness and relevance check and calibrate it against the labels. The techinterview.org
  applied-AI prep guide describes this as "a common take-home" with "thirty sample input/output pairs"; it is the

### The example that runs through this page

We will follow one take-home from start to finish. A company runs a support chatbot. For each customer question
it retrieves help articles and writes an answer. You get 30 of those answers, each labelled by a support lead as
**pass** or **fail** for faithfulness. Here is one failing pair (an illustrative example, not from a real
dataset):

| Part | Content |
|---|---|
| Question | "Can I return my headphones after 30 days?" |
| Retrieved context | "Returns are accepted within 14 days of delivery. Opened audio products cannot be returned." |
| Answer | "Yes, you have 30 days to return any product." |
| Human label | **fail** (the answer contradicts the context) |

Of the 30 pairs, the support lead marked **9 fail and 21 pass**. Keep those two numbers in mind; most of the
arithmetic on this page comes from them.

## What is being tested

The applied-AI guide is blunt about the trap. It shows a short faithfulness judge and says the interviewer is
checking "whether you'd trust its output before testing it against humans. The right answer is no."
(<Cite id="s-7d03a572e0" />). What they want instead is **calibration**: grade a labelled set with the judge,
compare with the human labels, measure agreement, and tighten the rubric until the two line up.

Under that, the interviewer is looking for three skills.

1. **Measuring a measurement.** A judge is a classifier, just like a spam filter. You need its error rates, with
   honest uncertainty, on data it was not tuned on. You also need to say what 30 labels can and cannot show.
2. **Knowing where human judgment is weak.** People disagree too. If two experts agree only moderately, no judge
   can be held to more than that. Some disagreement is a vague rubric; some is genuine taste.
3. **Product sense for outputs judged by taste.** For generated video or creative writing there is no reference
   answer at all. You split quality into parts you can check, ask people to compare outputs instead of scoring
   them, and tie the result to what users actually do.

My reading: interviewers care less about the judge's prompt than about the numbers you put next to it. They also
want to see that you know the judge's quality is not fixed. It drifts when the judge model, its prompt or the mix
of inputs changes.

Say it like this: "The judge prompt is the easy part. The work is proving how often it's wrong, and in which
direction."

## Clarify and scope

Spend the first few minutes pinning down what the judge is for. Each question below changes the design.

- **What decision will the score drive?** "If the judge blocks a release, it must catch most real failures. If it
  only feeds a trend dashboard, I can tolerate more noise."
- **Pass/fail or a score?** Push for pass/fail per failure mode. Hamel Husain and Shreya Shankar's evals FAQ puts
  it this way: "Binary evaluations force clearer thinking and more consistent labeling." A 1-to-10 score (a
  **Likert scale**) invites every annotator to use the scale differently.
- **Who made the labels, and how?** One expert or several? With a written rubric? Were the labels themselves
  checked for agreement?
- **How many failures are there?** "If only 3 of the 30 are failures, everything I say about catching failures
  rests on 3 examples." In our example there are 9.
- **One answer at a time, or comparing two?** Grading one answer against a rubric is called **pointwise**.
  Asking "which of these two answers is better?" is called **pairwise**. They have different biases, covered
  below.
- **Which judge model, at what cost?** "Can I pin an exact model version, so the judge doesn't change under me?"

Out of scope: training a reward model (a model trained to score outputs, used to train another model further), and
the release gate that consumes the judge's scores. That gate is the
[eval-system](/system-design-labs/interviews/walkthroughs/eval-system/) family.

## Core concepts

### The system in one picture

There are two loops. The **calibration loop** runs offline, on labelled examples, and tells you how good the
judge is. The **production loop** runs on live traffic and uses the judge, corrected by what calibration found.

```
            CALIBRATION (offline, on labelled examples)
 ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
 │  Sample of real outputs                                      │
 │        │                                                     │
 │        ▼                                                     │
 │  Domain expert labels each one pass / fail  ──► label set    │
 │        │                                        │            │
 │        │                         split into dev  and  test   │
 │        ▼                                   │          │      │
 │  Judge prompt ◄── tune on dev only ────────┘          │      │
 │        │                                              │      │
 │        ▼                                              ▼      │
 │  Judge grades test set ──► compare with labels ──► report    │
 │                            (TPR, TNR, kappa, intervals)      │
 └──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
                                  │  judge version + its error rates
                                  ▼
            PRODUCTION (online, on live traffic)
 ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
 │  Live answers ──► sampler ──► judges (async, one per         │
 │                               failure mode)                  │
 │                                    │                         │
 │                                    ▼                         │
 │                 raw failure rate ──► corrected rate          │
 │                                    │                         │
 │                                    ▼                         │
 │                    dashboard / release gate / alerts         │
 └──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

**Sample of real outputs.** You label outputs your system actually produced, not invented ones. Real outputs
contain the failures you actually have.

**Domain expert labels.** One person who knows what a correct answer looks like grades each output pass or fail.
This is the expensive, slow, irreplaceable part.

**Dev and test split.** You divide the labels into two groups. You look at the **dev** set while you improve the
judge's prompt. You only use the **test** set at the end, to report numbers. This is the same reason you don't
evaluate a model on its training data.

**Judge prompt.** The rubric plus instructions, sent to an LLM with each output to grade.

**Report.** The judge's two error rates (TPR and TNR in the diagram) and its agreement score (kappa), each with an
interval. The next sections explain all four.

**Production loop.** The judge runs on a sample of live answers, in the background. Its raw failure rate is then
corrected using the error rates from calibration, and the corrected number feeds dashboards and gates.

### The judge is a classifier: count its two kinds of mistake

Decide which outcome is the "positive" one. In this family the positive class is **fail**: an unfaithful or
irrelevant answer. That is the thing you are hunting for, like spam in a spam filter.

Now compare the judge's grade with the human label on each pair. The judge can be wrong in two ways, and they
cost different things.

- **True-positive rate (TPR)**: of the answers people marked fail, the share the judge also failed. A judge with
  low TPR lets bad answers through. In a release gate, that is how regressions ship.
- **True-negative rate (TNR)**: of the answers people marked pass, the share the judge also passed. A judge with
  low TNR raises false alarms. People stop trusting it and start ignoring it.

Why not just report **accuracy**, the share of all pairs where judge and human agree? Because accuracy hides
which kind of mistake is being made. Say 10% of answers fail. A "judge" that passes everything is 90% accurate
and catches nothing.

Here is our example. The judge was run on the 30 labelled pairs.

| | Human said **fail** (9) | Human said **pass** (21) |
|---|---|---|
| Judge said **fail** | 7 (caught) | 2 (false alarm) |
| Judge said **pass** | 2 (missed) | 19 (correctly passed) |

So **TPR is 7 of 9, about 78%**, and **TNR is 19 of 21, about 90%**. The judge agreed with the human on 26 of
30 pairs.

Say it like this: "I report TPR and TNR, not accuracy. Accuracy can be 90% for a judge that never fails anything."

The FAQ recommends exactly these two numbers. It also says to measure them on a held-out test split, after you
tune the judge prompt on a separate dev split. Tuning on the same labels you report on overfits the judge, even
if those labels never appear in its prompt: you keep editing until it gets those particular examples right.

### Uncertainty: what 30 labels can and cannot show

"78%" sounds precise. It isn't. It rests on 9 examples. If the support lead had happened to pick 9 slightly
different failing answers, the judge might have caught 6 or 9 of them.

A **confidence interval** is the range of true rates that are consistent with what you observed. The usual
choice for a small pass/fail count is the **Wilson interval**, which behaves sensibly near 0% and 100%. You
don't need its formula in the interview; you need its results:

| Measure | Value | 95% interval (Wilson) |
|---|---|---|
| Agreement | 26 of 30 = 86.7% | 70% to 95% |
| TPR | 7 of 9 = 78% | **45% to 94%** |
| TNR | 19 of 21 = 90% | 71% to 97% |

Read the TPR row out loud: the judge catches somewhere between about half and nearly all real failures. That is
not enough to let it block a release. With 50 labelled failures and the same observed rate (39 of 50), the
interval narrows to roughly **65% to 87%**. That is why the FAQ asks for at least 50 failing and 50 passing
examples before you trust a judge.

Say it like this: "On 30 labels the judge looks promising, but its catch rate is known only to within about
plus or minus 25 points. I wouldn't let it gate anything yet."

### Agreement that corrects for luck: Cohen's kappa

Agreement of 86.7% sounds good, but some of it would happen by chance. Imagine a judge that ignores the answer
and says "pass" 70% of the time at random. Because the human also says "pass" about 70% of the time, they would
often agree by luck alone.

**Cohen's kappa** (written κ) removes that luck. It compares the agreement you observed with the agreement you
would expect if the two raters were guessing at their usual rates. Kappa is 1 for perfect agreement and 0 for
agreement no better than chance.

In our example both the human and the judge said "fail" on 9 of 30 items (30%) and "pass" on 21 (70%). Chance
alone would give about 58% agreement. The judge got 86.7%. That works out to **κ = 0.68**. And a judge that
always says "pass" would agree 70% of the time with **κ = 0**.

- **Why the always-pass judge gets κ = 0:** it says pass 100% of the time, so p_e = 1.0 × 0.7 + 0 × 0.3 = 0.7. Its
  observed agreement is also 0.7. So κ = (0.7 − 0.7) ÷ 0.3 = 0.
- **Wilson interval for TPR, 7 of 9:** p = 0.778, z = 1.96. Centre = (p + z² ÷ 2n) ÷ (1 + z² ÷ n) = 0.695;
  half-width = z × √(p(1 − p) ÷ n + z² ÷ 4n²) ÷ (1 + z² ÷ n) = 0.242. Interval **0.45 to 0.94**.
- **With 39 of 50:** the same formula gives **0.65 to 0.87**.

The script that produced the interval table is in the Worked examples section; it was run on 2026-10-12.

</details>

Kappa is the right number when you compare judge-versus-human with human-versus-human, because both are
agreements between two raters. With more than two raters, or with some items missing labels, **Fleiss' kappa**
or **Krippendorff's alpha** play the same role. The FAQ recommends a chance-corrected measure such as Cohen's
kappa whenever more than one person annotates.

### Inter-annotator agreement is the ceiling

Before asking how well the judge agrees with people, ask how well people agree with each other. That number is
called **inter-annotator agreement**. Measure it by having two annotators label the same items without seeing
each other's labels, then compute kappa between them.

The FAQ's workflow: measure agreement, sit down together over the disagreements, sharpen the rubric, and repeat
until agreement is consistently high. The disagreements are the useful part. Each one shows where the definition
of "faithful" is ambiguous. For example: does a correct fact that isn't in the context count as unfaithful? Two
reasonable people can disagree until the rubric says.

This gives you the test for replacing people. A judge may stand in for an annotator on one failure mode when:

1. its agreement with the human consensus is about as high as one annotator's agreement with another,
2. on a held-out set it was never tuned on,
3. and it stays there when you re-check it on fresh labels.

That is the framing of Zheng et al.'s MT-Bench paper (2023), which tested GPT-4 as a judge of chat answers. It
reported GPT-4 matched human preferences, "achieving over 80% agreement, the same level of agreement between
humans" (arXiv 2306.05685). Treat that as evidence the method can work. It says nothing about today's judge on
your data.

Say it like this: "I can't hold a judge to a higher standard than my annotators meet. Human-to-human agreement is
the bar."

### The judge's known habits (biases) and how to neutralise them

LLM judges make mistakes in predictable directions. The applied-AI guide lists three: judges "favor longer
answers", favour "whichever response comes first when comparing two", and "lean toward text from their own model
family" (<Cite id="s-7d03a572e0" />). The MT-Bench paper names the same three: **position bias** (preferring the
first answer shown), **verbosity bias** (preferring longer answers) and **self-enhancement bias** (preferring
output from its own model family). It adds a fourth weakness: limited reasoning on maths.

| Bias | What it looks like | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Position (pairwise) | Swap the two answers and the winner changes | Run each pair in both orders. Count a win only if it holds in both; otherwise call it a tie (MT-Bench's conservative method) |
| Verbosity | Longer answers pass more often, even when they say no more | Grade one failure mode at a time with a pass/fail rubric. Compare answers of similar length. Check the judge's pass rate by length bucket |
| Self-preference | The judge favours text written by its own model family | Measure it: compare agreement on outputs from its own family with agreement on others. The FAQ says using the same model is "usually fine" for narrow pass/fail tasks if TPR and TNR on held-out labels are high |
| Weak reasoning | Wrong grades on maths or multi-step logic | Give the judge a reference answer or the exact context it needs, and let it answer "Unknown" (Anthropic's agent-evals guide, in Sources) |

The sources disagree on self-preference, so say both views. The guide treats it as a known bias. The FAQ treats it
as something your held-out agreement numbers will either show or not.

### Correcting the judge's failure rate on live traffic

Once you know TPR and TNR, you can fix the judge's raw numbers in production. The FAQ: "Once you know your judge's
True Positive and True Negative rates, you can correct its estimates to determine the actual failure rate in your
system."

The idea: the judge's raw failure rate mixes real failures it caught with good answers it wrongly failed. If you
know how often each happens, you can work backwards. Suppose the judge fails 12% of live answers, with TPR 0.85
and TNR 0.95. The true failure rate is about **9%, not 12%**. The difference is false alarms.

This correction is only as good as the TPR and TNR behind it. If the judge model changes, or the traffic shifts
to a new kind of question, re-measure first.

## Worked examples

### The 30-pair take-home, step by step

Back to the support chatbot. Here is the take-home as a sequence of decisions, with what to write in your README
at each step.

| Step | What you do | What to say in the README |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Split the problem | Write **two** judges, one for faithfulness and one for relevance | "One judge per failure mode is easier to check against labels. The FAQ calls one judge for many kinds of error the most common mistake." |
| 2. Use code where code can decide | A plain check that every cited source actually exists in the retrieved context | "Deterministic checks first; the LLM only for what needs judgment." |
| 3. Write the faithfulness judge | Input: retrieved context and answer. Output: pass/fail plus a list of unsupported claims | "Asking for the unsupported claims makes each fail easy to audit." |
| 4. Write the relevance judge | Input: question and answer. Output: pass/fail | "Relevance doesn't need the context at all." |
| 5. Split the 30 | Tune on about 15 (dev), report on the other 15 (test) | "30 is too few for both tuning and reporting. I'm saying so up front." |
| 6. Report | TPR, TNR, agreement and kappa, each with an interval | "Promising, not proven. Here's what 50+50 labels would tell us." |
| 7. Show disagreements | Each pair where judge and human differ, and what it taught you | "For each miss: what the judge got wrong, and which rubric line I changed because of it." |

The numbers in the Core concepts section use all 30 pairs to keep the arithmetic easy. On a real 15/15 split each
half has only 4 or 5 failures, so the test-set intervals are even wider. That is worth saying, because it shows
you understand why 30 labels is a starting point, not a result.

Here is the short script that computes the report. It was run on 2026-10-12.

```python
from math import sqrt

def wilson(k, n, z=1.96):
    p = k / n
    den = 1 + z * z / n
    mid = (p + z * z / (2 * n)) / den
    half = z * sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n + z * z / (4 * n * n)) / den
    return mid - half, mid + half

def agreement_report(human, judge):
    """Lists of 'pass' / 'fail' in the same order. 'fail' is the positive class."""
    n = len(human)
    tp = sum(h == j == "fail" for h, j in zip(human, judge))
    tn = sum(h == j == "pass" for h, j in zip(human, judge))
    pos, neg = human.count("fail"), human.count("pass")
    p_o = (tp + tn) / n
    p_h, p_j = pos / n, judge.count("fail") / n
    p_e = p_h * p_j + (1 - p_h) * (1 - p_j)
    return {
        "accuracy": (p_o, wilson(tp + tn, n)),
        "kappa": (p_o - p_e) / (1 - p_e),
        "TPR": (tp / pos, wilson(tp, pos)),
        "TNR": (tn / neg, wilson(tn, neg)),
    }
```

Fed our 30 labels, it gives:

| Measure | Value | 95% interval (Wilson) |
|---|---|---|
| Agreement | 26 of 30 = 86.7% | 70% to 95% |
| Cohen's kappa | 0.68 | (too few items for a useful interval) |
| TPR | 7 of 9 = 78% | 45% to 94% |
| TNR | 19 of 21 = 90% | 71% to 97% |

What to write in the README's conclusion: on 30 labels the judge looks promising, but its TPR is known only to
within about ±25 points, which is not enough to let it block a release. With 50 failing and 50 passing labels (the
FAQ's minimum for debugging a judge) and the same observed rate, 39 of 50, the TPR interval narrows to roughly 65%
to 87%. Then list what you would label next, and keep a test split you never tuned on.

Say it like this: "The judge is ready to be a dashboard signal, not a release blocker. Here is the labelling plan
that would change that."

### Video generation, judged by taste

The Amazon question moves the same method to a harder place. A text-to-video model takes a prompt such as "a red
car driving through rain at night" and produces a few seconds of video. There is no correct video to compare
against, and two viewers can honestly disagree about which of two clips is better.

The move is to separate what a machine can measure from what only people can decide.

**What a machine can measure, one dimension at a time:**

- **Realism across a whole set of videos.** **Fréchet Video Distance (FVD)** runs generated videos and real videos
  through a feature extractor and measures how far apart the two sets of features are. Lower is closer to real.
  Its authors report it correlates well with human judgment (Unterthiner et al., arXiv 1812.01717). Its limits:
  it needs a reference set of real videos, and it scores a whole set, so it says nothing about one prompt.
- **Specific defects.** **VBench** splits video quality into 16 dimensions, such as whether the subject keeps the
  same identity across frames, motion smoothness, flickering between frames, and spatial relationships ("the cup
  is left of the plate"). Each dimension was validated against human preference labels (arXiv 2311.17982).
- **Does the video match the prompt?** **CLIP** is a model that scores how well an image matches a caption.
  Scoring each frame against the prompt gives a rough adherence score (CLIPScore was proposed for image captions,
  arXiv 2104.08718). Or ask a **multimodal judge** (an LLM that can also look at images or video) narrow pass/fail
  questions: "Is there a red car?" "Is it raining?" Calibrate that judge against human labels exactly as in the
  take-home.
- **Operational numbers.** Generation time, cost per accepted video, and failure rate.

**When users judge by their own taste:**

- **Ask people to compare, not to score.** "Which of these two videos better fits this prompt?" is easier to
  answer consistently than "rate this from 1 to 10". **Chatbot Arena** ranks chat models from crowdsourced
  pairwise votes with a **Bradley–Terry model**, a statistical model that turns many "A beat B" votes into one
  strength score per model (arXiv 2403.04132). The same works for two video models.
- **Measure agreement per dimension.** If raters agree strongly on visual defects and weakly on style, the defect
  disagreements are errors and the style disagreements are taste.
- **Split results by user group.** Report win rates per segment. A model that wins with marketing teams can lose
  with film editors, and an average hides that.
- **Watch what users do.** Regenerate rate, download or share rate, and edits after generation are preference
  signals users give you for free.
- **Calibrate any automatic judge per dimension**, against the human pairwise data, not against one overall score.

Say it like this: "For taste, I don't look for one score. I measure defects automatically, ask people to compare
pairs, and check whether different user groups want different things."

## Common follow-ups in depth

### "When would you trust an LLM judge over human judges?"

Never in general. Per failure mode, when four things hold. People agree with each other well enough that the label
means something. The judge's agreement with the human consensus, on held-out items, is close to the
human-to-human level. Its TPR is high enough for the decision it drives. And you re-check it on fresh labels on a
schedule.

Anthropic's agent-evals guide keeps people in the loop for exactly this: "Reserve systematic human studies for
calibrating LLM graders or evaluating subjective outputs where human consensus serves as the reference standard."
People stay better for new failure modes nobody has labelled yet, for high-stakes individual decisions, and for
taste.

### "The judge disagrees with people on 20% of items. What do you do?"

Read the disagreements before touching the prompt. The FAQ lists the usual causes: the judge is missing context,
the instructions are vague, or the labels are wrong. It also warns that automated prompt tuning, done too early,
hides problems that are not about the prompt. A quick test: if you can't decide the right label yourself, the
rubric is the problem, not the judge.

### "Your labels themselves drift"

The FAQ calls this **criteria drift**: reviewing outputs changes what you think "good" means. Treat the rubric
like code. Version it. When it changes, relabel the examples it affects and re-measure the judge against the new
labels.

### "Pointwise or pairwise grading?"

Pointwise grading (one answer against a rubric) gives an absolute pass rate you can track over time and gate on.
It suits pass/fail failure modes such as "makes a claim the context does not support".

Pairwise grading (which of two answers is better) is easier for people and judges to answer consistently when
quality is a matter of degree. It is the natural design for comparing two prompts or two models. Its costs: you
must control position bias by running both orders, ties need a rule, and the result is a preference between two
versions, not a quality level. It can't tell you whether either version is good enough to ship.

A common split: pointwise pass/fail judges for regression gates and production monitoring, pairwise comparison
for choosing between candidate prompts or models, and people for the pairs where the judge's two orderings
disagree.

### "How many labels do you need?"

Work backwards from the uncertainty you can tolerate on the rate that matters, usually TPR. With 9 labelled
failures the 95% interval on TPR spans about 50 points. With 50 failures it spans about 20 points.

If failures are rare in production, don't label random traffic where 95% of items pass. Sample on purpose:
collect failures from **error analysis** (reading through real conversations and tagging what went wrong) until
you have enough.

### "Can the judge run inline as a guardrail?"

A **guardrail** is a check that runs before the answer reaches the user and can block it. The FAQ says you would
"almost never" use a slow, non-deterministic LLM judge as a synchronous guardrail. Run judges in the background on
samples. Keep inline checks fast and deterministic, such as a regex or a schema check.

## Small-team version

At a company of 50 to 500 people you have no labelling team. That is exactly why you lean on judges, and why a
cheap calibration check is your insurance against optimising toward a broken metric.

### Build, buy, skip

- **Build** the judges: plain API calls, one per failure mode, with a pinned model version. Build the agreement
  report above, and a small annotation screen where the expert sees one output and clicks pass or fail. The FAQ
  reports that teams with custom annotation tools "iterate ~10x faster". A coding agent can plausibly build a
  simple one in hours (an estimate, not a sourced figure).
- **Buy** nothing specific. If you already run a tracing tool with annotation queues (Langfuse self-hosted, or a
  hosted platform), use its queue to collect labels.
- **Skip** crowdsourced labellers for your core failure modes (the FAQ advises against outsourcing error
  analysis), judge models you train yourself, and 1-to-10 rubrics.

### Default, and when to change it

One domain expert labels. The FAQ calls this person the "benevolent dictator", and recommends it for most small
and medium companies. Add a second annotator and measure kappa when the domain is regulated or the expert's calls
are contested. Start with a capable judge model, then try a cheaper one and measure how much TPR and TNR you lose.

### What it costs

The labels cost more than the tokens. They are also the part you can't skip.

- **Labels:** 100 labels per judge is about **3.3 hours** of expert time, about **$310**.
- **Judge calls:** about **half a cent per call**, so 1,000 judged outputs cost about **$4.75**.
- **Production:** judging every one of an assumed 600 answers a day with two judges is about **$5.70 a day**,
  about **$170 a month**.

### Delivery plan

**Week 1:** read 30 or more real conversations, write down the failure modes you see, and label 100 examples per
failure mode with dev and test splits. **Week 2:** write the judges, tune on dev, and report TPR, TNR and kappa
with intervals on test. Publish the disagreements. **After that:** a monthly re-check on 20 to 30 fresh labels per
judge, and an immediate one whenever the judge model changes.

### What you check, and what interviewers probe

You personally check the labels, the held-out split, and every change to a judge prompt. Small-company
interviewers tend to ask what you did when you had no labels, how many you needed, and how you knew the judge was
still right three months later.

<AINative title="Let an agent tune the judge; keep the labels and the test split yours">
An agent can draft judge prompts, build the annotation screen, and iterate a judge prompt against your dev
labels; the FAQ suggests giving it one specific failure to detect and a way to measure progress against the
labels. What you keep: the first 30 or more labels, written before any agent suggests failure modes, and a test
split the agent never sees, because an agent optimizing against labels it can read will overfit them. Check
every diff to a rubric or a label as carefully as a code diff.
</AINative>

### Revisit when

Volume makes weekly label refreshes a real cost, several teams share one judge, or a regulator asks for documented
human review.

## Follow-ups

<FollowUps>
  <FollowUp q="(techinterview.org applied-AI guide) Would you trust the judge's output before testing it against people?">
No. Report its agreement with human labels on held-out data first, with TPR, TNR and kappa, and say how
many labels that rests on.
  </FollowUp>
  <FollowUp q="(Reported title, Apple, PracHub) What is inter-annotator agreement and why does it matter for judges?">
The agreement between people labelling the same items independently, usually chance-corrected with Cohen's
kappa. It is the ceiling for a judge and a test of the rubric: low agreement means the label is ill-defined.
  </FollowUp>
  <FollowUp q="(Reported, Amazon) Users have subjective standards for generated videos. How do you evaluate?">
Pairwise preferences per dimension, agreement measured to separate error from taste, Bradley–Terry rankings,
per-segment win rates, and behavioural signals such as regenerate and share rates.
  </FollowUp>
  <FollowUp q="(Not reported; a natural next push) The provider deprecates your judge model. Now what?">
Run old and new judges side by side on the held-out labels and one suite version, re-validate TPR and TNR, and
record the offset so historical scores stay comparable.
  </FollowUp>
  <FollowUp q="(Not reported; a natural next push) Your judge has 95% accuracy. Is it good?">
Depends on the base rate. With 5% failures, a judge that passes everything is 95% accurate. Ask for TPR on the
failures and kappa.
  </FollowUp>
</FollowUps>

## Common mistakes

- **Trusting the judge before testing it.** The guide's answer is "no" (<Cite id="s-7d03a572e0" />).
- **Reporting accuracy alone.** It hides class balance and chance agreement; report TPR, TNR and kappa.
- **Tuning and reporting on the same labels.** The judge overfits them; keep a test split you never looked at
  while tuning (FAQ).
- **One judge for everything.** The FAQ calls it the most common mistake; split by failure mode.
- **Ignoring human disagreement.** Without inter-annotator agreement you don't know the ceiling.
- **Averaging taste.** One average hides user groups that want opposite things; report per-segment preferences.

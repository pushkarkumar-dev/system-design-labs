## The prompt

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "This design will fall over under a burst, and it will lose work when a GPU dies. I'd make three changes
> first. Bound the queue by how long a request would wait, and turn extra requests away early with a 'try again
> later'. Retry in one place only, with a cap on how many retries we allow. And give each batch a lease, so a dead
> GPU's work goes back on the queue instead of vanishing. After those, I'd fix the capacity math, which assumes
> every batch is full and every GPU is 100% busy."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer, so you can say it with understanding.

In this round you don't design anything from a blank whiteboard. You are handed someone else's design document
(a "design doc") for a service that runs a machine-learning model on GPUs, and you are asked to read it, walk
through how it works, say what is wrong, and say what you would change first.

You already know how to review a normal backend design. A normal web service is cheap per request. A server
reads a few rows, answers in a few milliseconds, and any server can take the next request. If a burst arrives,
you add servers, and they come up in seconds.

A model-serving design is different in three ways, and every finding in this round comes from one of them:

1. **Each request is expensive, and it runs on scarce hardware.** The work happens on GPUs (graphics processors
   used for the large matrix math a model needs). They are costly and you can't add more in seconds. So when
   traffic exceeds capacity, the design has to decide who waits and who is turned away. A web service can often
   just scale out instead.
2. **Requests are grouped before they run.** A GPU does many requests at once almost as fast as one, so the
   service holds requests for a moment and sends them together. That makes the service far more efficient, but
   it adds a wait, and it means one failure takes many requests down with it.
3. **The GPU is busy for a long time per group.** If the machine dies halfway, everything in that group is lost
   unless the design says how to get it back.

So the reviewer's job is mostly about **overload** (too much traffic) and **failure** (a part dies), with a
quick check of the doc's numbers. You don't need to be a GPU expert. You need the instincts you already have
from queues and retries, applied to a system where every request is costly.

### Words you need first

- **Inference** means running a trained model on an input to get an output. It's the "serving" side of machine
  learning, as opposed to training.
- A **batch** is a group of requests the GPU processes in one go. **Batch size** is how many requests are in it.
  Grouping requests this way is called **batching**.
- **QPS** is queries (requests) per second, as in any backend.
- **Utilization**, written **ρ** ("rho"), is the fraction of time a GPU is busy. A fleet sized for ρ = 1.0
  (always busy) has no room for a burst, so real designs aim lower.

## What is being tested

The interviewer is checking four things. Each one comes from what candidates and prep sites report.

**Review judgment, not invention.** Can you find the two or three flaws that matter most and leave the rest?
The engineering-manager (EM) candidate expected a blank-sheet design. Instead "they showed me a partially built
design and asked me to review it", on inference and batch inference, and found it "much more about design
judgment, communication, and review skills than deep calculations" (<Cite id="s-4e3dc29a95" />). PracHub's
Anthropic ML system design list carries similar titles (<Cite id="s-3650fe20d8" />).

**Depth on failure and overload.** The Staff candidate's round, titled "Design Doc Review - Batch Sampling
Service", "asked me to review an existing design doc and give feedback". After pointing out a few issues, "it
becomes a typical deep dive session on failure handling, overload protection". Their advice: "Have a deep
understanding of the LLM inference stack and the failure scenarios" (<Cite id="s-eab681003c" />). ("Sampling"
here means generating output from a model, and an LLM is a large language model, the kind that writes text.)

**Checking the doc's math.** PracHub lists an onsite titled "Review a Batched GPU Inference API Design Doc:
Flaws, Throughput, Mixed-Model GPUs". The page says "The real document was not reported". Its body is the site's
own illustrative stand-in, with parts on flaws, throughput (100 ms per batch at 10,000 QPS) and two models on
one 8-GPU machine (<Cite id="s-f24bae1f6b" />). Only the title is reported; the numbers are the site's. They are
still good practice, so this page uses them.

**How you say it.** A design review is a conversation with an author. A Fireworks prep guide describes a good
comment: "A useful review comment names behavior, a reproducer and a narrow change" (<Cite id="s-b6632d5df9" />).
Behaviour is what goes wrong, a reproducer is how to make it happen on demand, and a narrow change is the
smallest fix.

Say this early: "I'll walk the flow first, then check the numbers, then go through overload, failure, mixed
models and monitoring, and finish with my top three changes."

## Clarify and scope

Before you critique anything, ask the doc's author (played by the interviewer) the questions the doc doesn't
answer. A missing answer is itself a finding: if the doc never says who calls it or how long they wait, nobody
has designed the timeout.

| Question | Why it matters | If unanswered, assume and say so |
|---|---|---|
| Who calls this, and do they wait for the answer? | Decides timeouts and how results get back | Interactive callers with a 1 s timeout |
| What is the latency target, and does the 100 ms batch time count inside it? | Decides whether batching fits at all | p99 of 300 ms end to end |
| What is the traffic: average, peak, burstiness, mix of customers? | Capacity and fairness | 10,000 QPS peak; one customer can spike |
| Is a request safe to retry? | Failure handling | Yes, it has no side effects |
| One model on the fleet, or several? | Placement and queueing | Two models |
| What does "batch" mean here? | Changes how waste and waiting behave | Fixed-cost passes first |

Two terms in that table. **p99** is the 99th-percentile latency, as in any backend. A **tenant** is one customer
or team sharing the service. "One tenant can spike" means one customer can suddenly send far more than usual.

The last question needs a sentence of background. For a classifier or an embedding model (one that turns text
into a list of numbers), every request in a batch costs about the same, and the batch finishes in a fixed time.
For a model that writes text, each request produces a different number of output words. Then you must ask
whether the doc uses **static batching** (the whole group waits for its longest request) or **continuous
batching** (finished requests leave and new ones join between steps). The rest of this page assumes fixed-cost
batches unless it says otherwise.

Then say what you'll cover and what you won't: "I'll cover the flow, capacity, overload, failure, mixed models
and monitoring, then rank. I won't rewrite the whole design."

## Back-of-envelope

Check the doc's numbers before its boxes. The doc says: "batches of 32 every 100 ms give 320 QPS per GPU, so
32 GPUs serve 10,000 QPS." Two checks cover most reviews: how many GPUs that really takes, and whether two
models can share the machines the way the doc proposes.

### How many GPUs the doc's numbers really need

The idea is simple. Each GPU can serve (batch size ÷ batch time) requests per second. Divide peak traffic by
that and you have the GPU count at 100% busy. Then divide by the utilization you are willing to run at, because
a fleet that is always busy has nowhere to put a burst.

The doc made two optimistic choices. It assumed every batch is **full** (32 requests), and it assumed every GPU
is **100% busy**. Fix only the second, aiming for ρ = 0.7, and the fleet goes from 32 GPUs to **45**. Fix both,
because batches only fill to 8 at some times of day, and you need about **179**. The doc's fleet needs 40% more
GPUs at best and more than five times as many at worst.

Say it like this: "The doc sizes at the maximum batch and 100% busy. At a 0.7 utilization target that's 45 GPUs,
not 32, and if batches only fill to 8 it's closer to 180. I'd want the batch-fill histogram before trusting
either."

Why real throughput falls short even of the corrected formula:

- **Batches don't fill at low load.** If only 8 requests arrive in the window, the GPU runs a batch of 8 for the
  same 100 ms.
- **Padding.** When inputs have different lengths, a batch is usually padded to its longest input. Ninety-nine
  inputs of 100 tokens plus one of 4,000 do only **3.5%** useful work. (A **token** is a chunk of text, roughly
  three-quarters of a word, and it's the unit models count in.)
- **Failed batches are redone,** and the GPU time spent on them is lost.
- **Uneven routing** leaves some GPUs idle while others have a queue.

### Two models on one 8-GPU machine

PracHub's write-up describes a proposal to keep two queues, one per model, and dispatch "in proportion to each
model's QPS". The large model needs all 8 GPUs of a machine for each batch; the small one needs 1
(<Cite id="s-f24bae1f6b" />).

The mistake is counting requests when you should count **GPU-time**: how many GPUs a request ties up, times for
how long. A large-model request ties up 8 GPUs; a small-model request ties up 1. So one large request costs
about 8 times as much.

Take an assumed mix of 9,000 QPS for the small model and 1,000 QPS for the large one, both in batches of 32 that
take 100 ms. The large model sends **10%** of the requests but needs **47%** of the GPU-time. Dispatching by
request count gives it about 10% of the machine, so its queue grows without limit.

There is a second, quieter problem. Every large batch needs all 8 GPUs free *at the same moment*. If small
batches are scattered across the machine, there is almost never a moment when all 8 are free, so the large model
waits even when the machine looks only half busy.

The fix: give the large model whole machines of its own, run small-model copies on separate GPUs, and size each
pool by GPU-time.

Say it like this: "Dispatching by QPS gives the large model 10% when it needs about half the GPU-time, and its
batches need all 8 GPUs free at once. I'd split them into separate pools and size each by GPU-time."

## High-level design

Here is the invented doc under review, as its author drew it. Every arrow is a synchronous call (the caller
waits), the queue has no size limit, and retries happen at two layers.

```
Clients  (interactive callers, 1 s timeout)
   │
   ▼
Load balancer ─────────── up to 3 attempts per call
   │
   ▼
API pods ──────────────── also up to 3 attempts
   │  push request
   ▼
In-memory queue ───────── no size limit, lives inside the dispatcher process
   │  every 100 ms, take up to 32
   ▼
Dispatcher ────────────── one process; sends batches round-robin
   │
   ▼
GPU workers ───────────── two models mixed on the same machines
   │  write results
   ▼
Results table ─────────── API pods poll it for their request's answer
```

**Clients.** Other services that wait for an answer. The doc doesn't say how long they wait; we assumed 1 s.

**Load balancer (LB).** Spreads requests across API pods. In this doc it also retries a failed call, making up to 3 attempts.

**API pods.** Check the request and put it on the queue. They also make up to 3 attempts. Two retry layers
stacked on each other is the first thing a reviewer should circle.

**In-memory queue.** Holds requests until the dispatcher takes them. It has no size limit and lives in one
process's memory, so a crash loses everything in it.

**Dispatcher.** Every 100 ms it takes up to 32 requests, forms a batch and sends it to the next GPU worker in
turn (**round-robin**). It doesn't look at how busy each worker is or which model the worker holds.

**GPU workers.** Run the batch through the model. Two models share the same machines.

**Results table.** Workers write answers into a shared table, and API pods poll it to find their request's
answer. Polling adds delay and load on the table.

The doc's claims, as written: "batches of 32 every 100 ms give 320 QPS per GPU, so 32 GPUs serve 10,000 QPS";
"the in-memory queue absorbs bursts"; "the load balancer and API pods retry on failure"; "round-robin keeps GPUs
evenly loaded"; "monitoring: GPU utilization dashboard".

### Follow one request through it

The best way to find gaps is to walk one request through the boxes, out loud, naming at each hop how long it can
wait and what happens if that hop fails. Take request #4,812 from a tenant called Acme. It's a normal day at
peak, 10,000 QPS.

| Clock | Hop | What happens | What to say in the interview |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 ms | **Load balancer** | Picks an API pod. | "What's the LB's timeout, and does it know the client gives up at 1 s?" |
| ~2 ms | **API pod** | Validates the request and pushes it on the queue. | "Is anything checking Acme's share before we accept this?" |
| 2–100 ms | **Queue** | Waits for the next 100 ms tick. | "At peak this wait is up to 100 ms. Under a burst, it has no limit." |
| ~100 ms | **Dispatcher** | Takes 32 requests, sends them to the next GPU in turn. | "Round-robin doesn't know whether that GPU is busy or which model it holds." |
| 100–200 ms | **GPU worker** | Runs the batch for 100 ms. | "If this GPU dies now, what happens to these 32 requests?" |
| ~200 ms + poll | **Results table** | Worker writes the answer; the API pod finds it on its next poll. | "Polling adds up to one poll interval. Why not send the answer straight to the pod that holds the connection?" |

On a normal day that's about 200–250 ms, inside our assumed 300 ms target. The design works on its happy path.
The questions in the right-hand column are where it doesn't.

Walking the request this way finds most of the gaps by itself: nobody owns a deadline, the dispatcher is a single
process, the queue lives in that process's memory, and results come back through a polled table.

## Deep dives

### A review method you can run in 30 minutes

Use the same six steps every time. They keep you from drowning in details or redesigning from scratch.

1. **Restate** the goal and the numbers in one sentence, and confirm them with the author.
2. **Walk** one request end to end, then walk one request that fails at each hop.
3. **Check the math**: capacity at the batch size actually achieved and at a target utilization; latency as wait
   time plus batch time.
4. **Look through five lenses**: overload, failure, mixed models and tenants, observability (what you can see
   from dashboards), and cost.
5. **Rank** findings by impact times likelihood. Present the top three as decisions; keep the rest as a list.
6. **Propose narrow changes**, each with how you'd know it worked.

### Overload: the finding that matters most

Now replay request #4,812 on a bad day. A burst arrives and traffic doubles to 20,000 QPS. The fleet as drawn can
serve about 10,000 QPS at full batches, so roughly 10,000 extra requests per second pile up in the queue.

| Time into the burst | Queue length | How long a new request waits | What callers see |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 s | about 0 | about 100 ms | Normal |
| 0.5 s | about 5,000 | about 0.5 s | Slow, but answers arrive |
| 1 s | about 10,000 | about 1 s | New requests hit the 1 s timeout |
| 2 s | about 20,000 | about 2 s | **Every** caller times out; GPUs compute answers nobody waits for |

(Wait is queue length divided by how fast the queue drains, about 10,000 per second. These are illustrative
numbers for the invented doc.)

Request #4,812 arrives at 1.5 s. It sits in the queue for about 1.5 s. Its caller gave up at 1 s. The GPU still
computes its answer half a second later, and that GPU time is wasted. Then the retries start, and it gets worse.
Three flaws compound here.

**Unbounded queue.** "Absorbs bursts" really means "turns bursts into latency". Once queued work can't finish
within the callers' timeouts, every request in the queue is wasted work. The fix is to bound the queue by
*time*, not by count. Estimate the wait as queue length ÷ drain rate, and when it passes the budget, reject at
once with `503` (service unavailable) and a `Retry-After` header that tells the caller when to try again. A
200 ms budget at this drain rate is a queue of about 2,000 requests.

**Retries at two layers.** The load balancer and the API pods each make up to 3 attempts. So one failing request
can become 3 × 3 = **9** attempts on the GPUs, at exactly the moment the GPUs are overloaded. Google's SRE book
describes this multiplication and recommends retrying only at the layer immediately above the failure, with a
**retry budget**: a cap on retries as a share of all traffic
([Handling Overload](https://sre.google/sre-book/handling-overload/)). The fix: retry at the API tier only,
capped at a share of traffic (the load-balancer lab uses 20%), with **jittered backoff** (each retry waits a
random, growing delay so clients don't retry in lockstep). With a 20% budget, total load stays at most 1.2× the
base load instead of multiplying.

**No admission control.** **Admission control** is deciding at the door whether to accept a request at all. The
doc has none, so one tenant can fill the queue for everyone. PracHub's follow-up asks about "A single tenant sends
60 percent of the traffic" (<Cite id="s-f24bae1f6b" />). The fix: a **token bucket** per tenant at the edge (a
counter that refills at the tenant's agreed rate; each request spends one, and an empty bucket means reject), a
priority class for interactive work, and shedding the lowest class first.

A fourth fix ties the first three together: **carry a deadline with every request.** If the request's deadline
has passed by the time it reaches the front of the queue, drop it before it reaches a GPU. Then the GPU never
computes an answer nobody is waiting for.

Say it like this: "The queue doesn't absorb bursts; it converts them into timeouts. I'd bound it at about 200 ms
of estimated wait, reject with Retry-After, drop expired work before it hits a GPU, and retry at one layer with
a budget."

### Failure: a GPU dies mid-batch, and other single points

Go back to request #4,812 on a normal day. It's in a batch of 32 on a GPU, and at 150 ms that GPU dies.

**GPU death.** The doc doesn't say what happens. With a dispatcher that pushes batches and forgets them, the
batch is simply lost and 32 callers time out. The fix is a **lease**: the worker takes the batch for a limited
time. If the worker doesn't report success before the lease expires, the items go back to the head of the queue
with an attempt count. Retry once if the deadline still allows it; otherwise fail fast so the caller can decide.
This is the same idea as a message queue's **visibility timeout**.

**Poison input.** Sometimes one input crashes the worker itself (a malformed input, an edge case in the model
code). If the batch is retried as-is, the crash walks across the fleet one worker at a time. The fix: if a batch
fails twice, split it in half and retry each half (**bisect**), until the bad input is alone. Then move it to a
**dead-letter queue** (a side queue for messages that keep failing) and return an error that tells the caller
not to retry.

**The dispatcher and its in-memory queue** are one process. If it dies, every queued request is gone. Fix: use a
replicated queue, or accept the loss explicitly and let callers retry, and say which in the doc.

**Results table polling** adds latency and load. Fix: send the result straight to the API pod that holds the
caller's connection, using a `reply_to` address carried with the request.

Say it like this: "A dead GPU loses 32 requests silently today. I'd lease each batch, requeue on lease expiry
with an attempt count, and bisect a batch that fails twice so one poison input can't take down the fleet."

### Batching policy and mixed models

"32 every 100 ms" is a timer-only flush. At low load it waits the full 100 ms even when the GPU is idle. At high
load it caps the batch at 32 even when the GPU could take more. A better rule: send a batch when a GPU is free
*and* either the batch is full or the oldest request has waited long enough.

Round-robin ignores both queue depth and which model a worker holds. **Pull-based workers** fix both: each worker
asks its own model's queue for the next batch when it's ready, so a busy worker simply doesn't ask.

For models that write text, ask whether the doc means static batches (where one long output holds up the whole
batch) or continuous batching. The [continuous-batching walkthrough](/system-design-labs/interviews/walkthroughs/continuous-batching/)
covers the second.

### Saying it like a reviewer

Lead with the outcome, not the list: "Under a burst this design turns overload into an outage. Three changes fix
most of it: bound the queue by time, retry at one layer with a budget, and lease batches so a dead GPU doesn't
lose work." Then take questions.

Write each comment as behaviour, reproducer, change: "At 2× peak the queue grows at 10,000 requests/s and every
caller times out after 1 s (reproduce by replaying peak traffic at 2×); bound the queue at 200 ms of estimated
wait and return 503."

Ask before asserting where the doc might be right for reasons it doesn't state. The EM report's emphasis on
communication (<Cite id="s-4e3dc29a95" />) and a separately reported Anthropic candidate's regret that "some of
the assumptions didn't feel realistic, and I pushed on that too directly" (<Cite id="s-4e04a1689c" />) point the
same way: disagree with the design, not the interviewer.

## Failure modes and overload

Here are the ranked findings for the invented doc. "Verify with" is the test that would prove each fix works.

| # | Finding | Impact | Fix | Verify with |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Unbounded queue, no deadlines | Burst → everyone times out; GPUs compute abandoned work | Time-bounded queue, deadline per request, drop expired work | Replay at 2× peak: accepted p99 stays bounded, rejections rise |
| 2 | Retries at LB and API, 3 attempts each | Up to 9× attempts under failure | Retry once, at one layer, within a budget | Inject 50% GPU errors; attempts per request ≤ budget |
| 3 | No lease on dispatched batches | GPU death loses 32 requests | Lease and requeue with attempt count; bisect repeats | Kill a worker mid-batch in staging |
| 4 | Dispatch by QPS across models | Large model starves (47% of GPU-time needed, 10% given) | Separate pools sized by GPU-time | Queue wait per model under mixed load |
| 5 | Sizing at max batch, 100% busy | Fleet undersized | Size at achieved batch and ρ ≈ 0.7 | Batch-fill histogram |
| 6 | Single dispatcher process | Total outage on crash | Replicated queue or explicit loss contract | Kill the dispatcher |
| 7 | Monitoring = GPU utilization | Can't tell busy from drowning | Queue wait, batch fill, rejections, retries | Dashboards in the doc |

Why this order? Findings 1–3 cause an incident on an ordinary bad day: a burst or one dead machine. Finding 4
starves one product every day the mix holds. Findings 5–7 make the others harder to see or fix but don't cause
an outage on their own.

## Evals and observability

The doc's only monitoring is a GPU utilization dashboard. That can't tell "busy and healthy" from "busy computing
answers nobody is waiting for": in the burst above, utilization is at 100% the whole time. Here is what the doc
should promise instead, and what each tells you:

- **Queue wait** (p50 and p99, per model). This is the leading sign of overload: it rises before anything times
  out.
- **Batch fill** (how full batches actually are) and **flush reason** (full, or timer). These test the sizing
  assumption directly.
- **Rejections by reason** (tenant limit, queue budget, deadline) and the **retry share** of traffic.
- **Requeues and dead letters** after GPU failures.
- **Each tenant's share** of GPU-time.
- **Output checks**: after any change to batching or the model, run a fixed set of inputs with randomness turned
  off (**temperature 0**, the setting that makes a model give its most likely output every time) and compare the
  answers. In ML this fixed, re-runnable test set is called an **eval set**.

PracHub's write-up asks which three dashboard metrics come first and their alert thresholds
(<Cite id="s-f24bae1f6b" />). A defensible three:

1. **Queue-wait p99** against the latency budget. Alert at, say, 50% of the budget.
2. **Rejection rate.** Alert on a sudden step up.
3. **Batch fill.** Alert when it falls below the level the sizing assumed.

Those thresholds are illustrative. Say so: "I'd set them from a baseline week of real traffic."

## Trade-offs

Every fix has a cost. Name it before the author does. The right-hand column says when the author's original
choice is actually fine, which shows you aren't critiquing for its own sake.

| Proposed change | What it costs | When the original choice is fine |
|---|---|---|
| Time-bounded queue with rejection | Some requests are rejected that would have succeeded | Offline batch traffic with no deadline |
| One retry layer with a budget | Fewer silent recoveries from brief errors | Never two layers; one layer is enough |
| Leases on batches | Duplicate work after a slow (not dead) GPU | Callers can tolerate loss and retry themselves |
| Separate pools per model | Lower packing efficiency | Models are the same size |
| Pull-based workers | The queue is on the hot path | A small, fixed fleet where round-robin is good enough |

The lease trade-off is worth saying out loud. A GPU that is slow but alive can let its lease expire, so the batch
runs twice. That's fine here because we assumed requests are safe to retry. If they weren't, you'd need to drop
the duplicate result.

<AINative title="Have an agent attack the doc; you decide what matters">
Review is where agents help most and mislead most. Ask one to list failure modes hop by hop and to recompute
every number in the doc; it is fast and thorough, and it will produce twenty findings of equal confidence. Your
job is the ranking and the evidence: which three findings would cause an incident, and what replay or
fault-injection test proves each fix. Check every number it recomputes yourself, because a confident wrong
capacity figure is worse than none. In the interview, assume no AI help unless told otherwise.
</AINative>

## Small-team version

At a 50–500 person company you probably never build a batch-inference service. But you will review AI designs
often: a teammate's plan to run a model on your own GPUs, a vendor's architecture for a feature you're buying, an
agent pipeline someone drafted with an assistant. Same skill, different documents.

### What you review, build, buy or skip

- **Review** every design that puts a model call on a user's path: timeouts, retries, fallbacks and spend limits.
- **Buy** the serving layer where you can. Then the review is of the vendor's limits. Anthropic's API docs, for
  example, set monthly spend caps per tier and return a 429 (too many requests) without a `retry-after` header at
  the cap, so an SDK's automatic retries keep failing until the month resets
  ([rate-limits docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/api/rate-limits), fetched 2026-10-11). That's a
  design-review finding for any feature built on it.
- **Skip** formal review boards. A one-page template and a 30-minute walk-through with one other engineer is
  enough.

### The cost check every AI design review needs

Many proposals to run your own GPUs (**self-hosting**) skip the comparison with just paying a provider per token.
The usual comparison is a **serverless endpoint**: a provider runs the same open model, and you pay only for the
tokens you use.

Cohorte's break-even analysis compares the two for the same open model, gpt-oss-120B. On Together's serverless
endpoint it costs $0.60 per million output tokens. A rented machine with 2 H100 GPUs (a high-end NVIDIA
data-center GPU), used only during business hours, would need 213% of its maximum throughput to break even, so
it can't ([Cohorte](https://cohorte.co/blog/self-hosted-llm-cost-vs-api-break-even), 2026-09-20).

For a concrete number: at an assumed 50,000 requests a day, self-hosting that model costs **about $8,550 a
month** (two GPUs at Lambda's $4.19 per GPU-hour for a 2-GPU instance, plus 15% of an engineer's
time), against **about $1,035 a month** on the serverless endpoint. List prices were fetched 2026-10-11
([Lambda](https://lambda.ai/pricing), [Together](https://www.together.ai/pricing)).

Say it like this: "Before I read the architecture, what's the measured traffic, and what does the same model cost
on a serverless endpoint? At 50,000 requests a day, self-hosting is about eight times the price."

### A review checklist to keep

1. Every external call has a timeout shorter than its caller's.
2. Retries happen at one layer, with a budget, and only for errors that are safe to retry.
3. Every queue has a bound and a rejection path the client understands.
4. A dead worker or provider loses no accepted work, or the doc says it may and why that's acceptable.
5. There is a fallback (a second provider or a degraded mode) for a provider outage.
6. Spend caps and per-user limits exist for anything priced per token.
7. The doc names the three metrics and alerts it will ship with.
8. The cost section names the comparison and the measured volume.

### Delivery plan

A written review takes one to two days. Spend half a day reading and walking flows. Spend half a day on the math,
plus a quick replay or fault-injection test if a staging environment exists. Then write a short note with the top
three findings and the checklist. That's a plan, not a sourced norm.

### What small-company interviewers probe

Employer-side reports describe probing failure paths. Elicit's hiring manager probes error handling, retries,
fallbacks and timeouts rather than the happy path ([Latent Space](https://www.latent.space/p/hiring),
2024-06-21). PostHog's handbook describes a pairing session debugging existing code for product engineers
([hiring process](https://posthog.com/handbook/people/hiring-process), fetched 2026-10-11). Expect to be handed
something that exists and asked what's wrong with it.

### Revisit when

Revisit this when the company starts running its own GPUs (then the full critique applies), when a second team
depends on the service (add tenants and quotas to the checklist), or when an incident shows a gap the checklist
missed (add it).

In plain terms: a Senior reviewer finds the right problems and fixes them. A Staff reviewer also decides which
three matter most, proves each fix with a test, and changes the structure (separate pools per model) where a
local patch wouldn't be enough.

## Follow-ups

<FollowUps>
  <FollowUp q="(Reported, Anthropic) After the first few issues: how does this design handle overload?">
    It doesn't: the queue grows without bound and retries multiply load. Bound the queue by time, admit per
    tenant, shed by priority, retry once at one layer within a budget, and return `Retry-After`
    (<Cite id="s-eab681003c" />).
  </FollowUp>
  <FollowUp q="(Reported, Anthropic) What are the weak points, and how would you improve it?">
    Rank them: overload first, then failure handling (leases, bisection), then placement for mixed models, then
    observability. Give each a test that proves the fix (<Cite id="s-4e3dc29a95" />).
  </FollowUp>
  <FollowUp q="(Listed on the PracHub page, Anthropic) Using the doc's formula, how many GPUs? Why does reality fall short?">
    1,000 ÷ B GPUs at 100% busy for 10,000 QPS and 100 ms batches; divide by a utilization target. Reality falls
    short because batches don't fill, padding wastes slots, failures redo work and routing is uneven (<Cite id="s-f24bae1f6b" />).
  </FollowUp>
  <FollowUp q="(Listed on the PracHub page, Anthropic) One tenant sends 60% of traffic. Protect the others.">
    Per-tenant token buckets sized by contract, a per-tenant share of queue capacity, and fair dequeuing across
    tenants (weighted round-robin over tenant queues) so a burst only delays its sender (<Cite id="s-f24bae1f6b" />).
  </FollowUp>
  <FollowUp q="(Listed on the PracHub page, Anthropic) How do you canary a new model version on this fleet?">
    A separate pool for the new version, a small share of traffic routed by user hash, comparison of error rate,
    latency and a fixed eval set, and automatic rollback on regression (<Cite id="s-f24bae1f6b" />).
  </FollowUp>
  <FollowUp q="(Not reported; a natural next push) The author disagrees with your top finding. What now?">
    Ask what traffic or constraint makes the queue safe in their view, and propose a replay at 2× peak as the
    tiebreaker. A design review settles on evidence, not seniority.
  </FollowUp>
</FollowUps>

## Common mistakes

- **Redesigning from scratch.** The round asks for a review; keep the author's structure and change what breaks.
- **Equal-weight lists.** Twenty findings with no order hide your judgment; rank them and lead with three.
- **Skipping the math.** The doc's sizing error is often the easiest finding to prove (<Cite id="s-f24bae1f6b" />).
- **Missing compound retries.** Retries at two layers multiply, so count attempts across all layers.
- **Critiquing the interviewer.** A reported candidate regrets that they "pushed on that too directly"
  (<Cite id="s-4e04a1689c" />); disagree with the design, not the person.
- **Vague comments.** "This won't scale" gives the author nothing to act on; name the behaviour, how to reproduce
  it, and the narrow change (<Cite id="s-b6632d5df9" />).

## Prove it with a lab

<LabRef lab="load-balancer" role="proof" note="Circuit breaker after 5 consecutive 5xx and a 20% retry budget, with a review-worthy bug in its v2 retry path." />

Treat its v2 retry as a review exercise. The retry runs after `forward` already wrote the first 5xx and its body,
so the client never sees the retried answer. A retried POST also goes out with an empty body, because `r.Body`
(a stream that can be read only once) is reused. Finding that by reading is the skill this round tests.

<LabRef lab="rate-limiter" role="proof" note="Per-tenant token buckets and a shared counter: the admission control the invented doc lacks." />

It fails open on a Redis pipeline error: if the shared counter can't be reached, it lets the request through.
Decide whether an expensive GPU tier should fail open or closed, and write that down as a review comment.

<LabRef lab="message-queue" role="partial" note="Visibility timeouts, receive counts and a dead-letter queue: leases and poison handling." />

`SendMessage` appends without a cap: the unbounded-queue finding in code. Its delayed-message test panics as of
2026-10-11.

return, and every request still gets its own forward pass (one run through the model), so the "batch" doesn't

## The prompt

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "I'll split the system into two halves. An indexing half copies each company's documents, cuts them into
> passages, and stores them in a keyword index and a vector index, together with the list of people and groups
> allowed to read each document. A serving half takes a question, finds the best passages the user is allowed to
> read, checks those passages once more against the live permission system, and has the model write a cited
> answer from them. The permission filter runs inside the search, never after it. And I'll give a number for how
> long a revoked document can still show up, built from how fast we hear about the change, how fast we apply it,
> and how long any cache or login token keeps old permissions."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer.

### What makes this different from normal search

In a normal backend, permissions are easy to get right. A user asks for a record, the service runs a query such
as `SELECT … WHERE owner_id = :user`, and the database applies the rule on every read. Nothing is copied. If you
revoke someone's access, the next query already sees the change.

An AI assistant over company documents breaks that habit in three ways.

1. **The content is copied, several times.** To search a document by meaning, the system copies its text out of
   Drive or Slack into its own indexes. Those copies do not update themselves when the original changes or
   is deleted. Every copy is a place where an old permission can live on.
2. **The model reads before the user does.** The system puts the best passages into the model's input, and the
   model writes an answer from them. If a forbidden passage reaches the model, its facts can appear in the answer
   even when you hide the citation. Once the model has read it, it is too late.
3. **Permissions come from many systems.** Drive has folder sharing and "anyone with the link". Slack has
   channels. Email is per message. Groups contain groups. You don't own any of these rules; you copy them, and
   copies lag.

So the interview is less about search quality and more about a distributed-systems question you already know:
how do you keep a copy of someone else's data, and its access rules, correct enough, and how stale can it get?

### Words you need first

- **RAG** (retrieval-augmented generation): search your documents first, then give the best passages to a large
  language model (LLM) and ask it to answer *only* from them, citing which passage each claim came from.
- **Chunk**: a passage of a few hundred words cut from a document. The system searches and cites chunks, not
  whole documents.
- **Embedding**: a list of numbers (a vector, here 768 of them) that a small model computes from a piece of text.
  Texts with similar meaning get vectors that point in similar directions. **Vector search** (also called dense
  retrieval) finds the chunks whose vectors are closest to the question's vector.
- **Keyword search**: the classic inverted index you know from Elasticsearch or Lucene. Its standard scoring
  formula is called **BM25**. It is good at exact names, ticket ids and codenames, which vector search often
  misses. Using both is called **hybrid retrieval**.
- **ACL** (access control list): the list of who may read a document. Each entry is a **principal**: a user or a
  group. A user's **principal set** is their own id plus every group they belong to, with nested groups
  flattened.
- **Tenant**: one customer company. Each tenant's data must never reach another tenant. A **connector** is the
  code that pulls documents and their ACLs from one source, such as Drive or Slack.

## What is being tested

**Where access control runs.** The Glean guide says interviewers push on "Where do you enforce access control?"
It names the tension: "Filtering after ranking is simple and wrong at scale", while filtering during retrieval is
faster but needs permission data in or beside the index that "has to update the moment someone's permissions
change". Its summary: "Being able to name that tension, and pick a side with a reason, is most of the round"
(<Cite id="s-768711771a" />). This page picks a side: filter during retrieval, then check again before the model
sees anything.

**Indexing separated from serving, and freshness as a failure case.** The same guide wants a Drive edit to
appear "within minutes, not on the next daily crawl". It asks you to reason about "change events versus full
recrawls", and about what happens "when a connector goes down for six hours and comes back with a backlog to
replay".

**A number for deletion.** PracHub's write-up asks how quickly and how reliably a deleted document leaves answers
(<Cite id="s-f8053ff3a7" />). "Quickly" is not an answer. A bound with named parts is.

**Breadth before depth.** The rejected Cohere candidate's own hindsight: "it's better not to go too deep at the
high level and instead lay out the general components first". They also wished they had let the model plan with
function calling rather than splitting the system into many fine-grained parts. The post doesn't say what the
interviewers cited (<Cite id="s-d5d3016775" />).

My reading, not a sourced rubric: the round checks whether you hold three rules with numbers attached, while you
add the standard parts (hybrid search, a reranker, caches) around them.

1. No content ever crosses from one tenant to another.
2. No answer cites, or uses, a document the user can't open right now.
3. Staleness has a stated upper bound, and that includes permission staleness.

Say it like this: "I'll treat those three as invariants. Every component I add, I'll check against them."

## Clarify and scope

Spend two or three minutes here. Each question changes the design.

1. **Tenancy.** How many tenants, and how uneven are they in size? Does any contract require separate storage or
   separate encryption keys per tenant?
2. **Connectors.** Which sources? Do they push change events (a webhook saying "file X changed"), or can you only
   list files and compare? Do they expose permissions, including folder inheritance, nested groups and "anyone
   with the link"?
3. **Granularity.** Are permissions per document, or also per section, channel or message? Slack and email are
   per message in practice.
4. **Freshness targets.** How fast must an edit appear? And, separately, how fast must a deletion or a revoked
   share take effect? The second one usually has a security or compliance owner. Ask who.
5. **Load and latency.** Questions per second at peak, and the time budget until the first word of the answer
   appears.
6. **Answer contract.** Citations on every claim? What should happen when the documents don't contain an answer?
7. **Federated or centralized** (the Google variant). May you copy the user's mail and chat into your own index,
   or must you query each product's own search at request time?

Assumptions used below (all assumed): the largest tenant has 5M documents averaging 2,000 tokens (a **token** is
the unit LLMs read and bill by, roughly ¾ of a word) and 20,000 employees asking 10 questions a working day.
Documents carry ACLs of users and groups. Deletions and revocations must take effect within 5 minutes. Out of
scope: fine-tuning the model that writes answers, and the code inside each connector.

### The example that runs through this page

Take one tenant, **Northwind** (an invented company), and one employee, **Dana**, a sales engineer. In Drive,
Northwind has a document called *Kestrel pricing exception*, which records a special discount given to a
customer. It sits in a folder shared with the group `sales-leads`. Dana is in that group on Monday morning.

Dana asks the assistant: *"What discount did we give Kestrel last year?"* At 10:00 an admin moves Dana out of
`sales-leads`. The page follows two things: how Dana's question is answered safely, and how long after 10:00
the assistant might still quote the Kestrel document to her.

## Back-of-envelope

Three numbers matter for this design. The first two size the system. The third one is what the deletion
follow-up is really asking for.

**How big is the index?** The largest tenant's 5M documents become about **30M chunks**. Stored as plain 32-bit
floats, their vectors plus the graph that makes vector search fast (explained in the deep dives) take about
**96 GB**; stored as 8-bit integers, about **27 GB**. The
ACLs are tiny next to that: about **200 MB**. So the largest tenant needs its own set of index shards, while a
median tenant fits in one process. That skew argues for one shared service, partitioned by tenant, with dedicated
shards for the few large tenants.

**How busy is it?** 20,000 people asking 10 questions in a working day is about **7 questions a second**, or about
**21** at an assumed 3× peak. Each question also asks a **reranker** to score candidates. A reranker is a slower,
more accurate model that reads the question and one chunk together and scores how well they match (the interview
name is **cross-encoder**). Scoring 50 candidates per question means about **1,050 question-chunk pairs a
second** at peak. The rag lab's small cross-encoder scored 20 candidates in
<LabNumber lab="rag" metric="Cross-encoder rerank, 20 candidates" />, so on CPU the peak needs about **22
processes**, or GPUs, or fewer candidates. Each answer sends about 4,000 tokens to the answering model: **800M
input tokens a day** for this tenant. The answering model is the cost line; the reranker is the latency line.

### The number the deletion follow-up wants

Go back to Dana. She lost access at 10:00. How long can the assistant still quote *Kestrel pricing exception* to
her? Four delays add up, and each one has a plain meaning:

- **Detect** (t_detect): how long until our connector learns about the change. With change events this is
  seconds to a minute. With polling, it is up to the polling interval.
- **Apply** (t_apply): how long until the new ACL is written into the indexes the search reads.
- **Claims** (t_claims): if the user's groups travel inside their login credential, how long that credential
  lives. A login **access token** (for example a JWT, a signed credential the browser sends with each request; not
  the same thing as an LLM token) can carry the user's groups as **group claims**. The server trusts those claims
  until the token expires, whatever the directory says now.
- **Cache** (t_cache): how long any cached answer or search result can outlive the change, if its key doesn't
  include the permission version.

Detection plus applying runs in parallel with the token's lifetime, so the slower of the two counts, and the
cache adds on top:

In Dana's terms: with the first design, the Kestrel document can still be quoted to her until about **11:10**.
With the second, until about **10:03**. The surprise is where the 70 minutes come from. Most of it is the login
token, not the index.

Say it like this: "Deleted documents disappear within a bound I can name: detection plus apply, or the token
lifetime if groups ride in a token, plus any cache that isn't keyed by permission version. In this design that's
about 3 minutes, and I'd measure it with a probe instead of trusting the formula."

## High-level design

The system has two halves that share a set of stores. Indexing runs in the background and is measured in
throughput. Serving runs while the user waits and is measured in latency. Splitting them means a backlog makes
results older but never makes queries slower.

```
INDEXING (background)
  Connectors (Drive, Slack, wiki, mail)
     │  change events: create / update / delete / ACL change, with a source version
     ▼
  Change log ── durable, one cursor per connector
     │                                    │
     │ content lane                       │ ACL + delete lane (fast, never waits)
     ▼                                    │
  Parse + chunk ──► Embed                 │
     │                 │                  │
     ▼                 ▼                  ▼
  ┌─────────────── Per-tenant stores ───────────────┐
  │ keyword index · vector index · ACL store · docs │
  └──────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
     ▲                 ▲                  ▲
SERVING (user waits)   │                  │
  Question ──► Auth + groups ──► Filtered retrieval ──► Fuse + rerank
                                                            │
                                    ACL re-check ◄──────────┘
                                         │
                                         ▼
                               LLM writes cited answer ──► stream to user

  Answer cache key = tenant + principal set + ACL version
```

**Connectors.** One per source. Each turns what the source tells it into change events: created, updated,
deleted, or permissions changed, each with the source's version number for that document.

**Change log.** A durable log (think Kafka) with one cursor per connector, so a connector that crashes resumes
where it stopped. Writes downstream are **idempotent upserts** keyed by (tenant, document id, source version):
applying the same event twice does nothing new, so replaying the log is safe.

**Two lanes.** Content changes go through parsing, chunking and embedding, which is slow and costs money. ACL
changes and deletions take their own lane. They update the ACL store and mark or re-tag the existing chunks
without re-reading or re-embedding anything. A burst of edits never delays a revocation.

**Parse, chunk and embed.** Workers extract text, cut it into chunks that keep their heading path ("Pricing >
Exceptions > Kestrel"), and compute an embedding for each chunk.

**Per-tenant stores.** A keyword index and a vector index per tenant partition. A document store with versions
and **tombstones** (a record saying "this document is deleted", which every query checks, because some indexes
can't remove an item instantly). An ACL store mapping documents to principals and users to groups. Keep groups as
groups on documents: expanding a 10,000-person group onto every document turns one membership change into
millions of writes.

**Auth and groups.** Authenticate the user and tenant, then ask a permissions service for the user's principal
set: their user id plus all their groups, flattened. Use the login token for *who* the user is, not for *which
groups* they're in.

**Filtered retrieval.** Run keyword search and vector search inside the tenant's partition, with the principal
filter applied as part of the search, so forbidden chunks are never scored at all.

**Fuse and rerank.** The two searches return scores on different scales, so merge them by rank instead. The usual
method is **reciprocal rank fusion (RRF)**: each chunk gets 1 ÷ (60 + its rank) from each list, summed. Then the
reranker reorders the top candidates.

**ACL re-check** (also called the **post-check**). Before the model sees anything, check each surviving
document once more against the authoritative ACL store, and drop anything that fails. If the permissions service doesn't answer, drop
everything. This is called **failing closed**: when in doubt, deny.

**LLM writes a cited answer.** The prompt contains the chunks with their ids. The model must cite ids. Code then
checks every cited id was actually retrieved and permitted for this user, and the answer streams to the browser.

### Dana's question, step by step

| Step | What happens to Dana's question | What to say in the interview |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Auth + groups | Dana's principal set is \{dana, sales, sales-leads, all-staff\}. It's looked up now, not read from her token. | "Identity comes from the token; groups come from a lookup with a one-minute cache." |
| 2. Filtered retrieval | Keyword search matches "Kestrel" exactly; vector search matches "discount". Only chunks whose ACL shares a principal with Dana's set are scored. | "Forbidden chunks are never scored, so nothing downstream can leak them." |
| 3. Fuse + rerank | RRF merges the two lists; the reranker puts the Kestrel exception chunk first. | "Fuse by rank because the two scores aren't comparable." |
| 4. ACL re-check | The live ACL store confirms Dana can read the Kestrel document. | "This catches an index copy that lags behind the source." |
| 5. Answer | The model writes "a 12% exception, approved in March [chunk 4417]" from the passage. Code checks chunk 4417 was retrieved and permitted. | "Citations are checked in code, not trusted from the model." |

(The 12% and the chunk id are invented for the example.) If Dana asks again at 10:05 under the 3-minute design,
step 1 no longer contains `sales-leads`, step 2 never scores the Kestrel chunk, and she gets an answer without it.

### The serving core in code

Here is the serving-side core in Python: the filter inside retrieval, the final re-check that fails closed, and a
cache key that includes permissions. It was run on 2026-10-12 (Python 3.13, numpy) with a test where an HR
document is the best match for the query but the user can't read it, a share revoked at the source while the
index copy lags, and a permissions-service timeout. The HR chunk never came back, the re-check dropped the three
revoked chunks, and the timeout returned nothing.

```python
import hashlib
import numpy as np

def retrieve(q, vecs, chunk_doc, index_acl, principals, acl_store, k=8, stats=None):
    """Permission-aware dense retrieval inside ONE tenant's partition.
    index_acl: doc -> set of principals, copied beside the index (may lag).
    acl_store: the authoritative permissions service (current version)."""
    allowed = np.array([i for i, d in enumerate(chunk_doc) if index_acl[d] & principals], dtype=int)
    if allowed.size == 0:
        return []
    # Pre-filter: only chunks the user may read are ever scored. With a small allowed
    # set an exact scan is cheap and has perfect recall; past that, use filtered ANN.
    scores = vecs[allowed] @ q
    top = allowed[np.argsort(-scores)[:k]]
    # Post-check against the authoritative store, failing closed.
    kept = []
    for i in top:
        try:
            ok = acl_store(principals, chunk_doc[i])
        except Exception:
            ok = False                      # permissions service down: drop, never pass through
        if ok:
            kept.append(int(i))
        elif stats is not None:
            stats["post_check_drops"] = stats.get("post_check_drops", 0) + 1
    return kept

def cache_key(tenant, principals, acl_version, question):
    """Answer-cache key: two users with different access never share an entry."""
    fp = hashlib.sha256("|".join(sorted(principals)).encode()).hexdigest()[:16]
    qh = hashlib.sha256(" ".join(question.lower().split()).encode()).hexdigest()[:16]
    return f"{tenant}:{fp}:{acl_version}:{qh}"
```

Read it in three parts. `allowed` is the set of chunks Dana may read according to the index's copy of the ACLs;
only those are scored (`vecs[allowed] @ q` is the similarity of each allowed chunk to the question). The loop
re-checks the top results against the live store and treats any error as "deny". And `cache_key` hashes the
sorted principal set together with an ACL version, so two users with different access can never share a cached
answer. The list comprehension that builds `allowed` is a toy. A real index keeps a list or bitmap of chunks per
principal and intersects them, the same way keyword search intersects the lists of documents for each word.

## Deep dives

### Where the permission check runs

There are three places to put it, and the reported follow-ups are built to tell them apart.

**After generation** (let the model answer, then remove citations the user can't open). This leaks by
construction. The model already read the forbidden text, so its facts can be in the answer with no citation
attached. If Dana had lost access and the Kestrel chunk still reached the model, "12%" could appear in her answer
with the footnote stripped.

**After ranking, before generation.** Retrieve the top 20, drop what the user can't see, send the rest. It works
in a demo and fails three ways:

- *Users with narrow access get almost nothing.* If 18 of the top 20 are forbidden, the user gets two chunks, the
  "near-empty page" the Glean guide warns about. Raising the number retrieved to compensate costs latency and
  money on every query.
- *Shared stages see forbidden content.* Anything between search and the filter that is cached or shared (a cache
  keyed by the question, "related documents", result counts, query suggestions) can carry it to another user.
- *It fails open under pressure.* If someone adds "return results anyway" when the filter times out, you leak
  exactly when the permissions service is overloaded.

**During retrieval** (pre-filtering). Store each document's principals beside the index, compute the user's
principal set once per query, and only score chunks where the two sets overlap. On the keyword side, that is a
bitmap intersected with the word lists. On the vector side it is harder, and needs one more idea.

#### Why filtering vector search is hard

Comparing the question to every vector is exact but slow at 30M chunks. So vector databases use
**approximate nearest neighbour (ANN)** search: they give up a little accuracy for a lot of speed. The common
structure is **HNSW** (hierarchical navigable small world), a graph where each vector links to its nearest
neighbours. A search walks the graph from a starting point toward the question, visiting a few hundred nodes
instead of 30 million. How many good candidates it keeps along the way is a setting (`ef_search` in pgvector).

A filter fights with that walk. If the search collects 40 candidates and only then applies the filter, most may be
filtered out. The vector-index lab notes that pre- and post-filtering "have very different recall
characteristics" (<LabRef lab="vector-index" inline />). **Recall** here means the share of the truly best
allowed chunks that the search actually returns. pgvector's README gives the concrete case: "If a condition
matches 10% of rows, with HNSW and the default hnsw.ef_search of 40, only 4 rows will match on average", which is
why it added iterative index scans in 0.8.0 ([pgvector README](https://github.com/pgvector/pgvector), fetched
2026-10-12).

So choose the method by how much of the tenant the user can see:

- **The user sees a small slice** (say 20,000 chunks): skip the graph and compare against every allowed chunk
  exactly. That is about **15M multiply-adds per query** (20,000 × 768), which is cheap, and recall is perfect.
- **The user sees most of the tenant:** use graph search with the filter applied *during* the walk, and widen the
  search so enough allowed results survive.

Then keep a **post-check as defense in depth**: re-verify the 20 to 50 reranked documents against the ACL store's
current version. It costs a few dozen lookups, catches a lagging index, and fails closed. Count what it drops. The
count should be near zero, and a rising count is your earliest sign that the ACL lane is falling behind.

Tenant isolation sits above all of this. Enforce it by partitioning (separate index namespaces or shards), not by
a `WHERE tenant = ?` clause someone can forget. Plant unique **canary** strings in each tenant, so that a
cross-tenant hit is detectable.

Say it like this: "I filter inside retrieval so recall holds for narrow users and nothing downstream sees
forbidden text. Then I re-check the final few dozen against the live ACL store and fail closed, and I alert on how
many it drops."

### How fast a deleted or revoked document disappears

Walk the four terms of the formula from the back-of-envelope, and say what you'd build for each.

- **Detection.** Change events give seconds to a minute. A polling connector sees a deletion only as an absence:
  the file is simply no longer in the listing. So it also needs a periodic **reconciliation** that compares the
  full list of document ids with the index. That interval is part of your bound.
- **Applying it.** Keyword indexes can remove a document directly: the search-engine lab's delete walks every
  posting list (<LabRef lab="search-engine" inline />). Graph vector indexes can't remove a node cheaply. The
  vector-index lab notes HNSW deletion needs tombstones and periodic compaction. So tombstones go into a fast
  lookup that every query filters against, and compaction (rebuilding the graph without deleted nodes) runs later.
- **Group membership.** If groups ride inside a token, they change only when the token does. The oauth-provider
  lab issues JWT access tokens that last an hour with no way to revoke them (<LabRef lab="oauth-provider" inline />),
  so trusting group claims in such a token makes t_claims 60 minutes. That is the 60 in Dana's 70-minute case. Use
  the token for identity and look groups up per request, with a short cache.
- **Caches.** An answer cached a second before the revocation stays valid until its expiry time. Key caches by ACL
  version, or invalidate by document id when the ACL lane writes.
- **Derived copies.** The chunk text also lives in the embedding cache, in logs and traces that captured the
  retrieved context, and in evaluation sets built from production questions. Retention rules have to cover them.
  Treat an embedding as a copy of the text.

Measure the bound instead of asserting it. A scheduled **revocation probe** per tenant creates a document, shares
it with a test user, revokes the share, and records how long queries keep returning it. That turns "reliably" in
PracHub's follow-up into a dashboard.

The Cohere follow-up ("a downstream party ... shouldn't have access to certain data anymore") is this same path,
with one addition for a research assistant: a report already delivered may quote the data. Store which chunks
each report used, so a revocation can find, flag or regenerate the reports that cited it.

### Freshness after a connector outage

The Glean guide's scenario is a connector that is down for six hours and comes back with a backlog
(<Cite id="s-768711771a" />). Here is what each piece of the design does about it.

- **A durable cursor per connector.** Replay from the last committed cursor. Idempotent upserts keyed by source
  version make duplicates harmless. Comparing versions rather than arrival order handles events that arrive out
  of order.
- **Two lanes.** ACL changes and deletes go first and are cheap. Content edits wait for embedding capacity.
- **Keyword first.** New documents become searchable by keyword before their embeddings are ready.
- **Visible lag.** Track "now minus last successful sync" per connector, show it on citations ("synced 3 h
  ago"), and alert on it.
- **A permissions policy during the outage.** While a connector is down you can't learn about its revocations. If
  Dana had lost access during those six hours, nothing would tell you. Decide with the customer whether that
  source keeps serving with a banner, or is hidden past a threshold. That is a product decision; raise it rather
  than choose silently.

### Federated versus centralized (the Google variant)

A unified query engine across one user's email, calendar, documents and chat can be built two ways.

- **Federated:** at question time, call each source's own search API as the user, in parallel, then merge.
  Permissions and freshness are the source's problem, so revocation staleness is near zero. But latency is the
  slowest source, ranking across sources is hard (rank fusion such as RRF helps, because it ignores scores that
  can't be compared), and meaning-based search is only as good as each source's own search.
- **Centralized:** copy everything into your own per-user or per-tenant index. Fast, consistent ranking and real
  hybrid retrieval, but you now own ACL replication, deletion lag, and a copy of everyone's mail.

A common answer is a mix. Centralize the sources where you can get change events and permissions (documents,
wikis). Federate the ones that are personal, change constantly, or are sensitive (mail, calendar). Give the
fan-out a deadline, so one slow source returns partial results rather than blocking the whole answer. Personal
data also changes the partitioning: per-user partitions for mailboxes, per-tenant partitions with ACLs for shared
content.

### Retrieval quality, briefly

The [RAG design walkthrough](/system-design-labs/interviews/walkthroughs/rag-system-design/) covers chunking,
hybrid retrieval, how many chunks to pass, and reranking in depth. Two points matter specifically here.

First, hybrid retrieval only pays when the keyword side is decent. The rag lab measured **hit@3** (the share of
questions where a correct passage is in the top 3) on BEIR SciFact, a public scientific-claims dataset, for
vector-only, hybrid and reranked search: <LabNumber lab="rag" metric="hit@3 on BEIR SciFact" />. Hybrid didn't
beat vector-only until the keyword side split words properly: a `\w+` tokenizer raised hybrid to
<LabNumber lab="rag" metric="BM25 tokenizer (SciFact)" />. Enterprise text is full of names, ticket ids and
project codenames like "Kestrel", which is why keyword search earns its place.

Second, the PracHub follow-up on **tuning the embedding model to a customer's domain**. You need pairs of "this
question should find this passage". Mine them from the corpus: headings paired with their sections, or questions
generated from passages by a model and then filtered. Train with a **contrastive loss**, which pulls each
question's vector toward its passage and pushes it away from the other passages in the same training batch (the
other passages are called **in-batch negatives**). Compare **recall@k** (how often the right passage is in the
top k) against the base model on held-out labelled questions. A new embedding model means re-embedding everything
into a **shadow index** (a second index built alongside the live one) and then switching. Between MiniLM-L6 and
MiniLM-L12 the embedding-pipeline lab measured neighbour overlap@10 of
<LabNumber lab="embedding-pipeline" metric="Neighbour overlap@10" />, so about a third of each chunk's nearest
neighbours change even between closely related models. You can't mix vectors from two models in one index.

### Citations and caching frequent questions

**Citations.** Put chunk ids into the model's input. Then check in code that every cited id was retrieved *and*
permitted for this user, and that any quoted text appears word for word in the cited chunk. For the Cohere report
variant, keep a citation per claim and store the chunk ids behind each section.

**Caching.** The obvious cache keys on the question. A **semantic cache** goes further: it embeds the question and
serves a stored answer when a new question is close enough in meaning. Both serve user A's answer to user B.
Suppose Dana's manager, still in `sales-leads`, asked about Kestrel's discount at 9:58, and Dana asks at 10:04:
a question-keyed cache hands Dana the Kestrel answer after her access is gone. Three safe options, cheapest
first:

1. **Cache question embeddings.** They contain no document content, so they're safe to share, and they save one
   embedding call per repeated question.
2. **Cache answers only for content visible to the whole tenant** (the handbook, public wiki spaces). Most
   "frequent questions" are about exactly that content.
3. **Cache personalized answers keyed by tenant, principal-set fingerprint and ACL version**, as in `cache_key`
   above. Safe, but fragmented: users in different groups never share an entry.

A similarity lookup can sit inside option 2 or 3. It must never sit in front of the permission filter.

## Failure modes and overload

Each failure has a safe default. The rule behind all of them: lose quality before you lose permissions.

- **Permissions service down.** Fail closed: refuse, or serve only documents the whole tenant can read. Never fall
  back to unfiltered retrieval.
- **Embedding backlog after a large import.** Serve keyword results first, cap each tenant's share of embedding
  capacity, and never let the ACL lane wait behind it.
- **Reranker saturated.** Rerank fewer candidates, or skip reranking, at a quality cost you measured in advance.
- **Answering model rate-limited or down.** Return the ranked, permitted passages with links and no generated
  answer. Search still works.
- **Noisy tenant.** Per-tenant limits on questions and on ingestion, so one large customer can't starve the rest.
- **Connector flood after an outage.** Replay with backpressure. Content can wait; revocations cannot.

<Pitfall title="A semantic cache in front of the permission check">
Teams add a similarity cache to cut cost, key it on the question and put it before retrieval. The first time two
users with different access ask the same thing, one gets an answer built from the other's documents. Put the
principal fingerprint and ACL version in the key, or cache only content visible to the whole tenant.
</Pitfall>

## Evals and observability

An **eval** is a fixed set of test questions with known good outcomes that you run before every release. For this
system, permission evals are different from quality evals: they have exact right answers, so they can block a
release.

**Permission evals.**

- Synthetic users with known access. For every eval question, assert that no retrieved, reranked or cited
  document is outside that user's set. Include a "Dana" user who was just removed from a group.
- Cross-tenant canaries: unique strings planted in tenant A, searched for from tenant B. Any hit is a
  severity-one incident.
- The revocation probe, tracked as a latency metric against the target (5 minutes in the assumptions).

**Retrieval and answer quality.** Measure recall@k and ranking metrics such as **MRR** (how high the first correct
result is, on average) or **nDCG** (a score that rewards putting the best results first) on labelled questions.
Slice them by source and by how much of the tenant the user can see, because users with narrow access suffer
first. Report a range, not one number: at recall 0.8 on 200 questions, the true value could be about **5.5
points** either way, so compare two configurations on the *same* questions. The llm-eval lab's simulation puts
the smallest real difference that such a paired comparison catches 80% of the time at
<LabNumber lab="llm-eval" metric="Smallest true gap with 80% power, paired test, n=500" /> for 500 questions.
After that, check **faithfulness** (does the answer say only what the passages say?) and citation precision. You
can have another LLM grade those (an **LLM judge**), but only after checking its grades against human labels.

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the interval (for a Staff-level follow-up)</summary>

The 95% half-width of a proportion p measured on n questions is 1.96 × √(p(1 − p) ÷ n). At p = 0.8 and n = 200:
1.96 × √(0.16 ÷ 200) ≈ 1.96 × 0.0283 ≈ 0.055, about 5.5 points.

</details>

**Operational signals.** Latency per stage, lag per connector, ACL-lane lag, post-check drop rate, cache hit rate
per tenant, how often the assistant says "I couldn't find that", and citation-check failures.

## Trade-offs

| Decision | What you gain | What you give up | Choose differently when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shared service, tenant-partitioned indexes | Efficient for thousands of small tenants | Isolation rests on partitioning code | A contract requires separate storage or keys |
| Filter inside retrieval, plus a post-check | No leaks through shared stages; recall holds for narrow users | ACLs must live beside the index and stay fresh | Nearly everyone sees everything: a post-check alone may do |
| Groups looked up per request | Membership changes apply in about a minute | A permissions call per question | The service can't take the load: cache inside your bound |
| ACL-versioned answer cache | Repeated questions stay cheap without leaking | Lower hit rate | Most traffic is about tenant-wide content: cache that aggressively |
| Centralized index | Fast, consistent hybrid ranking | You own ACL replication and deletion lag | Personal or regulated data: federate at question time |
| Reranking 50 candidates | Better top results | About 1,050 pairs/s at peak | Tight latency: rerank 20, or only when the two searches disagree |

<AINative title="Where an agent helps, and what you still check yourself">
Coding agents are good at the volume work here: a connector written from an API's documentation, synthetic users
and permission test cases, synthetic questions for the labelled set. Two things stay yours. Read the filter code:
an agent asked to "add permissions" often writes the post-ranking filter, because it is the smallest change that
makes a demo pass. And check every credential the system and its tools run with. In the Supabase MCP incident (MCP is a protocol that lets an agent call outside tools) a
coding agent connected with the `service_role` key, which bypasses row-level security, and a support ticket
containing instructions got it to copy a tokens table into the ticket
([Simon Willison, 2025-07-06](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Jul/6/supabase-mcp-lethal-trifecta/)). Permission
tests are the part you can make exact, so make them exact and keep them in the release gate.
</AINative>

## Small-team version

At a 50–500 person company this question becomes "an internal assistant over our Drive, Slack and Confluence".
There is one tenant, a few hundred users, and one engineer working with coding agents. The scale problems shrink.
The permission problem does not: one leaked HR or legal passage is a real incident.

### The scoping move: v1 without per-user permissions

Index only content every employee can already open: public wiki spaces, public Slack channels, a company-wide
Drive folder, the handbook. There is no per-user filter to get wrong, though deletion still has to work. Most
questions people ask an internal assistant are about this content anyway. Say it out loud in the interview: "v1
indexes only what everyone can already read; v2 adds per-user permissions, and here is how." It is a real
small-team design choice, not a dodge, as long as you also say how v2 works.

### Build (v2, per-user permissions)

- **Postgres** with a `chunks` table holding the text, its embedding (stored with pgvector's `halfvec`, a 16-bit
  vector type), a full-text search column, and an `acl_principals text[]` column with a GIN index (Postgres's
  index for "does this array contain any of these values"). Retrieval filters on
  `acl_principals && :user_principals`.
- **Principals per request** from the identity provider's groups, cached for about a minute. Identity comes from
  the session; groups never ride in a long-lived token.
- **Filtered vector search, done on purpose.** For narrow users, scan the allowed rows exactly. For broad users,
  enable pgvector's iterative index scans (0.8.0 and later) so a selective filter doesn't silently return 4 rows
  instead of 40 (README, fetched 2026-10-12).
- **The post-check and the canary suite** from the code above, in CI.
- **A revocation bound you can state.** Polling every 15 minutes with 2 minutes to apply, groups per request and
  no answer cache: **17 minutes**. With change events where the source offers them: **about 3 minutes**. Write the
  number down and get the security owner to accept it.

### What it costs at 300 employees

Assume 150,000 documents of 1,500 tokens and 10 questions per person per day. Embedding everything once costs
about **$6**. The vectors take about **2.3 GB** in your existing database. Answering the questions costs about
**$1,080 per 30 days** on Claude Sonnet 5, or about **$162** on gpt-5-mini. Prices were fetched 2026-10-11, and
the two models' answer quality was not compared, so run your own eval before choosing.

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the costs</summary>

- **Chunks:** 400-token chunks with 50 tokens of overlap: ceil((1,500 − 50) ÷ 350) = 5 per document → 750,000
  chunks, about 300M tokens to embed. At $0.02 per 1M tokens (text-embedding-3-small, fetched 2026-10-11): $6.
- **Vectors:** 750,000 × 1,536 dimensions × 4 B = 4.6 GB as float32; 2.3 GB as `halfvec`.
- **Questions:** 300 × 10 = 3,000 a day, each about 4,000 input and 400 output tokens.
  Claude Sonnet 5 ($2 / $10 per 1M input / output): 4,000 × $2 ÷ 1M + 400 × $10 ÷ 1M = $0.012 per question →
  $36 a day → about $1,080 per 30 days.
  gpt-5-mini ($0.25 / $2): $0.001 + $0.0008 = $0.0018 per question → about $162 per 30 days.
- **Revocation:** max(15 + 2, 1) + 0 = 17 minutes polling; max(1 + 2, 1) + 0 = 3 minutes with events.

</details>

### Buy instead?

Enterprise search products exist for exactly this; Glean, the company behind the second reported question, sells
one. Glean's pricing page shows no public price, only a demo request (checked 2026-10-12), and model providers'
enterprise plans include connectors. Before buying, ask each vendor two questions and get the answers in writing.
Does it enforce each source's permissions per user at question time? And what is its bound on how long a revoked
document stays citable? A vendor that can't give a number has the same problem you would, hidden. Buying is
usually right when you need many connectors with permission sync and nobody to maintain them. Building is right
when you need two or three sources, custom answer flows, or data that must stay in your own database.

### Delivery plan

- **Week 1.** Company-wide content from two sources, deletion reconciliation every 15 minutes, citations with
  links, a refusal when nothing matches, and tracing. Release to a pilot group.
- **Week 2.** The permission eval suite: synthetic users, canary documents in a restricted folder, a nightly
  revocation probe. Per-user ACLs for one source behind a flag. The post-check with a drop-rate metric.
- **Week 3.** A second permissioned source, connector lag shown on citations, and a written revocation bound
  signed off by whoever owns security.

### What to verify yourself

That a canary document in an HR-only folder never appears for a non-HR test user, in retrieval, citations, caches
or logs. That the revocation probe meets the stated bound. That no service, agent or tool runs with a credential
that bypasses row-level security. That an offboarded user's session and cached groups stop working within the
bound.

### Revisit when

A second business unit needs isolation from the first (now you have tenants). Connectors multiply past what you
can maintain. The corpus passes a few million chunks with heavy updates. Or a customer-facing version needs
contractual isolation.

### What small-company interviewers probe instead

Little first-hand evidence exists for this question at 50–500 person companies. Expect the problem framed as "our
docs" rather than "many tenants". Expect the same signals small companies probe elsewhere: failure handling (an
Elicit hiring manager describes probing error handling, retries, fallbacks and timeouts,
[Latent Space, 2024-06-21](https://www.latent.space/p/hiring)) and a working build over a whiteboard.

## Follow-ups

<FollowUps>
  <FollowUp q="(PracHub's write-up of an OpenAI-tagged question) A document is deleted in the source. How quickly and how reliably does it disappear from answers?">
Give the bound, not "quickly": detection (events, or polling plus reconciliation), applying a tombstone, the
token lifetime if groups ride in a token, and any cache not keyed by ACL version. "Reliably" means a scheduled
revocation probe per tenant that measures it, plus cleanup of derived copies such as logs and eval sets.
  </FollowUp>
  <FollowUp q="(PracHub's write-up of an OpenAI-tagged question) How would you fine-tune the embedding model for a customer's domain?">
Mine question-passage pairs from the corpus (headings to sections, generated questions to passages, filtered),
train with a contrastive loss and in-batch negatives, compare recall@k against the base model on held-out
labelled questions, then re-embed into a shadow index and switch over.
  </FollowUp>
  <FollowUp q="(Reported, Cohere) How would caching by question similarity work without leaking?">
Cache question embeddings freely. Cache answers only for tenant-wide content, or key personalized answers by
tenant, principal fingerprint and ACL version. The similarity lookup sits inside those keys, never in front of
the permission filter.
  </FollowUp>
  <FollowUp q="(Reported, Cohere) A downstream party should no longer see some data. What happens?">
The ACL lane updates the store and re-tags chunks without re-embedding, queries filter on the new principals, the
post-check catches index lag, and ACL-versioned caches miss. Reports already generated from that data are found
through their stored chunk ids and flagged or regenerated. Name the remaining window in minutes.
  </FollowUp>
  <FollowUp q="(Glean prep guide) A connector was down for six hours. What do users see, and what happens on recovery?">
The last synced content with its lag shown, or the source hidden past an agreed threshold. On recovery, replay
from the cursor with versioned idempotent upserts, ACL changes and deletes first, content after.
  </FollowUp>
  <FollowUp q="(Reported title, Google) Search across one user's email, calendar, docs and chat: federate or centralize?">
Centralize sources with change events and permission APIs; federate personal and fast-changing ones with a
fan-out deadline; merge by rank. Federation makes revocation near-instant at the cost of latency and ranking
quality.
  </FollowUp>
  <FollowUp q="(Not reported; a natural next push) One tenant has 50M documents and most have 10,000. How do you shard?">
Partition by tenant, give large tenants their own shard sets split by a hash of the document id, pack small
tenants together, route by tenant id, and keep per-tenant rate limits so a large neighbour can't starve the rest.
  </FollowUp>
</FollowUps>

## Common mistakes

- **Filtering after ranking or after generation.** The Glean guide calls post-ranking filtering "simple and wrong
  at scale" (<Cite id="s-768711771a" />). Filter inside retrieval and keep a post-check.
- **"It disappears quickly."** That is not a bound; give one with its terms (<Cite id="s-f8053ff3a7" />).
- **Group claims in long-lived tokens.** One hour of token lifetime outweighs every other term.
- **Re-embedding on a permission change.** Revocations become as slow as the embedding queue.
- **Expanding groups onto documents.** One membership change becomes millions of writes.
- **A similarity cache in front of the filter.** It hands one user's answer to another.
- **Going deep before laying out the components.** That is the rejected Cohere candidate's own hindsight, not a
  reason the interviewers gave (<Cite id="s-d5d3016775" />).

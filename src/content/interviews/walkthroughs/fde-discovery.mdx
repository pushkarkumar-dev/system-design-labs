## The prompt

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "Before I design anything, I'd like to understand the problem. Who uses this, what do they do today, and what
> would make it a success, as a number? What data would it see, does it only answer or does it also take actions,
> and what must never go wrong? Then I'll propose one small first version for one team, a test set of about 50 real
> examples that your expert grades, and a date where we decide together whether to widen it or stop."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer, so you can say it with understanding.

A customer brings an open problem: "help with our support tickets", or "put Claude on our knowledge base". Claude
and ChatGPT are **large language models** (LLMs): programs that read text and write text back, and that you
usually rent through a paid web API. The interviewer is waiting to see what you do next. If you start drawing a
design, you fail. If you start asking questions, and the answers visibly change what you build, you pass.

This page covers four related rounds that test the same habit:

- **Discovery** with a customer who has a vague ask (eng-079).
- **Scoping** one common ask, "AI on our support tickets", into a measurable first project (eng-081).
- **A skeptical executive** who asks why they shouldn't just use a competitor's model (eng-082).
- **Debugging from a diagram** when a customer says "requests are failing" (eng-080).

### Why this is not the requirements meeting you already know

You have run requirements meetings for normal software. There, the hard part is usually the build. Once you know
what the system must do, a correct design gives correct results every time.

An AI feature is different in three ways, and every good answer in this family is built around them:

1. **The output is sometimes wrong, and it looks right when it is.** A normal service either returns the right row or
   throws an error. A model can write a fluent, confident answer that is false. So the first design question is not
   "how do we build it?" but "who sees a wrong answer, and what does it cost them?"
2. **"Does it work?" is a measurement, not a yes or no.** You can't write a unit test that proves a model's replies
   are good. You need real examples, graded by someone who knows the domain, and a number. Without that number,
   the project ends in opinions.
3. **The model is the easy part.** You call it through an API in an afternoon. The real work is getting the right
   data in front of it, deciding what it may do, and measuring it. Naming a model early answers the wrong question.

That is why an engineer who opens with architecture fails this round, even with a good architecture. The technical
answer is easy. Reaching it before you understand the problem is the mistake being tested.

## What is being tested

### Your first move

The OpenAI prep guide puts it in one line: "strong candidates start from the customer and the definition of
success; weak ones start naming models" (<Cite id="s-26f0cae3fe" />). The first-hand redirect in eng-079 shows a
real interviewer sending the same signal.

Formation's design-interview guide makes the same case for AI design rounds in general. Before architecture, it
says, you settle who the users are, how they interact with the system, what documents it uses, whether it reads or
also writes, and how much damage it does when it is "confidently wrong"
([Formation, 2026-06-17](https://formation.dev/blog/the-biggest-mistake-engineers-make-in-ai-system-design-interviews)).

Say this when you start: "Before I suggest anything, can I understand how this works today?"

### Turning answers into scope

Asking questions is not enough. You are graded on what you do with the answers. A strong candidate ends with three
things: a narrow first project, a number that defines success, and a plan for widening it. The Anthropic prep
guide's worked example ends with "I would start with one team and one document set" (<Cite id="s-a982325743" />).

### Staying calm with a skeptic

In the executive round, your reflex will be to pitch. The vendor blog says strong candidates instead "take notes",
"reflect back" and "do not open a code editor" (<Cite id="s-2f725b952e" />). To **reflect back** means to repeat the
problem in your own words and check that you heard it right.

### Debugging by hypothesis

For eng-080 the candidate writes: "The entire round is about whether you can run a hypothesis-driven debugging
process under ambiguity. You're expected to drive the investigation: ask for specific logs, metrics, or traces."
(<Cite id="s-88b41bdbd6" />). You already do this on incident calls. The new part is knowing where a model in the
path tends to fail.

### Communication is the job

Pragmatic Engineer describes FDE work as roughly 25% coding, 50% integration and 25% customer-facing
(<Dated asOf="2026-05-24" src="s-e4c26adb42">The Pulse on forward-deployed engineering</Dated>). So these rounds
are not a soft add-on. They test a quarter of the actual job.

My reading: an experienced backend engineer has run requirements meetings and incident calls. This round is both,
with a model in the middle.

## Clarify and scope

Here, clarifying *is* the design work. You do it out loud, with the interviewer playing the customer.

### Words you need first

- A **prompt** is the text you send to the model: instructions, any documents it should use, and the user's
  question.
- **Retrieval** means searching the customer's documents for the few passages relevant to a question and putting
  them into the prompt. A system built this way is called **RAG** (retrieval-augmented generation). You need it
  when the knowledge is too large to paste into every prompt.
- An **eval** (short for evaluation) is a test set for a model feature: real examples, each with a judgment of what a
  good answer is, plus a score. It plays the role a test suite plays for normal code.
- A **labelled example** is one input plus the expert's judgment of the right output. "Labelling 50 tickets" means
  the support lead marks what a good reply to each would be.

### The worked example used on this page

Follow one customer through the whole page. A software company of about 300 people sends 3,000 support tickets a
month. The head of support says: "We want to use AI on our tickets." Every number below is an assumption you would
state out loud, and the interviewer may change it.

Ask in layers: first the outcome, then the data, then the risk. After each answer, say what it changes.

| You ask | Example answer | What it changes | What to say in the interview |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who uses it, and what do they do with the output? | "Our 12 support agents." | An internal user catches mistakes; an end customer doesn't. | "Since agents see drafts before customers do, a wrong draft costs an edit, not a complaint." |
| What happens today, and what does it cost? | "About 6 minutes per ticket." | This is the baseline you must beat. | "Then success means cutting that time, and we can measure it." |
| What does success look like, as a number? | "Not sure." | Becomes the eval, and later the decision on whether to widen. | "Can we agree on one: say, handle time down 20% with no rise in re-opened tickets?" |
| How many requests, and how bursty? | "3,000 a month, busy on Mondays." | Sets cost and rate limits; tells you whether replies must be instant. | "That's a small volume. Cost won't decide this; quality will." |
| Should it answer a person, or act on their behalf? | "It might issue refunds one day." | Read-only versus taking actions. Actions need approval, safe retries and an audit log. | "Phase one only drafts. No action happens without a person." |
| Where does the knowledge live, how big, how fresh, who may see what? | "A help centre of 400 articles, partly out of date." | Whether you need retrieval, how you keep it fresh, and access control. | "Stale articles mean confident wrong answers. Let's sample them in week one." |
| What have you tried, and why did it fail? | "A chatbot pilot last year. Customers hated it." | Saves you from rebuilding the failed pilot. | "Then let's not start with customers talking to a bot." |
| What must never happen? | "Promising refunds we don't give." | Defines the worst case, sometimes called the **blast radius**. | "So anything about refunds or policy goes to a human." |
| Who owns the labels and signs off? | "Me, I suppose." | Without an expert who grades answers there is no eval. | "Could you grade 50 real tickets with me next week? That becomes our test." |

Two kinds of system came up in that table. An **interactive** system answers a person and stops. An **agentic**
system takes actions on its own, such as issuing a refund or editing a record. Writes are where the real damage
happens, so they need a human approval step, retries that are safe to repeat (**idempotent**), and an audit trail.

If the role-player won't answer a question, write down the assumption you will carry and keep going: "I'll assume
internal agents, read-only, and the support lead as the grader. Tell me if that's wrong."

Then say what is out of scope for phase one: full automation, every team at once, and **fine-tuning** (retraining a
model on your own examples) before simpler prompting and retrieval have been measured.

Finish the step by reflecting back in one sentence: "So the pain is the time agents spend on billing questions, not
volume overall, and the worst case is a wrong promise about refunds. Is that right?"

## Back-of-envelope

Two numbers matter in a scoping conversation. First: can the value pay for the work? Second: how many graded
examples do you need before you can trust the result?

### Words you need first

- A **token** is the unit a model reads and writes, roughly ¾ of an English word. Providers charge per million
  tokens, with **input tokens** (what you send) cheaper than **output tokens** (what the model writes).
- **Handle time** is how long an agent spends on one ticket. It is the number most support teams already track.

### Value versus cost, in plain words

For our customer, agent time today costs about **$10,500 a month** (3,000 tickets × 6 minutes, at an assumed
loaded $35 an hour). If AI-written drafts cut handle time by 25%, an
assumption the pilot must measure, that saves about **$2,625 a month**.

The model itself costs about **$33 a month** for all 3,000 tickets on Claude Sonnet 5 at list price. That is about
1% of the saving. So the business case does not rest on the model bill at all. It rests on the measured cut in
handle time and on whether the drafts are good enough to use.

The bigger cost is engineering. An assumed three weeks of one engineer costs about **$11,200**. At a 25% cut, that pays back in
about **four months**. If the pilot measures only 10%, payback stretches to about **11 months**.

Say this out loud, because it is the sentence an executive remembers: "The model costs about $33 a month. The real
question is whether the drafts save agents time, and the pilot will tell us how much."

### How many examples you need

When you test 50 examples and 85% pass, the true pass rate is not exactly 85%. With only 50 examples, it could
easily be anywhere from about 75% to 95%. That spread is called the **confidence interval**: the range where the
true value very likely lies. More examples narrow it.

- **50 examples** give about **±10 points**. Enough to tell whether the idea works at all.
- **200 examples** give about **±5 points**. Enough to see a moderate difference.
- Choosing between two close options needs hundreds, or a **paired comparison**: run both options on the same
  examples and compare them item by item, which cancels out how hard each example is.

Say it like this: "Fifty graded tickets tell us whether this works at all. They won't tell us which of two good
models is better."

## High-level design

The design you present is a plan with a narrow first slice, not a platform. A **slice** is one small, complete piece
of the feature: one team, one job, end to end.

**Shadow mode** means the feature runs on real traffic, but its output is only recorded or shown to staff, never
acted on automatically. You learn how it behaves before anyone depends on it. A **go/no-go** meeting is a decision
date, set in advance, where the agreed number decides whether you widen or stop.

### Three possible first slices, ranked by risk

For the support-ticket case there are three natural slices. Rank them by who sees a mistake.

1. **Triage and routing.** The model reads each ticket, tags it and sends it to the right queue. A mistake costs a
   re-route. No customer ever sees the model.
2. **Agent assist.** The model drafts a reply, with links to the help-centre articles it used. An agent edits it and
   sends it. The agent catches mistakes. You measure handle time and **edit distance** (how much the agent changed
   the draft).
3. **Deflection.** A bot answers customers directly, so the ticket never reaches an agent. Highest value, and highest
   risk. A Canadian tribunal held Air Canada responsible for its chatbot's wrong advice
   ([Law360, 2024-02-14](https://www.law360.ca/ca/articles/1804075)), and Cursor's support bot invented a login
   policy that users then cancelled over
   ([Fortune, April 2025](https://fortune.com/article/customer-support-ai-cursor-went-rogue)).

Recommend 1 or 2 first. Make deflection a later phase, gated on measured **grounding**: whether the answers stick to
what the source documents actually say.

Say it like this: "I'd start with agent assist. The agents are a free safety net, and their edits tell us exactly
where the drafts are wrong."

### The first slice, as a system

Only now do you name components. For agent assist:

```
Ticketing tool (where agents already work)
   │  1. new ticket arrives (webhook)
   ▼
Integration service ── reads the ticket, checks which customer and queue
   │  2. find relevant help articles
   ▼
Retrieval ── searches the help centre (keyword or hybrid search)
   │  3. build the prompt: instructions + articles + ticket
   ▼
Model client ── one provider's API behind a thin interface
   │  4. draft reply with links to the articles used
   ▼
Review panel inside the ticketing tool ── agent edits, sends or discards
   │
   └── every step writes to one trace per ticket (articles, prompt version, tokens, time, outcome)
```

**Ticketing tool.** Agents keep working where they already work. A new tool they must open is a new tool they will
ignore.

**Integration service.** A small service you own. It receives the ticket, knows which customer and queue it belongs
to, and decides whether this ticket is in the pilot at all.

**Retrieval.** Searches the 400 help articles for the few relevant to this ticket. Start with **keyword search**
(matching words, often called BM25) or **hybrid search** (keyword plus meaning-based search), inside a database you
already run. A specialist **vector database** (a database built for meaning-based search) is not needed at this size.

**Model client.** One provider, called through a thin interface of your own, so switching provider later is a
configuration change.

**Review panel.** The agent sees the draft and the linked articles, and edits, sends or discards it. Each of those
actions is a free label for your eval.

**Trace.** One record per ticket of everything that happened. A **trace** is the request's path through every
service, with timings, tied together by a shared ID. It is what you will ask for later in the debugging round.

## Deep dives

### The discovery conversation, run well

Open by restating the ask and asking permission to dig: "Before I suggest anything, can I understand how tickets
flow today?" Then go from outcome, to data, to risk, as in the table above.

After the main questions, reflect back what you heard in one sentence ("so the pain is first-response time on
billing questions, not volume overall"), and only then propose. The prep guide's sequence matches: outcome, where
the data lives, what was tried, reflect the constraint, then "a narrow first slice", and name how you'll know it
worked (<Cite id="s-a982325743" />).

Take notes visibly. The vendor blog says strong candidates do (<Cite id="s-2f725b952e" />). It also signals that the
answers will change your design.

Run it as a conversation, not a questionnaire. If the customer mentions the failed chatbot pilot, follow it: "What
did customers dislike about it?" That one answer may decide your first slice.

### The skeptical executive

Now the interviewer plays the company's chief technology officer (CTO). In the vendor blog's version, they say:
"Honestly, I'm not sure Claude does anything ChatGPT can't already do — convince me." (<Cite id="s-2f725b952e" />).
A prep guide describes this round as a discovery session with no code editor and calls it "the gatekeeper"
(<Cite id="s-a982325743" />).

The trap is a feature war. You can't win it from memory, and the executive will remember any overclaim. A strong
answer has five moves:

1. **Acknowledge.** Both are capable models, and for many tasks the difference is small.
2. **Reframe to their outcome.** What would the assistant do, for whom, and what does a wrong answer look like?
3. **Offer evidence instead of adjectives.** A **bake-off**: run both models on 50 of their real questions, graded by
   their people, with the pass criteria agreed before anyone sees the outputs.
4. **Name what really differs for a company, as things to check in current documentation**, not things to assert:
   data retention and training terms, which clouds and regions it is offered in, how it handles their long
   documents, tool and connector support, rate limits, and price after **tokenization** (how text is split into
   tokens, which differs between vendors). Anthropic's pricing page notes newer Claude models produce about 30%
   more tokens for the same text
   ([Anthropic pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing), fetched 2026-10-11), so price per
   token alone misleads.
5. **Lower the stakes.** Keep the model behind a thin interface, so switching later is a configuration change. The
   retrieval, permissions and eval you build carry over whichever model wins.

Say it like this: "I can't settle this with adjectives, and you shouldn't trust me if I tried. Let's run both on 50
of your real questions, graded by your people, and decide on the result."

If you interview at a model vendor, you are allowed to be an advocate. But the credible form of advocacy is
confidence in the bake-off.

### Debugging from a diagram (eng-080)

The interviewer shows a diagram and says a customer reports that requests are failing. No logs are given, so your
method is the answer.

**First, narrow the symptom.** Which requests fail: all, one endpoint, one customer, only large inputs? Since when,
and what changed then? Is it an error or a timeout? What fraction, steady or bursty?

**Then walk the diagram hop by hop.** At each hop, ask for the cheapest evidence that splits the possibilities in
half. The candidate's framing is a good script: "The most likely failure domain is X because of Y. To confirm, I'd
want to see Z. If that's clean, I'd move to W." (<Cite id="s-88b41bdbd6" />). When a data point contradicts your
hypothesis, say so and move on.

Practise on the system you just designed. Picture the agent-assist slice a month after launch: one queue's agents
say drafts have stopped appearing (an illustrative case, not the reported one). You would ask for error rates per
hop, find the first hop that fails, then pull one failing trace and one working trace side by side.

For a system with a model in the path, these are the likely failure areas, roughly in order of how often they
explain "requests are failing":

- **Provider limits.** The API returns HTTP **429** ("too many requests") when you exceed a rate limit or a monthly
  spend cap. Anthropic's docs say that at the cap the API returns 429 without `retry-after`, so SDK retries keep
  failing ([Anthropic rate limits](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/api/rate-limits), fetched 2026-10-11).
- **Input size.** One customer's documents push prompts past the **context window** (the most tokens a model can read
  in one request) or past the max-token budget.
- **Timeouts.** Longer outputs or a slower model exceed a gateway timeout that was set for the old model.
- **Parsing.** A model or prompt change breaks the output format your code expects, and the service returns 500 when
  it can't parse the reply.
- **Dependencies.** The retrieval store, an expired auth token, a key rotated in one region only.
- **Ordinary infra.** A deploy, DNS, a saturated connection pool.

Ask for: error rate and status codes per hop, a sample trace of a failing request, deploy and config history, and
the provider's status page. **Trace context** (the shared request ID passed through every hop) is what turns this
into a five-minute question in real life.

Say it like this: "I'd rather ask for one per-hop error rate than all the logs. It tells me where to look next."

## Failure modes and overload

Most failures here belong to the engagement, not the system. Each one has a cheap guard you can set in week one.

- **Scope creep.** Every stakeholder adds a use case. Hold the first slice and log the rest for phase two.
- **No labels, no owner.** Without someone who can say what a good answer is, there is no eval, and the pilot ends
  in opinions. Make the owner a precondition.
- **Pilot on clean data.** Demo documents behave. Real ones are scanned PDFs and stale wiki pages. Sample real data in
  week one.
- **Silent writes.** Any action on the customer's behalf without approval and audit. Require a human approval step until
  measured **precision** (the share of its actions that were right) supports removing it, one action at a time.
- **Untrusted content.** Tickets and emails are written by outsiders, so an attacker can hide instructions in them
  for the model to follow (**prompt injection**). A support agent that has private data, reads untrusted input and
  can send messages out is the "lethal trifecta" Simon Willison describes
  ([2025-07-06](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Jul/6/supabase-mcp-lethal-trifecta/)).
- **Provider outage.** Assembled measured provider uptime over recent months at 99.80% (OpenAI) and 99.58%
  (Anthropic), and built failover within a capability tier
  ([Assembled, 2025-06-12](https://www.assembled.com/blog/your-llm-provider-will-go-down-but-you-dont-have-to)).
  Decide during scoping whether the feature degrades to "no draft" or needs a fallback provider.

For agent assist, "no draft" is a fine fallback: agents just work as they did before. Say so, because it means you
don't need a second provider in phase one.

## Evals and observability

The success number from discovery becomes three things.

- **Offline:** a labelled set of real tickets, graded by the support lead, with pass criteria agreed in advance.
  "Offline" means you run it on stored examples, like a test suite. Run it on every prompt or model change.
- **Online:** measurements from real use. Handle time, how often drafts are accepted and how much they are edited,
  re-open rate, escalations, **CSAT** (customer satisfaction score) on AI-assisted tickets, and cost per ticket.
- **Traces:** one per request, with the retrieved documents, prompt version, model, tokens, latency and outcome.
  That turns the next "requests are failing" into a lookup.

For a model bake-off (the executive's question), use a paired comparison on the same items. Even so, small evals
can't see small differences. The llm-eval lab's simulation shows that even 500 items only detect gaps of about
<LabNumber lab="llm-eval" metric="Smallest true gap with 80% power, paired test, n=500" /> with 80% power (the two
figures are for two systems that disagree on 20% and on 40% of items). So agree
in advance that "within a few points" means "pick on cost and terms".

Say it like this: "If the two models land within a few points, we can't tell them apart with this test. Then we
pick on price and contract terms."

## Trade-offs

| Decision | Chose | Gave up | Choose differently when |
|---|---|---|---|
| First slice | Agent assist or triage | Faster headline value from deflection | Grounding is measured and errors are cheap |
| Breadth | One team, one document set | Company-wide announcement | The first slice clears its metric |
| Model | Managed API, one provider behind an interface | Lowest possible unit cost | Data-residency rules, volume or an eval result says otherwise |
| Retrieval | Keyword or hybrid search in the existing database | Specialist vector features | Millions of vectors with heavy filtering and updates |
| Automation | Human approval on every write | Throughput | Per-action precision clears an agreed bar |

**Data residency** in that table means rules about which country or region the data may be stored and processed
in. It is one of the few reasons to run a model yourself.

<AINative title="Agents in discovery, and what stays human">
An agent can do the prep: read the customer's public docs and help centre, cluster a sample of 500 exported tickets
into themes with counts, draft the discovery questions and a one-page plan, and produce a first labelled set for the
domain owner to correct. What it can't do is the conversation: hearing which constraint the customer cares about,
noticing the pilot that already failed, or getting a support lead to agree what counts as a good answer. Verify the
agent's ticket clusters by reading 30 tickets yourself; theme counts from a model are a hypothesis, not data.
</AINative>

## Small-team version

At a 50–500 person company, the "customer" is the head of support, the sales lead or the CEO, and discovery is most
of the job. Job postings ask AI engineers to talk with users to define problems
([Granola](https://api.ashbyhq.com/posting-api/job-board/granola), still listed 2026-10-11), and some small-company
loops include a session where the interviewer plays the product manager
([Elicit on Latent Space](https://www.latent.space/p/hiring), 2024-06-21) or a product-manager chat
([Avantos.ai on Hacker News](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=46860342), 2026-02-02).

### Build, buy, skip

- **Build:** the discovery notes, the labelled set, the success metric, the thin first slice inside the tool people
  already use, and tracing.
- **Buy or call:** the model (a managed API). Also seriously consider the AI add-on of the ticketing or knowledge
  tool you already pay for. Put its per-seat or per-resolution price into the value comparison above, next to your
  build cost, and check what it lets you evaluate and export.
- **Skip:** a custom platform, fine-tuning, and agents that take actions before anyone has measured the drafts.

### Default stack and when to deviate

A managed API from one provider; retrieval in the database you already run; prompts in version control; the
labelled set run in CI; tracing with cost per request. Switch to a vendor product when it covers the first slice
and its quality on your labelled set is close. Run your own model only for data residency or very high, steady
volume.

### Cost math (dated)

About **$33 a month** in model calls for 3,000 tickets on Claude Sonnet 5. **Prompt caching**, where the provider
charges much less for a repeated start of the prompt such as the fixed instructions, cuts that to about **$22**. That
compares with $10,500 a month of agent time.

Discovery itself costs engineer-days: two days of interviews and labelling, about **$1,500**. It is the cheapest
insurance in the project.

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the numbers (for a Staff-level follow-up)</summary>

- **With caching:** 2,000 cached prefix tokens at $0.20 per M ($0.0004) + 2,000 uncached input at $2 per M
  ($0.004) + 300 output at $10 per M ($0.003) ≈ $0.0074 per ticket, × 3,000 ≈ $22 a month. This ignores the occasional cache write, which costs
  a little more than a normal input token.
- **Discovery:** $747 a day loaded ($194K ÷ 260 working days) × 2 days ≈ $1,500.

</details>

### A realistic delivery plan

- **Week 1:** interviews with the support lead and two agents; export 500 tickets; label 50 with the lead.
- **Weeks 2–3:** the first slice in shadow mode; read 20 traces a day.
- **Week 4:** go/no-go meeting on the agreed metric; widen to one more queue, or stop.

### What you verify yourself

The labels and who signed them off, which data the model may see, that no write happens without approval, the
provider's data terms, and the spend cap.

### What small-company interviewers probe

"Our CEO wants AI in the product by next quarter; what do you do first?", "how would you say no?", and "what did it
cost and what did it save?". The Elicit and Avantos.ai loops above show product-manager voices in these rounds.

## Follow-ups

<FollowUps>
  <FollowUp q="What would you ask the customer before designing anything? (Reported, OpenAI FDE)">
    Users and what they do with the output; today's process and its cost; success as a number; volume; whether it
    only answers or also acts; where the knowledge lives and who may see it; what was tried; what must never happen;
    who owns the labels.
  </FollowUp>
  <FollowUp q="How would you know it is working well enough to expand to the next team? (OpenAI FDE prep guide)">
    An agreed metric on a labelled set from that team, plus online signals (acceptance, re-open rate, escalations)
    over a fixed period, with the go threshold written down before the pilot started.
  </FollowUp>
  <FollowUp q="The customer's data turns out to be a mess. Now what? (Not reported; a natural next push)">
    Measure the mess: the share of documents that are scans, duplicates or stale. Shrink the first slice to the clean
    subset or add a cleaning step, and show the effect on the eval rather than promising it.
  </FollowUp>
  <FollowUp q="The interviewer hands you data that contradicts your hypothesis. (Reported, Cohere: acknowledge and pivot)">
    Say it contradicts, drop or narrow the hypothesis, and ask for the next cheapest piece of evidence that splits
    the possibilities. Explaining contradictions away is the failure the candidate warns about.
  </FollowUp>
  <FollowUp q="The executive says the pilot is too slow; they want it company-wide next month. (Not reported; a natural next push)">
    Offer the trade explicitly: what widening skips (labels for other teams, access-control checks), the risk that
    brings, and a faster but still gated plan, such as two teams in parallel.
  </FollowUp>
  <FollowUp q="When is AI the wrong answer here? (Not reported; a natural next push)">
    When the pain is a missing help-centre article, a broken workflow or a rule a few lines of code would encode. Say
    so; it builds more trust than a pilot.
  </FollowUp>
</FollowUps>

## Common mistakes

- **Architecture first.** The eng-079 candidate was stopped for exactly this (<Cite id="s-812dbc9385" />).
- **Pitching instead of asking.** The vendor blog names pitching as the common mistake in the executive role-play
  (<Cite id="s-2f725b952e" />).
- **A success metric nobody owns.** Without labels there is no go/no-go, only opinions.
- **Generic debugging checklists.** The Cohere candidate warns against a checklist with no hypothesis behind it
  (<Cite id="s-88b41bdbd6" />).
- **Promising deflection first.** It is the riskiest slice, with public examples of what goes wrong.
- **Feature claims from memory.** Name what to check, not what you half-remember.

## The prompt

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "This is a job queue, like the ones I've built for emails or reports, but each job holds a whole GPU for a minute
> or more, so losing or repeating one costs real money. I write every job to a database before I return its ID, and
> that table is the only source of truth. Workers borrow a job for a short time and keep renewing the loan. Every
> loan gets a new number, and a worker can save its result only if its number is still the current one, so a
> worker that froze and woke up late can't overwrite anything. GPUs are shared by GPU time used, not by job count,
> with a weight for the paid tier. And when demand outruns the fleet, I say no at the door instead of accepting work
> I can't finish."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer, so you can say it with understanding.

Users submit text-to-video requests: "a 12-second clip of a fox running through snow". Each request becomes a task
that runs on exactly one GPU for a minute or more. Design the backend: how a request is accepted so it is never
lost, how tasks share the fleet fairly with a paid tier that must get results faster, how a crashed or stalled
worker is detected and its task safely re-run, how a new model version that fails only on some GPU types is
contained, and how the system copes when thousands of tasks arrive at once.

### What makes this different from a normal job queue

You have probably built a background job queue: send an email, resize an image, build a PDF report. Those jobs take
milliseconds to seconds. They run on cheap CPU machines you can add in seconds. If one runs twice, a user gets two
emails and nobody loses much.

A GPU job queue keeps the same shape but changes three things, and the whole design follows from them:

1. **Each job is long and expensive.** A video task holds one GPU (a graphics processor rented for about one or two
   dollars an hour) for 90 seconds or more. Losing a job means a user who paid and got nothing. Running it twice means paying
   twice and maybe delivering two different videos.
2. **The hardware is not all the same.** A fleet often mixes two or more GPU types. A new model version can work on
   one type and crash on another, so a failure can come from the job, the machine, or the pairing of model and GPU
   type.
3. **You can't add capacity quickly.** A new GPU worker must first load the model, which can be tens of gigabytes.
   That takes tens of seconds, so you can't simply "autoscale" your way out of a sudden burst.

Three reported questions are the same problem at three zoom levels: the whole scheduler, the "never lose a request"
guarantee, and the burst.

## Clarify and scope

Spend two or three minutes pinning down numbers, because every later decision depends on them. Say each assumption
out loud.

1. **Rates and durations.** How many tasks arrive per second, and how bursty is it? How long does a task use the
   GPU, on average and in the worst case? "Let's assume 2 tasks a second on average and 90 seconds of GPU time per
   task, with a long tail."
2. **Fleet.** How many GPUs, of which types? "Assume 200 GPUs across two types."
3. **Fairness unit and tiers.** Is fairness per user, per organization or per tier? What does "faster" mean for the
   paid tier: a wait-time target, a guaranteed share, or the right to interrupt free work?
4. **Durability.** Define "lost". Propose: "We returned a job ID, and then the job never reached a final state the
   user can see." Turning a request away before you give it an ID is allowed; that's not loss.
5. **Delivery and cancellation.** Does the user poll for the result, get a callback (a **webhook**: an HTTP call we
   make to a URL they gave us), or both? Can they cancel a queued or running task?
6. **Checkpointing.** Can a task save its progress partway and resume after a crash (a **checkpoint**), or must it
   restart from zero? "Assume it restarts from zero."

**Out of scope, said out loud:** the video model itself, content moderation details, and billing beyond quotas.

## Back-of-envelope

Three numbers shape this design: how busy the fleet is, how long a burst takes to clear, and how much GPU time a
crash wastes. Explain what each one means before you compute it.

### Words you need first

- **Utilization**: the share of the fleet that is busy on average. Written **ρ** (the Greek letter rho). At 50%
  utilization a new job usually finds a free GPU. Near 100%, any small burst makes a queue that takes a long time to
  clear.
- **Little's law**: on average, the number of things inside a system equals the rate they arrive times how long
  each one stays. Here: GPUs busy = tasks per second × GPU seconds per task. It is the one formula you need.
- **GPU-seconds**: one GPU busy for one second. A 90-second task costs 90 GPU-seconds. This is the unit of work,
  and later the unit of fairness.

### How busy is the fleet?

With our assumptions, Little's law gives **180 GPUs busy out of 200, so the fleet is 90% utilized**. That sounds
efficient. It is also dangerous: there is only a little spare capacity, so any burst piles up.

"At 90% utilization I only have about 10% spare capacity, so a burst doesn't just cause a short delay. It creates a
backlog that takes a long time to drain."

### How long does a burst take to clear?

The fleet can finish about **2.2 tasks a second** (200 GPUs, each finishing a task every 90 seconds). Suppose a
10-minute burst arrives at 5 tasks a second. The queue grows by the difference, and ends up about **1,667 tasks
deep**. When traffic falls back to 2 a second, the fleet has only about 0.22 spare tasks a second to drain it, so the
backlog takes **about 2 hours** to clear.

That is the number that justifies **admission control**: deciding at the front door whether to accept a job, based
on how long it would wait. Without it, a 10-minute burst becomes two hours of slow results for everybody. With it,
you can turn away or delay free-tier work, or add capacity, before the queue gets that deep.

The same arithmetic gives the **honest wait estimate** the write-up asks for: the GPU-seconds of work ahead of you
in your tier's queue, divided by the GPU-seconds per second your tier is getting right now.

### How much does a crash cost?

A worker borrows a job for a limited time and renews the loan by sending a small "still here" message every few
seconds, called a **heartbeat**. The length of the loan is its **TTL** (time to live). If heartbeats stop and the
TTL runs out, the system assumes the worker is dead and gives the job to someone else. Assume a heartbeat every 10 seconds and a 30-second TTL. Then a crash is noticed **within 30 seconds**. With no
checkpoints, a crash at a random point of a 90-second task wastes **about 45 seconds of work plus up to 30 seconds
of waiting to notice**.

### Two more numbers for the follow-ups

- **The Modal burst.** 10,000 short tasks of an assumed 0.5 seconds each are 5,000 GPU-seconds of work. On 250 warm
  containers (assumed; a **warm** container already has the model loaded and can start a task at once) that clears in **about 20 seconds**. A new container must first load a 40 GB model (the size
  in the Modal article), which at an assumed 2 GB/s also takes **about 20 seconds**. So starting new containers
  during a one-second burst only helps tasks that would otherwise wait longer than about 20 seconds.
- **The NVIDIA "5,000 concurrent" question.** If each request takes 2 seconds end to end (assumed), 5,000
  concurrent requests means **2,500 new requests a second** to write durably. That sounds like a lot of disk
  writes, but the database can save them in groups (explained in the deep dive), which brings it down to about
  **200 disk flushes a second**. One well-provisioned database or log can absorb that.

## High-level design

Here is the whole system. The rule that holds it together: **the job table in the database is the only truth.**
Everything else (queues in memory, the scheduler's view of who is running what) is a copy that can be rebuilt from
that table.

```
Client
   │  1. POST /jobs  (with an idempotency key)
   ▼
API ── writes the job row and commits it to disk, THEN returns the job ID
   │
   ▼
Job store (database) ── one row per job: state, tier, attempt, fence, lease expiry, output path
   │  2. scheduler reads queued jobs
   ▼
Scheduler ── per-tenant queues, picks the next job by fair share of GPU-seconds,
   │          matches it to a free GPU of a compatible type
   │  3. grants a lease and a new fence number
   ▼
GPU workers ── heartbeat to renew the lease, run the model
   │  4. write the video to object storage under outputs/<job>/attempt-<fence>/
   │  5. commit to the job store ONLY IF the fence is still theirs
   ▼
Notifier ── reads finished jobs from an outbox table, calls the user's webhook (with retries)
```

**API.** It takes `POST /jobs` with an **idempotency key**: a unique string the client makes up for each request
and sends again on every retry, so the server can tell "a retry of the same request" from "a new request". The API
returns a job ID only after the database has saved the row. It also serves `GET /jobs/:id` for status, an optional
webhook, and `DELETE` to cancel.

**Job store.** One row per job, with these columns: `id, tenant, tier, model_version, gpu_type_constraint, state,
attempt, fence, lease_owner, lease_expires_at, output_ref, error, created_at`. A **tenant** is one customer (a user or
an organization). The states run: queued → leased → running → succeeded, failed or dead.

**Scheduler.** It builds one queue per tenant from the store, picks the next job by a weighted fair share of
GPU-seconds, matches it to a free GPU of a type that can run it, and grants a lease. Everything it holds in memory is
a cache.

**Workers.** A worker on each GPU takes a lease, heartbeats to renew it, writes output to a path that includes its
attempt number, then commits.

**Notifier.** It reads finished jobs from an **outbox table** (a table of "messages to send", written in the same
transaction as the state change) and calls webhooks with retries.

## Deep dives

### Accepting without losing anything

"Never lose a request" needs a precise meaning, and you should give it: **once you return a job ID, the job reaches
a final state the user can see.** That final state may be success, a visible failure, or cancelled. Silence is the
only thing not allowed.

Three rules deliver that guarantee:

1. **Acknowledge only after the durable write.** A write is **durable** when it is on disk (or on several machines)
   and would survive a crash. The API commits the row first, then returns the ID. Commit and truth are the same
   thing: there is no second copy that could disagree.
2. **Make retries harmless.** Client retries reuse the idempotency key, so a timeout after the commit doesn't create
   a second job. Further down, each step runs **at least once** (it may run twice after a crash) and is
   **idempotent** (running it twice has the same effect as once). A job may be picked up twice, as in Maya's story,
   but only one completion is saved.
3. **Look for stragglers.** A **reconciler** is a background job that scans for jobs stuck in a non-final state past
   their deadline, and re-queues them or fails them visibly. It is also your lost-work detector: its count is how
   you measure "lost", and you alarm when it is not zero.

**Overload is handled before acceptance, not after.** When the estimated wait is longer than the tier allows, reply
with HTTP 429 (too many requests) or 503 (unavailable) and a hint about when to retry. The wrong move is to accept
the job into memory and drop it later. That is how requests get lost.

**Saving 2,500 jobs a second.** Writing each job to disk on its own would need one **fsync** per job. An fsync is the
call that forces data out of memory onto the disk, and it is the slow part of every durable write. Databases and
logs use **group commit** instead: they collect every write that arrives in a short window (say 5 ms, assumed) and
flush them with a single fsync. Each caller waits at most one window, and the disk sees about 200 flushes a second
instead of 2,500. "The hard part of 5,000 concurrent requests isn't the connections. It's 2,500 durable writes a
second, and group commit is how one database handles that."

### Leases, heartbeats and fencing

A **lease** is a time-limited loan: "worker W may run job J until time T". The worker renews it with heartbeats. If
the lease runs out, the scheduler may give the job to someone else. A message queue such as Amazon SQS calls the
same idea a **visibility timeout**: a message you received is hidden from other consumers until the timeout passes.

A lease alone is not safe. A worker can pause without dying: a long garbage-collection pause, a network split, a
driver call that hangs. It then wakes up after its lease expired, unaware, and writes its output anyway. Now two
workers have both "finished" the job. That is W1 in Maya's story.

Martin Kleppmann's
[2016 post](https://martin.kleppmann.com/2016/02/08/how-to-do-distributed-locking.html) gives the fix. The lock
service hands out a number that increases on every grant, called a **fencing token**. The storage side remembers
the highest number it has seen and rejects writes that carry an older one. His example: token 33 is rejected after
34 has written.

Here the fence is simply a column on the job row, incremented on every lease grant. The worker writes output to
`outputs/J/attempt-<fence>/`, so two attempts never write to the same files. Then it commits with a conditional
update:

```sql
-- the worker's commit; it only succeeds if no one has been granted the job since
UPDATE jobs
SET state = 'succeeded', output_ref = 'outputs/J-1042/attempt-2/'
WHERE id = 'J-1042' AND fence = 2;
-- 1 row updated: we own the result. 0 rows: we were fenced out; stop and clean up.
```

A stale worker's update matches zero rows, and its files are garbage-collected later. Duplicate results become
impossible at the commit, not merely unlikely. "A lease decides who should be working. The fence decides whose
result counts."

**Heartbeats prove the process is alive, not that it is making progress.** A GPU **kernel** (a program running on
the GPU) can hang while the worker process keeps heartbeating happily. Add **progress heartbeats** (frames rendered,
or the model's refinement steps done) and a maximum runtime
per model, so a hung task can't hold a GPU forever.

### Fair queueing with a paid tier

**Count fairness in GPU-seconds, not tasks.** If fairness counts tasks, a user sending 10-minute videos gets the
same "one task" credit as a user sending 10-second clips, but uses sixty times more GPU. Counting GPU-seconds makes
each tenant pay for what they actually use.

The standard tool is **deficit round robin**: go around the tenants in turn; each turn, give each tenant a credit of
GPU-seconds in proportion to its weight; a tenant may start a job when its credit covers the job's estimated cost.
(A "virtual-time" scheduler is a smoother version of the same idea.) Add three guards:

- **A weight per tier**, so the paid tier gets a bigger share.
- **A cap on how many GPUs one tenant can hold at once**, so one huge customer can't take the fleet.
- **No saving up credit while idle**, so a tenant who was quiet all night doesn't return and take over the fleet.

The paid-tier follow-up has three answers. Each has a cost, and naming the cost is the point:

| Option | What paid users get | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| Higher weight (e.g. 4:1) | proportionally more share when the fleet is busy | no hard wait guarantee |
| Reserved capacity | a floor of GPUs that are always theirs | reserved GPUs sit idle when paid demand is low, unless lent to free work and taken back when needed |
| **Preemption** of free tasks (stopping a running free task to make room) | the lowest paid wait | wasted free-tier GPU time, unless tasks checkpoint |

A common combination: weights for the normal case, plus a small reserved pool that free work may borrow under a
lease the scheduler can revoke.

One more GPU-specific problem: each task holds a whole GPU, so long tasks block short ones behind them. Separate the
pools or queues by duration class, or cap task duration and split long videos into segments.

### A model version that fails only on some GPU types

When a task fails, first ask **why**. There are three kinds of cause:

- **Job-caused**: bad input. It will fail anywhere. Retrying is waste.
- **Node-caused**: this particular GPU is unhealthy. Retry somewhere else; count it against the node.
- **Model-by-GPU-type**: the new model version crashes on one GPU type (a driver or kernel difference). It will fail
  on every GPU of that type.

Record failure counts per pair of (model_version, gpu_type). When one pair's failure rate crosses a threshold, open
a **circuit breaker** for it: stop placing that model on that GPU type, route its jobs to other types, and alert.
Retries go to a different GPU, and after a type-specific failure, to a different type.

**Bound the retries** (say 3 attempts). After the last one, the job moves to a **dead-letter** state: a final
"failed, here is why" state the user can see. Without a cap, a **poison job** (one that always fails) loops forever,
burning GPU time.

**Prevent it in the first place with a canary**: roll the new model version out to a small share of traffic on
*each* GPU type separately, compare its failure rate and output-quality checks with the old version, and widen only
when both look healthy. "I canary per GPU type, because a version that's fine on one type can crash on another, and
a fleet-wide canary average would hide it."

### 10,000 tasks in one second

This is the Modal follow-up. Recall that a warm container already has the model loaded in GPU memory and can start
a task at once. A **cold start** is everything a new container must do before its first task, mostly
loading the model weights.

Walk it in order:

1. **Accept in batches.** Append the burst to the durable log in batches. Don't do 10,000 single-row transactions,
   each taking a lock; that makes the database the bottleneck.
2. **Dispatch by pull, not push.** Each warm container leases a batch of tasks (20 at a time means 500 lease grants
   instead of 10,000) and runs them back to back, or batched on the GPU if the model allows. Pulling balances load
   by itself: a fast container simply comes back for more sooner.
3. **Prefer containers that already have the model loaded.** Send only the overflow to containers that would have to
   load it.
4. **Size the warm pool with a control loop**, which is the framing the Modal article suggests: predict demand, hold
   enough warm containers for the latency target, and start cold containers early when the backlog's expected wait
   is longer than a cold start.

From the back-of-envelope, this burst clears on the warm pool in about 20 seconds, and a cold container needs about
20 seconds before its first task. "Starting cold containers for this burst would mostly add cost. They'd be ready
just as the warm pool finishes."

### Cancellation and result delivery

**Cancellation is a state change, not a kill signal.** Cancelling a queued job moves it straight to cancelled in the
store. Cancelling a running job sets a cancel flag. The worker sees it in its next heartbeat reply, stops, and frees
the GPU.

The fence makes this race-free. If the worker finished first, its commit wins, and the cancel is reported as too
late. If the cancel won, the scheduler bumps the fence, and the worker's late commit is rejected. Either way the user
sees exactly one outcome.

**Delivery has the same at-least-once shape as execution.** The final state change and its outbox row are written
in the same transaction. The notifier sends webhooks from the outbox with retries and a signature (so the receiver
can check the call came from you). A crash between "job done" and "webhook sent" therefore re-sends instead of
forgetting. Receivers must tolerate duplicates, so include the job ID and attempt number.

Keep polling available as the fallback, because webhooks fail in ways you don't see. Store outputs in object storage
and hand out a time-limited **signed URL** (a link that works only for a while) instead of streaming large video
files through the API. Delete outputs on a retention schedule the tenant can see.

## Failure modes and overload

Walk these in order of how often they happen. For each, say what breaks and why the design survives it.

- **Worker crash or network split.** The lease expires and the job is granted again with a new fence. The old
  worker's commit fails. (Maya's story.)
- **Hung task.** Heartbeats continue but progress doesn't. The progress watchdog or the maximum runtime kills it.
- **Scheduler crash.** It holds no truth. A replacement rebuilds the queues from the store. Leases already granted
  continue and commit normally. Run two schedulers, with a leader chosen by a lease and fenced the same way, so two
  schedulers never both think they are in charge.
- **Job-store outage.** Stop accepting and return 503. Accepting into memory is how requests get lost.
- **Object-store errors.** The worker retries the upload. The commit happens only after the output exists.
- **Retry storms.** When many clients or workers retry at once, they can knock over a recovering service. Use
  exponential backoff (wait longer after each failure) with jitter (a random spread so retries don't line up), and a
  retry budget per model version.
- **Overload.** Admission control on estimated wait per tier. Turn away or delay free work first.
- **A bad GPU.** Node-caused failures feed a per-node health score. Drain the node (stop sending it work) after
  repeated failures across different models.

## Evals and observability

You prove the system works with a handful of numbers. Each one answers a question someone will ask you:

- **"Are paid users getting what they paid for?"** Queue wait p50 and p95 per tier, against the target, and how far
  off the wait estimate shown to users was.
- **"Is it fair?"** Each tenant's and tier's actual share of GPU-seconds, against their weights.
- **"Is failure handling working?"** Lease expiries, fence rejections, retries per job, dead-letter count. Fence
  rejections should be rare but not zero; zero may mean fencing isn't wired up at all.
- **"Is a model broken on some hardware?"** Failure rate per (model_version, gpu_type), and which breakers are open.
- **"Are we wasting money?"** GPU busy fraction, warm-pool size, cold starts per minute.
- **"Did we lose anything?"** The reconciler's count of jobs past their deadline in a non-final state. This is your
  definition of lost work, measured.
- **"Is the new model any good?"** Output quality checks per model version on the canary, not just crash rates.

## Trade-offs

| Decision | You gain | You give up | Revisit when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Database as the queue | one source of truth, transactions | a ceiling on how fast you can hand out jobs | job dispatch shows up in database CPU or lock waits |
| Fairness in GPU-seconds | long jobs can't crowd out short ones | needs duration estimates | durations are nearly uniform |
| Pull with batch leases | natural load balancing, fewer grants | a batch held by a crashed worker waits for the TTL | tasks are long; then lease one at a time |
| Short TTL | fast detection | more heartbeat traffic, false expiries on slow networks | expiries without crashes appear |
| No mid-task checkpoints | simpler workers | a crash wastes the whole attempt | tasks grow past several minutes |

<AINative title="Where an agent helps and what you still check">
An agent can write the jobs table, the lease and heartbeat code, the scheduler and the retry wiring in an afternoon.
It is just as likely to write a completion update without the fence condition, or a retry loop with no cap. What you
check: a test that pauses a worker past its lease, lets another worker finish, then resumes the first and asserts its
commit is rejected; a test that kills the scheduler mid-dispatch and asserts every accepted job still finishes; and a
load test that bursts past capacity and asserts the API rejects instead of accepting and dropping.
</AINative>

## Small-team version

This family matters to small teams. Any company running image, video, transcription or batch LLM (large language model) jobs on a handful
of GPUs needs exactly this queue, and the good news is that most of it is a database table you already know how to
run.

### Build

**A Postgres `jobs` table is the queue.** Workers claim a job with `SELECT … FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED`. `FOR UPDATE`
locks the row you picked; `SKIP LOCKED` makes other workers skip rows that are already locked instead of waiting for
them. The [Postgres docs](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/sql-select.html) describe SKIP LOCKED as a way "to
avoid lock contention with multiple consumers accessing a queue-like table". Add `lease_expires_at`, `attempt` and
`fence` columns and the conditional completion update shown in the fencing deep dive.

A sketch of the claim, in the same transaction:

```sql
-- claim one queued (or expired) job, bump its fence, and take a 30 s lease
UPDATE jobs
SET state = 'leased', attempt = attempt + 1, fence = fence + 1,
    lease_owner = 'worker-7', lease_expires_at = now() + interval '30 seconds'
WHERE id = (
  SELECT id FROM jobs
  WHERE state = 'queued' OR (state IN ('leased', 'running') AND lease_expires_at < now())
  ORDER BY (tier = 'paid') DESC, created_at   -- paid first, then oldest
  FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED
  LIMIT 1
)
RETURNING id, fence;
```

A Redis queue also works. StoryGraph, a two-person team, is reported to run about a dozen self-hosted models on
Hetzner (a German hosting provider) GPU servers behind a Redis queue
([ZenML summary](https://www.zenml.io/llmops-database/scaling-llm-and-ml-models-to-300m-monthly-requests-with-self-hosting)).

If you use a managed queue, its lease is the visibility timeout. Amazon SQS sets it to 30 seconds by default, lets
you extend it while you work, caps it at 12 hours, and may still deliver a message more than once
([SQS docs](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AWSSimpleQueueService/latest/SQSDeveloperGuide/sqs-visibility-timeout.html)).
So you still need the idempotent, fenced completion.

### Buy

**Rent GPUs by the second, at least at first.** "Serverless" GPU providers bill only for the seconds a GPU is busy.
Dedicated GPUs bill every hour whether busy or idle, but cost less per hour.

A worked example with list prices fetched 2026-10-11: 20,000 jobs a day at an
assumed 30 GPU-seconds each is **166.7 GPU-hours a day**. On RunPod's serverless L40S (a mid-range data-center GPU)
at $1.75 an hour, that is **about $292 a day, or $8,750 per 30 days**, paid only for busy seconds.

When do dedicated GPUs win? When they would be busy enough. RunPod's dedicated L40S costs $0.79 an hour (community)
or $1.09 (secure). Dedicated is cheaper once the pool is busy more than **45% (community) or 62% (secure)** of the
time.

Average demand here is about **7 busy GPUs**. But you must size a dedicated pool for the peak, not the average. If
peaks are 3× the average (assumed), a pool sized for the peak is busy only about **33%** of the time, below break-even,
so per-second billing wins. "I'd rent by the second until steady load would keep a dedicated pool more than about
half busy." The common middle path: a few dedicated GPUs for the steady base load, plus per-second capacity for
peaks.

  $292 a day; × 30 ≈ $8,750.
- **Average busy GPUs:** 166.7 ÷ 24 ≈ 6.9.

### Skip

Multi-region schedulers, preemption, and elaborate fair-share math. Start with a cap on concurrent jobs per tenant
and a queue that serves paid jobs first, with an age boost so free jobs still finish.

### Still own

Idempotency keys, the fence check, bounded retries with a visible dead-letter state, per-tenant cost caps, and the
reconciler that proves nothing accepted was lost.

### Revisit when

Dispatch load shows in database metrics, a dedicated pool would be busy past break-even, tenants complain about
fairness, or tasks get long enough that mid-task checkpoints pay for themselves.

## Follow-ups

<FollowUps>
  <FollowUp q="A paid tier must get results faster. Weights, reservation or preemption? (Listed on the PracHub page, OpenAI)">
    Weights give more share but no guarantee; reservation gives a floor but can sit idle; preemption gives the lowest
    wait but wastes free-tier work. Combine weights with a small reserved pool lent out under revocable leases.
  </FollowUp>
  <FollowUp q="A new model version fails on some GPU types. How do you contain it? (Listed on the PracHub page, OpenAI)">
    Track failures per model version and GPU type, open a breaker on the bad pair, route to other types, cap retries,
    and canary each version per GPU type next time.
  </FollowUp>
  <FollowUp q="How do you show users an honest wait estimate? (Listed on the PracHub page, OpenAI)">
    GPU-seconds of work ahead of them in their tier divided by the GPU-seconds per second the tier is receiving, shown
    as a range, and measure how wrong the estimate was.
  </FollowUp>
  <FollowUp q="Ten thousand short tasks land in one second. How are they placed? (Modal prep guide)">
    Accept them into a durable log in batches, dispatch by pull with batch leases to warm containers that have the
    model loaded, and start cold containers only when the expected wait is longer than a cold start.
  </FollowUp>
  <FollowUp q="How do you size the warm pool? (Modal prep guide)">
    As a control loop: predict demand, hold enough warm instances for the latency target, shrink when traffic falls.
    Keeping everything on is the answer the guide calls weak.
  </FollowUp>
  <FollowUp q="The scheduler itself crashes. What is lost? (Listed on the PracHub page, OpenAI)">
    Nothing, if the job store is the truth: a new leader rebuilds queues from it, in-flight leases keep running, and
    commits still check the fence.
  </FollowUp>
</FollowUps>

## Common mistakes

- **Acknowledging before the durable write.** A crash between "OK" and the write loses a job you promised to run.
- **Buffering accepted jobs in memory during a database outage.** A restart drops them all; return 503 instead.
- **Leases without fencing.** A worker that paused past its lease can still overwrite the real result.
- **Fairness by task count.** Users with long jobs get far more GPU than users with short ones.
- **Unbounded retries.** A poison job, or a model that crashes on one GPU type, burns GPUs forever.
- **Treating heartbeats as progress.** A hung kernel still heartbeats.
- **No admission control.** At 90% utilization a 10-minute burst becomes about two hours of queueing (see the
  back-of-envelope).

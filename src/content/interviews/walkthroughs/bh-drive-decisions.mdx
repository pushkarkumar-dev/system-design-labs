## The prompt

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "I drove the decision to put all our AI model calls behind one shared gateway, instead of letting each team add
> its own retries. The team that owned our main user-facing path pushed back, and their worry was fair: a new hop
> on their most important request. We settled it with a two-week trial where the gateway only watched copies of
> real traffic, and a pass mark we both agreed before it started. It passed. Outages caused by our model provider
> dropped from about six a quarter to one, and one team still hasn't moved its batch jobs. Trial-first is now how
> we test infrastructure changes."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer, so you can build the same shape from your own work.
> (That story, and every number in it, is invented to show the level of detail. Use your own.)

The core question in this family is short: "Tell me about a technical decision you drove and how you got people
on board." The interviewer can then move you to any of five related questions with one follow-up. How did you
change the mind of a peer who disagreed? How did you tell an executive their date wouldn't work? When did you
turn customer complaints into a real product change? When were you confident and wrong? Which failure of yours
hit a customer?

So prepare them as one set of stories, not six speeches.

### Words you need first

- A **behavioral round** is an interview made of "tell me about a time" questions. You answer with stories from
  your own work, and the interviewer scores what the stories show about how you behave.
- A **hiring-manager round** is a behavioral round run by the person you would report to. A **values round**
  is one that asks how you reason about honesty, safety and hard trade-offs. Both appear in this family.
- A **forward-deployed engineer** (**FDE**) is an engineer who works inside a customer's organization to get
  the company's product (here, an AI model) working for that customer. Several of the reports below come from
  FDE loops, so the "other side" in their stories is often a customer.
- An **eval set** is a fixed list of real questions with known good answers, used to score an AI feature the
  way a test suite scores code. It comes up in almost every AI-era decision story, because it is how you turn
  "is it ready?" into a number.

The example story at the top uses three more terms. A **model provider** is the company whose AI model you call
over a web API, such as Anthropic or OpenAI. A **gateway**, here, is one internal service that every other service
calls instead of calling the provider directly, so retries and spending limits live in one place. A **shadow
trial** sends a copy of real traffic to a new system: it does all its work, but its results are thrown away, so
users are never affected while you measure it.

### What makes this different from the behavioral rounds you already know

You have probably done behavioral rounds before. At most companies they are a separate, softer part of the
loop: a manager listens for teamwork and conflict handling, and a clean, rehearsed story does fine.

At the AI labs and AI infrastructure companies in this family, three things change:

1. **It is partly a technical round.** The interviewer wants the mechanics of the decision: the architecture
   before and after, the numbers, what could break. A leadership story with no system in it gives them little to
   score.
2. **They hunt for your part.** Expect repeated questions about what *you* did, how big the team was, and who
   decided. A story told entirely in "we" leaves them nothing to score.
3. **The round is short and the follow-ups carry the signal.** If your story takes eight minutes to reach the
   decision, there is no time left for the follow-ups, and the follow-ups are where they decide.

## What is being tested

Strip the wording away and every prompt here asks the same three things:

- Did you, personally, make a call that mattered?
- Did you bring other people with you, rather than going around them?
- When the call or the system went wrong, did you own it and change something?

Here is what the reports say interviewers actually push on. Each line says who reported it, because a
candidate's first-hand account counts for more than a prep site's advice.

- **Your own part, in technical detail.** An Anthropic Senior/Staff inference candidate says the hiring manager
  "explicitly told me she cared about technical detail, not just generic leadership stories" and "kept drilling
  on what I personally did, how big the team was, how I got buy-in on a technical decision". Follow-ups included
  "How did you make sure everyone was aligned?" and "How do you receive feedback and resolve conflict?"
  (<Cite id="s-4e04a1689c" />).
- **Getting to the point.** The same candidate writes "I know I was too wordy and slower to land the point" in
  that round. There was no offer, but the candidate doesn't name this as the reason. Treat it as their hindsight,
  not as feedback from the company (<Cite id="s-4e04a1689c" />).
- **How you framed a hard conversation.** In an OpenAI FDE hiring-manager round, when the candidate mentioned
  telling an executive their timeline was unrealistic, "the interviewer leaned in and asked how exactly I framed
  that conversation." The candidate's summary: "Communication skills are not a nice-to-have for this role.
  They're the job." (<Cite id="s-812dbc9385" />).
- **A pattern, not a patch.** A Cohere FDE candidate reports the VP round wanted you to "identify recurring
  customer pain, separate local symptoms from product gaps", that "They're not looking for stories about
  workarounds", and that the interviewer asked "how did I get buy-in? How did I prioritize it against other work?
  What was the outcome?" (<Cite id="s-88b41bdbd6" />).
- **Fairness to the other side.** Hello Interview's OpenAI prep guide says explaining "why a disagreement was
  reasonable on both sides says more about your judgment" than a story where others came round. For Staff it asks
  for examples where you "influenced a decision, handled disagreement, or helped other engineers take on work they
  couldn't have owned before" (<Cite id="s-4d55d9b987" />).
- **Real stakes.** A Modal prep guide says strong answers are "specific and a little uncomfortable" and that "Real
  ownership has scar tissue, and they're looking for it." It cites no candidates, so read it as advice
  (<Cite id="s-86890a1069" />).
- **Learning, not a disguised win.** Aced's Anthropic-tagged list advises showing how you debugged and what you
  learned for the "confident and wrong" question, and keeping "honest, specific examples ready, not rehearsed
  mission-speak" for safety-focused companies (<Cite id="s-d7a8e88bf0" />).

Two things follow from that list.

First, at these companies the behavioral round is partly a technical round. Say it to yourself like this: "They
want the decision's mechanics, not a leadership parable."

Second, the round is short. Your job in the first 90 seconds is to give the interviewer a decision they can ask
about. Everything else waits for their questions.

## What a strong story contains

A strong story in this family has seven parts. When a story sounds rehearsed or thin, it is almost always
because one of these is missing.

The table follows the gateway story from the top of the page through all seven parts. Read the middle column as
"what the story says" and the right column as "the sentence you would actually speak". (The story and its
numbers are invented to show the level of detail.)

| Part | In the gateway story | What to say in the interview |
|---|---|---|
| **1. A decision with a real alternative** | Shared gateway vs. each team adding retries vs. buying a vendor gateway. | "The cheapest option was for each team to add its own retries, and a sensible person would have picked it." |
| **2. Stakes in one sentence** | About six outages a quarter traced to provider errors, and no one could say what the company spent on model calls. | "We were losing about six incidents a quarter to one provider's errors." |
| **3. Your part, apart from the team's** | I wrote the proposal and ran the trial; two engineers built it; the platform team reviewed it. | "I wrote the proposal and ran the trial. Two engineers built the gateway with me." |
| **4. The people who disagreed, described fairly** | The team that owned the main user-facing path feared a new hop adding delay and a new single point of failure. | "Their worry was right in principle: they'd had an outage from a new proxy the year before." |
| **5. How agreement actually happened** | A two-week shadow trial with an exit test agreed in advance. | "We agreed the pass mark before we saw any data, so the result settled it." |
| **6. Outcome with a number, and a cost** | Six outages a quarter fell to one. It took most of my quarter, and one team's batch jobs still bypass it. | "It worked, and it cost more than I planned." |
| **7. What changed afterwards** | Every new model integration starts on the gateway. Trial-first became the team's way to test infra changes. | "The trial pattern outlived the project." |

Now each part in plain words.

### 1. A decision with a real alternative

"We moved to Kubernetes" is not a decision story unless staying put, or a different move, was a reasonable option
someone argued for. Name the option you rejected and why a sensible person would have picked it. If you can't, the
story is about execution, not judgment, and it belongs to a different question.

### 2. Stakes you can state in one sentence

Stakes are what would have gone wrong if the team chose badly: cost, delay for users, a customer contract, an
on-call burden, a launch date. Put a number on it if you have one: "the batch job missed its window twice a week"
or "the integration was 40% of the support queue". Use your real figures, and say when a figure is your estimate.

### 3. Your part, separated from the team's

Interviewers are reported to drill on "what I personally did" and "how big the team was"
(<Cite id="s-4e04a1689c" />). Say "I" for what you did and "we" for what the team did, and keep the two apart.

A Staff story usually includes something you made possible but did not do yourself. That might be the design
review you ran, the document that let another team decide, or the engineer you set up to own the follow-on work.

### 4. The people who disagreed, described fairly

Every buy-in story has someone who said no. **Buy-in** means the people who must live with a decision actually
agree to it and act on it, not just that they stopped objecting.

Describe the other person's position in the strongest form you can: what they were protecting and why that was
reasonable. This is where the Hello Interview advice about a disagreement "reasonable on both sides" applies
(<Cite id="s-4d55d9b987" />). A story where the other side was simply wrong tells the interviewer you don't model
other people well.

### 5. How agreement actually happened

Interviewers call this the mechanism of persuasion. "I explained it clearly and they agreed" is not a mechanism.
The credible ones are:

- **A written proposal** with the alternatives laid out, so people argue about options rather than about you.
- **A small experiment or prototype** whose result settles the question.
- **A reversible first step with a rollback trigger**: a number agreed in advance that, if crossed, means you undo
  the change.
- **A change of scope** that meets the objection.
- **Escalation with both positions written down**, when the disagreement really belonged to someone above you.

In the gateway story the mechanism is the shadow trial. Because users never see the gateway's results, the
cautious team loses nothing by agreeing to it, and both sides get real numbers to decide on.

### 6. An outcome with a number, and a cost

Say what happened, how you measured it, and what it cost you. Strong stories include the downside: the migration
took twice as long, one team never adopted it, the experiment showed a smaller win than you expected. A clean
story with no cost sounds polished rather than true. The Modal prep advice puts this bluntly with "scar tissue"
(<Cite id="s-86890a1069" />).

### 7. What you changed afterwards

For the failure and "wrong" stories, this is the point of the story. Name a changed process, a new check, a
different default, or a habit. It should be specific enough that a colleague could confirm it: "every schema change
now ships behind a dual-write with a read comparison", not "I communicate more now".

## Story structure

The usual structure in prep material is **STAR**: situation, task, action, result. Aced uses it, for example
(<Cite id="s-d7a8e88bf0" />). It works, but it puts the decision late, after the situation and the task. A
technical interviewer can spend two minutes waiting to hear what you actually decided.

For this family, use a structure that lands the decision in the first sentence and leaves time for follow-ups.

### The shape of a good answer

```
Interviewer: "Tell me about a decision you drove."
   │
   ▼
Headline ─────────── what you decided, against what, and the result   (1 sentence)
   │
   ▼
Stakes ───────────── what was at risk, and what limited the options   (2 sentences)
   │
   ▼
Alternatives ─────── the other options, and who wanted each one      (2–3 sentences)
   │
   ▼
The call ─────────── the evidence or principle that decided it        (2 sentences)
   │
   ▼
The people part ──── who disagreed, what they protected, how you agreed (2–3 sentences)
   │
   ▼
Outcome and cost ─── the number, and what went worse than planned     (2 sentences)
   │
   ▼
What changed ─────── the habit or process that outlived the project   (1 sentence)
   │
   ▼
STOP ─────────────── let the interviewer choose the next question
   │
   ├── Technical layer      (kept in reserve)
   ├── People layer         (kept in reserve)
   └── Counterfactual layer (kept in reserve)
```

**Headline.** One sentence that contains the decision, the rejected alternative and the result. If the
interviewer stopped you here, they would still know what the story is about.

**Stakes.** Two sentences on what was at risk and what limited your options. A number helps; a vague "it was
important" does not.

**Alternatives.** Two or three real options, and who preferred each. This is where you show it was a judgment,
not a task.

**The call.** The evidence or principle that decided it. "The trial showed 8 ms of added delay against a limit
of 20 ms" is evidence. "It felt cleaner" is not.

**The people part.** Who disagreed, what they were protecting, and how agreement happened. This is the part the
reported interviewers keep returning to.

**Outcome and cost.** The before-and-after number, and one thing that went worse than you planned.

**What changed.** One sentence on what outlived the project.

**Stop.** Then let the interviewer choose the next question. That stop is deliberate. The Anthropic candidate's
"too wordy and slower to land the point" (<Cite id="s-4e04a1689c" />) is exactly the failure this shape prevents.
If the interviewer wants detail, they will ask, and the detail they ask for tells you what they are scoring.

### The gateway story, against a running clock

Here is the opening of the gateway story as you would say it, with a rough clock. The whole thing is about
**90 seconds** of speech (worked out in the box below). Every number in the story is invented.

Two terms in the script, for readers who haven't met them:

- A **fallback provider** is a second AI company's API the gateway can switch to when the first one fails.
- The **99th percentile** (written **p99**) of added delay is the delay that 99 of every 100 requests stay under.
  You already use p99 for latency; here it measures what the new hop costs.

| Clock | Part | What you say |
|---|---|---|
| 0–10 s | **Headline** | "I drove the decision to put all our model calls behind one shared gateway, instead of each team adding its own retries. Two product teams adopted it within a quarter." |
| 10–25 s | **Stakes** | "We had about six incidents a quarter caused by our model provider's errors, and nobody could tell finance what we spent on model calls." |
| 25–40 s | **Alternatives** | "Each team could add its own retries, which was cheapest and needed no coordination. We could buy a vendor gateway, but it would send our customers' questions through another company. Or we could build a thin internal one, which is what I proposed." |
| 40–50 s | **The call** | "I picked the internal one because it gave us one place for retries, a spending budget and a fallback provider, without new data-handling approvals." |
| 50–70 s | **The people part** | "The team that owned our main user path disagreed. A new hop on their most important request could add delay and become a single point of failure, and they'd been burned by a proxy before. So we agreed a two-week shadow trial, with a pass mark set first: added delay under 20 ms at the 99th percentile." |
| 70–85 s | **Outcome and cost** | "It came in at 8 ms. Provider-caused incidents went from about six a quarter to one. It took most of my quarter, and one team's batch jobs still bypass it." |
| 85–90 s | **What changed** | "Every new model integration now starts on the gateway, and shadow trials became how we test infra changes." |

Then stop. The interviewer now has six things to ask about, and each one is a door you have prepared.

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the numbers (for a Staff-level follow-up)</summary>

- **90 seconds of speech.** The script above is about 225 words. At an assumed speaking pace of about 150 words a
  minute, 225 ÷ 150 = 1.5 minutes, so about **90 seconds**.
- **Why 90 seconds and not five minutes.** The family's rounds are about 45 minutes. Assume about 10 minutes go to
  introductions and your questions at the end, leaving about 35 minutes. If each story takes 1.5 minutes to open
  and about 8–10 minutes of follow-ups, you fit three stories. A five-minute opening leaves room for two.
- **The gateway story's outcome.** About six provider-caused incidents a quarter falling to one is a drop of five,
  or about 83%. In a real story, say which you measured: the count, or the minutes of user impact.

</details>

### Layers you keep in reserve

Prepare three deeper layers for each story, and don't volunteer them. Each one answers a predictable follow-up.

- **Technical layer.** The architecture before and after, the numbers you measured, and the failure modes you
  designed for. For the gateway story: how the gateway handled a provider timeout, what happened when the gateway
  itself was down (the services fell back to calling the provider directly), and how the spending budget was
  enforced. At AI and infra companies, interviewers are reported to want this even in a hiring-manager round
  (<Cite id="s-4e04a1689c" />).
- **People layer.** The exact words you used in the hard conversation. The OpenAI interviewer asked "how exactly I
  framed that conversation" (<Cite id="s-812dbc9385" />). Have the framing ready almost word for word. For the
  gateway story: "I said: your worry is right, so let's not argue about it. Let's agree what number would make it
  safe, and measure it on your traffic."
- **Counterfactual layer.** What you would do differently, what would have happened with the other option, and
  what evidence would have changed your mind at the time. For the gateway story: "If the trial had shown more than
  20 ms, I'd have dropped the shared gateway for that path and let that team keep its own retries."

### One story, several questions

You don't need six stories. You need three or four that each answer two or more prompts, because the follow-ups
will move you between prompts anyway.

| Story type | Answers | Must include |
|---|---|---|
| A cross-team technical decision | infra-105, eng-113 | A peer's fair objection and the mechanism that resolved it |
| A hard conversation upward | eng-110, infra-105 | The exact framing and the options you offered |
| A customer pattern turned into product | eng-114, eng-110 | Several customers, a durable fix, prioritization against other work |
| A call that went wrong | eng-107, infra-111 | The signal you missed, the blast radius, the changed process |

The **blast radius** in the last row is how much of the system, or how many customers, a failure reached.

Say it like this when an interviewer jumps prompts: "That's the same project. The part you're asking about now is
the disagreement with the platform lead, so let me pick it up there."

## Example story outlines

These are illustrative outlines, not real stories. They show the shape and the level of detail. Every bracket is
a gap you fill with your own facts. If you don't have a fact for a bracket, pick a different story rather than
inventing one.

Each outline matches one of the answer cards for this family, and uses the same example as that card.

### Outline A: a technical decision you drove (infra-105, eng-113)

*Illustrative outline.* This is the gateway story from the top of the page, with the invented numbers replaced by
brackets.

- **Headline.** "I proposed moving [service]'s model calls from [direct provider SDK calls in each service] to
  [a shared gateway with retries, budgets and a fallback provider], and got [the two product teams] to adopt it
  within [time]." (An **SDK** is the provider's client library that each service calls directly.)
- **Stakes.** "[Number] outages a quarter traced to provider errors; [cost figure] of untracked spend."
- **Alternatives.** "Each team adds its own retries (cheapest, no coordination). Buy a vendor gateway (fast, but
  [data-handling concern]). Build a thin internal one (my choice)."
- **The objection, fairly.** "[Peer]'s team owned the main user-facing path. Their concern was a new hop adding
  latency and a new single point of failure on the most important request. That was reasonable: [their past
  incident]."
- **Mechanism.** "I wrote a two-page proposal with the three options and their failure modes. We agreed a
  two-week shadow mode: the gateway sees a copy of traffic and doesn't serve it. The agreed exit criterion was
  [p99 added latency under X ms]. It came in at [Y]."
- **Outcome and cost.** "[Metric before → after]. It cost [time] of my quarter, and [team] never moved their batch
  jobs, which still bypass it."
- **What changed.** "Every new model integration now starts on the gateway; the shadow-mode pattern became how we
  trial infra changes."

What makes it Staff: the shadow mode turned a disagreement about opinions into a test both sides accepted, and the
story says what didn't get adopted.

The peer version of this story (eng-113) often turns on a different choice. A common one is whether to
**fine-tune** a small model (train it further on your own examples) or keep a hosted model and add **retrieval**
(searching your own documents for relevant passages and pasting them into the model's input). The eng-113 card
walks through that example.

### Outline B: telling an executive a timeline won't work (eng-110)

*Illustrative outline.*

- **Headline.** "A [VP / customer executive] committed to [launching an AI assistant to all customers by date]. I
  told them that date would ship something we couldn't stand behind, and we launched a narrower version on time."
- **The evidence.** "On our [N]-example eval set, the assistant answered [x of N] correctly; the failure cases were
  [account-specific questions where retrieval found the wrong customer's policy]."
- **The framing, close to word for word.** "I said: here is what will be true on [date] if we ship as planned, here
  are three examples of what customers will see, and here are two options. Option one: same date, scope cut to [the
  topics that pass]. Option two: full scope, [N] weeks later, gated on [the eval passing threshold]. I recommend
  option one."
- **What made it land.** "Showing failures the executive could read in a minute rather than a percentage; giving
  options, not a refusal; and tying the gate to a number they could track weekly."
- **Outcome and cost.** "[What shipped, adoption, what the executive said]. The cost: [a sales commitment had to be
  walked back]."
- **What changed.** "New AI features now get a one-page 'what it can't do yet' note before a date is set."

A **gate** here is a condition that must be true before something ships, such as "the assistant passes 90% of the
eval set". It turns "is it ready?" from an argument into a number the executive can watch.

This is the shape the OpenAI FDE interviewer reportedly pushed on: the framing, not just the fact that you said no
(<Cite id="s-812dbc9385" />).

### Outline C: customer feedback into product (eng-114)

*Illustrative outline.* The eng-114 card uses the example of an AI assistant that kept answering from the wrong
version of a product's documentation.

- **Pattern.** "Over [period], [N] customers raised [the same failure], each filed as a separate ticket. I pulled
  [the tickets / traces] and found they shared [one cause]." (A **trace** is the recorded path of one request
  through the system, including what the AI was given and what it answered.)
- **Symptom versus gap.** "The local fix was [a per-customer config override]. The product gap was [the system had
  no concept of X]."
- **Buy-in and priority.** "The product team's roadmap was full. I brought [the ticket count, the support hours, the
  renewal at risk] and proposed [a scoped fix] that displaced [a specific item]."
- **Outcome.** "[Tickets per month before → after]; [the workaround count removed]."

This matches what the Cohere VP round reportedly wanted: recurring pain, not a workaround, plus buy-in and priority
(<Cite id="s-88b41bdbd6" />).

### Outline D: confident and wrong (eng-107)

*Illustrative outline.* The eng-107 card uses the example of a **reranker** (a second model that re-sorts search
results before the AI reads them) that was expected to fix answer quality and didn't.

- **The belief.** "I was sure [a cache / an index / a model] would fix [latency or quality problem], because [the
  reasoning]."
- **The evidence you had and the evidence you skipped.** "I tested [on synthetic data / on one tenant]; I didn't
  test [the real distribution]."
- **How you found out.** "[A metric, a customer, a colleague] showed [the result]."
- **What you did next.** "Rolled back within [time], told [stakeholders] it was my call, and [measured properly]."
- **The change in you.** "I now [write down the result I expect before running an experiment], and [colleague]
  reviews any change where I'm the only person who believes it."

The reflective part matters most in a values round. One Anthropic candidate describes an interviewer who "kept
pushing on how I felt then and how I feel now" and seemed to want them "to name the discomfort instead of
rationalizing it away" (<Cite id="s-42f1c7f6dd" />). Have an honest answer to "how did that feel?"

### Outline E: an infrastructure failure you owned (infra-111)

*Illustrative outline.* The infra-111 card uses the example of a new model-loading path rolled out to every worker
at once.

- **Incident.** "[Date-range], a change I made to [component] caused [symptom] for [N customers / X% of requests]
  for [duration]."
- **Detection and response.** "We noticed via [alert or customer]; I [mitigated by]. Time to mitigate: [time]."
- **Ownership.** "I wrote the incident review and said the change was mine; the review was blameless in format, but
  I didn't hide behind that." (A **blameless** review focuses on the system that allowed a mistake, not on
  punishing the person who made it.)
- **Root cause beyond the trigger.** "The trigger was [my change]. The cause was [no staged rollout for config
  changes]."
- **What changed.** "[A concrete control: staged config rollout, an alert on the leading indicator, a load test in
  CI]."
- **The bold-decision half.** Modal's prep guide pairs this with "a bold systems decision you made that could have
  failed badly" (<Cite id="s-86890a1069" />). Use a decision where you stated the downside in advance, set a
  rollback trigger, and accepted the risk on purpose.

## Small-team version

This family matters more, not less, when you are the AI owner at a 50–500 person company. Hiring managers at that
size write ownership into the job. Some postings say it outright: "You will be the only engineer on this platform"
([Supabase AI Platform Engineer posting, 2026-07-31](https://api.ashbyhq.com/posting-api/job-board/supabase)).
Small-company loops also tend to put product managers and founders in the room: Elicit's hiring manager describes a
coding exercise where the interviewer plays PM ([Latent Space, 2024-06-21](https://www.latent.space/p/hiring)).
That means your decision stories will be heard by people who will rely on you making these calls alone.

### What changes when there is no committee

At a large company a decision gets reviewed by a design committee, a platform team and a security team. At a small
one you are often all three. That changes what a good story shows:

- **A written decision record you made for yourself.** One page: the options, the choice, the evidence, and the
  number that would prove you wrong. Nobody asked you to write it. That's the point. It takes about an hour.
- **A reversal condition agreed in advance.** "If cost per resolved ticket goes above [X] for two weeks, we turn
  the assistant off for that queue." Small teams can't afford a long argument about money already spent.
- **Buy-in from non-engineers.** Your hardest buy-in is often from the CEO, sales or the support lead, not a peer
  engineer. Tell those stories with the same care as the technical ones.

Say it like this: "There was no review board, so I wrote the decision down for myself, with the number that would
make me reverse it."

### Explaining AI limits to executives

The executive-timeline story (eng-110) is the most common real situation for a small-team AI owner. The pattern
repeats: an executive sees a demo, sets a date, and the eval set says the feature isn't ready.

Public incidents give you the vocabulary for why that matters. In April 2025 Cursor's AI support agent invented a
login policy and users cancelled
([Fortune, 2025-04](https://fortune.com/article/customer-support-ai-cursor-went-rogue)). In February 2024 a tribunal
held Air Canada responsible for its chatbot's wrong fare advice
([Law360 Canada, 2024-02](https://www.law360.ca/ca/articles/1804075)). In both cases the company, not the model
vendor, owned what the bot said.

Use incidents like these to explain risk in terms an executive already cares about, then offer options with a
gate, as in Outline B. Say it like this: "If it gets this wrong in front of a customer, we own the answer, not the
model vendor."

### Owning incidents with no SRE team

At a large company a site reliability engineering (**SRE**) team runs on-call and incident response. At a small one,
your AI feature's incidents are yours end to end: the provider outage, the bad change to the instructions you send
the model, the silent drop in answer quality.

The quality drop is the hardest, because nothing errors. Anthropic's own postmortem of three 2025 infrastructure bugs
says standard benchmarks, safety evals, spot checks and canary deployments did not catch the quality degradation
([Anthropic, 2025-09-17](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/a-postmortem-of-three-recent-issues)). (A **canary
deployment** sends a new version to a small slice of traffic first and watches for problems.) A strong small-team
failure story includes the monitoring you added afterwards: regularly sampling and grading real answers, not just
watching error rates.

### What you can skip

You don't need a story about aligning twelve teams. You do need stories where the scope was the whole company's use
of AI, or a whole customer relationship, even if few people were involved. Scope at a small company is measured by
consequence, not headcount.

### Revisit when

If you're interviewing at a frontier lab or a company with thousands of engineers, swap in at least one story where
you influenced people outside your reporting line across several teams. The OpenAI Staff guidance asks for exactly
that (<Cite id="s-4d55d9b987" />).

## Senior vs Staff

<LevelContrast
  title="Driving a decision"
  senior={[
    "Owns a decision inside one system or team, with a clear alternative and a measured result.",
    "Describes a peer's objection and resolves it with data or a prototype.",
    "Owns an incident: mitigation, root cause, a fix that prevents recurrence.",
  ]}
  staff={[
    "Owns a decision whose consequences cross teams or customers, and states the reversal condition set in advance.",
    "Shows the disagreement was reasonable on both sides, and changed their own plan in response to it.",
    "Changes the system that let the incident happen (rollout policy, review gate, default) and helps someone else own the follow-on work.",
  ]}
/>

The difference is less about story size than about where the leverage sits. A Senior story shows you made a good
call. A Staff story shows you changed how calls get made: the template, the experiment pattern, the gate, or the
person you set up to make the next one.

In the gateway story, the Senior version ends at "it came in at 8 ms and incidents dropped". The Staff version adds
that shadow trials became the team's default, and that the user-path team's objection shaped the design (the
services fall back to calling the provider directly if the gateway is down). The OpenAI guide's phrase "helped other
engineers take on work they couldn't have owned before" is a Staff marker (<Cite id="s-4d55d9b987" />).

## Follow-ups

<FollowUps>
<FollowUp q="How did you make sure everyone was aligned? (Reported, Anthropic)">
Name the document and the moment: the written proposal, who reviewed it, the meeting where the decision was
recorded, and how you checked a month later that the teams were actually doing what was agreed. Alignment you
didn't check is just a meeting (<Cite id="s-4e04a1689c" />).
</FollowUp>
<FollowUp q="How do you receive feedback and resolve conflict? (Reported, Anthropic)">
Give a specific time feedback changed your plan, and what you changed. Then one conflict you resolved by changing
the question, for example by turning an argument about opinions into an agreed experiment
(<Cite id="s-4e04a1689c" />).
</FollowUp>
<FollowUp q="How exactly did you frame that conversation with the executive? (Reported, OpenAI)">
Have the words ready: the risk shown as examples, two or three options, your recommendation, and the measure you
would report weekly. Say what the executive pushed back on and what you conceded (<Cite id="s-812dbc9385" />).
</FollowUp>
<FollowUp q="How did you get buy-in, how did you prioritize it against other work, and what was the outcome? (Reported, Cohere)">
Name what got displaced. Prioritization without a cost isn't prioritization. Give the before-and-after number and
who else had to agree (<Cite id="s-88b41bdbd6" />).
</FollowUp>
<FollowUp q="What did the person who disagreed with you get right? (Not reported; a natural next push)">
This tests whether your fairness was real. Name one thing their objection improved in the final design. In the
gateway story, it was the direct-call fallback when the gateway is down. If the answer is nothing, pick another
story.
</FollowUp>
<FollowUp q="What would you have done if the experiment had gone the other way? (Not reported; a natural next push)">
Say you would have dropped your proposal, and how. Having agreed the decision rule before the result is the
strongest sign that you were persuading with evidence, not stamina.
</FollowUp>
<FollowUp q="How did you feel when you realized you were wrong, and how do you feel about it now? (Reported in a values round, Anthropic)">
Answer the question asked. Name the feeling briefly and honestly, then what it changed. One Anthropic candidate
describes an interviewer pressing on exactly this "then and now" contrast (<Cite id="s-42f1c7f6dd" />).
</FollowUp>
</FollowUps>

## Common mistakes

- **Leading with context instead of the decision.** The candidate who wrote "too wordy and slower to land the
  point" diagnosed this in their own loop (<Cite id="s-4e04a1689c" />). Fix: headline first, then stop.
- **"We" all the way through.** Interviewers drill on what you personally did and how big the team was
  (<Cite id="s-4e04a1689c" />). Fix: say "I" for your part and name the team's size.
- **Generic leadership stories at a technical company.** The same hiring manager said she wanted technical detail,
  not generic leadership stories (<Cite id="s-4e04a1689c" />). Fix: keep the architecture and the numbers in every
  story.
- **Workaround stories for a product question.** The Cohere VP round reportedly wanted product gaps, not
  workarounds (<Cite id="s-88b41bdbd6" />). Fix: show the pattern across customers and the durable change.
- **A failure with nothing at stake.** The Modal prep guide calls these out (<Cite id="s-86890a1069" />). Fix: pick
  an incident that cost something, and own the part that was yours.
- **A "wrong" story that is a humble-brag.** "I was wrong to doubt that my design would scale even better" is not
  being wrong. Fix: pick a belief that cost time or trust, and say what it cost.
- **The other side as a villain.** It tells the interviewer you can't see why reasonable people disagree. Fix: state
  their position as they would, as the OpenAI guidance suggests (<Cite id="s-4d55d9b987" />).

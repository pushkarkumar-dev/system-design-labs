## The prompt

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "Let me accept the numbers: 50 engineer-years at $400K is $20M. I'll compare that with what the project returned
> each year, minus what it costs to keep running, and only count the part we wouldn't have got anyway from a
> vendor or an open-source release. On my assumptions it pays back in about two and a half years if all the gain
> is ours, and closer to seven if half of it would have arrived anyway. So my verdict is yes, with a condition,
> and here is the part of the $20M I would cut if I did it again."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer, so you can say it with understanding.

After you present a project, the interviewer turns it into money. The sharpest version: "your project took 50
engineer-years at about $400K each; was that $20M worth it?" The same family has three more questions about
scarce resources and ownership: how to share limited compute between competing projects, what to do when many
users want conflicting features at once, and how to justify building a system in-house when an existing product
already covered most of the need.

### Why this is not the design question you already know

In a design round, there is a right answer the interviewer can check. A queue either loses messages or it does not.
This round has no single right answer. The interviewer is checking whether you can put a price on your own work and
judge it honestly. Three things make that harder than it sounds:

1. **The biggest cost is people, not servers.** Most engineers think of cost as the cloud bill. Here the bill is
   usually small next to the salaries of the people who built and now maintain the system.
2. **"Worth it" always means "better than the alternative".** A project that saved $10M a year is not automatically
   worth it. You have to ask what would have happened without it: buying a product, waiting for an open-source
   release, a cheaper partial fix, or doing nothing.
3. **In AI infrastructure, the alternative moves fast.** Open-source tools and vendors improve every few months.
   So a gain you built yourself may have arrived for free a year later. That shrinks the part of the gain your
   project can honestly claim.

A strong answer does simple arithmetic out loud, labels every assumption, and ends with a verdict that has a
condition attached: "yes, unless…". A weak answer defends the architecture and never mentions money.

### Words you need first

- **Loaded cost**: what an engineer costs the company per year, not just their salary. It adds benefits, payroll
  taxes and the rest. Treat the interviewer's $400K as a loaded cost.
- **Counterfactual**: what would have happened if you had not done the project. "Worth it" is always measured
  against this.
- **Attribution**: the share of a gain that is really yours, because the counterfactual would not have delivered
  it. If an open-source release would have given you half the gain anyway, your attribution is one half.
- **Payback period**: how many years of net gain it takes to earn back what the project cost to build.
- **Opportunity cost**: what the same people could have built instead. It never appears on an invoice, and it is
  often the real argument against a project.

### The example that runs through this page

We will follow one made-up project from start to finish. Every number in it is an **assumption**, chosen only to
show the method. Replace them with your own.

A team spent several years building an internal **serving platform**. That is the software that takes a trained AI
model, loads it onto **GPUs** (the expensive chips that run models), and answers requests from it. Running a
trained model to answer requests is called **inference**. The company's collection of GPUs for inference is
called the **fleet**, and we assume it costs **$60M a year**.

The platform's main result: each GPU could now handle 20% more requests per second at the same response time.
Requests handled per second is called **throughput**. The interviewer prices the build at 50 engineer-years ×
$400K = **$20M**, and asks whether it was worth it. The sections below answer that question step by step.

## What is being tested

The reports give the questions but not the rubric the interviewer scores against. So this section separates what
is reported from what the questions imply.

**Reported.** The $20M question was asked in the project deep dive of a Senior Staff infra loop
(<Cite id="s-e98cbef9cd" />). The NVIDIA questions sit in rounds
the candidates describe as about working with others and project management (<Cite id="s-79598dd069" />). The
CoreWeave question sits in a hiring-manager round (<Cite id="s-e5c097ed4e" />).

**Implied by the questions.** This is this guide's reading, not a published rubric:

1. **You can price your own work.** Engineer-time, compute and opportunity cost, not only the cloud bill.
2. **You compare against a counterfactual.** "Worth it" means better than the next best use of the same people
   and money, including doing nothing or buying.
3. **You will say no.** An answer where every project was worth it and every request got served is not credible.
   Interviewers want to hear the rule you used to refuse something.
4. **You decide with data and revisit.** Allocations and build decisions are bets. A strong answer names the
   measurement that would reverse them.
5. **You know when to ask for help.** The NVIDIA follow-up about outside expertise tests whether you escalate at
   the right time: not too early, and not after the deadline.

A useful way to hold all five: the $20M question is less about whether the answer is yes than about whether you
can show the arithmetic, the assumptions, and the conditions under which the answer would be no.

Say it like this: "I'll give you the numbers and the assumptions behind them, and I'll tell you what would make
the answer no."

## What a strong story contains

For any question in this family, a strong answer has six parts. Whichever one you leave out is where the
follow-up questions go.

1. **The decision and who owned it.** Your role in it, said with "I", not "we".
2. **Costs, all of them.** People (engineer-years times a loaded cost), compute or API spend, licences, ongoing
   maintenance, and the opportunity cost of what those people did not build.
3. **Value, with how you measured it.** Revenue enabled, cost avoided, risk reduced, time saved. Each needs a
   measurement method and a baseline, meaning the "before" number you compare against.
4. **The counterfactual.** What would have happened without the project: a vendor product, an open-source release,
   a cheaper partial fix, or nothing.
5. **The result and the payback.** Over a stated number of years, counting only the share of the value you can
   claim.
6. **What you would do differently, or what would have made it not worth it.** This is where judgment shows.

### The ROI template

Here is the one formula that turns those six parts into a number. Read it as: "how many years of yearly net gain
does it take to pay back the build?"

<Formula
  title="Payback period with attribution"
  expr="payback (years) = C_build ÷ (V_annual × a − M_annual)"
  vars={[
    { sym: 'C_build', meaning: 'one-time cost: engineer-years × loaded cost per year, plus build-time compute' },
    { sym: 'V_annual', meaning: 'gross annual value: savings, revenue enabled or risk avoided, measured against a baseline' },
    { sym: 'a', meaning: 'attributable share: the fraction of V you would not have got anyway (0 to 1)' },
    { sym: 'M_annual', meaning: 'ongoing yearly cost: maintenance people plus run cost' },
  ]}
  example="Assumed C = $20M, V = $10M/yr, a = 1, M = $2M/yr: 20 ÷ (10 − 2) = 2.5 years. With a = 0.5: 20 ÷ (5 − 2) = 6.7 years"
/>

The attributable share `a` is the term most answers leave out, and it often decides the answer. If the next
framework release or a vendor would have delivered half the gain within a year, the project only earned the other
half. Notice what that does to the result above: halving `a` does not double the payback, it nearly triples it.
That is because the maintenance cost stays the same while the gain you can claim shrinks.

Here is the running example filled in. The "What to say" column is the sentence you would actually speak.

| Line | Running example (all assumed) | What to say in the interview |
|---|---|---|
| Build cost C | 50 engineer-years × $400K = **$20M** | "I accept the $20M; let me check it against what came back." |
| Yearly value V | 16.7% of a $60M fleet ≈ **$10M a year** (see the next subsection for why it is not 20%) | "The platform freed about a sixth of the fleet, and that capacity absorbed growth we would otherwise have bought." |
| Attribution a | **1** if nobody else would have delivered it; **0.5** if an open-source release would have given half within a year | "Part of that gain would have come for free from the open-source engine. I'll show both cases." |
| Upkeep M | 5 engineers × $400K = **$2M a year** | "It still takes five people to run it, so I subtract that every year." |
| Payback | **2.5 years** with a = 1; **about 6.7 years** with a = 0.5 | "Two and a half years if the gain is all ours, closer to seven if half would have arrived anyway." |
| Three-year view | net gain $24M against $20M with a = 1; $9M against $20M with a = 0.5 | "Over three years it clears the bar only if most of the gain is really ours." |

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the numbers (for a Staff-level follow-up)</summary>

- **Build:** 50 × $400K = $20M.
- **Yearly value:** throughput up by g = 1.2 frees 1 − 1 ÷ 1.2 = 0.167 of the fleet; 0.167 × $60M ≈ $10M a year.
- **Upkeep:** 5 × $400K = $2M a year.
- **Payback, a = 1:** 20 ÷ (10 × 1 − 2) = 20 ÷ 8 = 2.5 years.
- **Payback, a = 0.5:** 20 ÷ (10 × 0.5 − 2) = 20 ÷ 3 ≈ 6.7 years.
- **Three-year net gain:** a = 1 gives 3 × 8 = $24M; a = 0.5 gives 3 × 3 = $9M. Neither counts the opportunity cost
  of the 50 engineer-years.

</details>

### Throughput gains are not the same size as cost savings

A common slip in infrastructure ROI goes like this: "we made serving 20% faster, so we saved 20% of the fleet."
It sounds right but is not. If each GPU now handles 20% more, the same load needs fewer GPUs, but the fraction
you need is 1 ÷ 1.2, not 1 − 0.2.

Put simply: if one GPU used to handle 100 requests a second and now handles 120, then 1,000 requests a second used
to need 10 GPUs and now need 1,000 ÷ 120 ≈ 8.33. You saved 1.67 GPUs out of 10, which is 16.7%.

<Formula
  title="Capacity saved from a throughput gain"
  expr="fraction saved = 1 − 1 ÷ g"
  vars={[
    { sym: 'g', meaning: 'throughput per GPU after ÷ before, at the same latency target' },
  ]}
  example="g = 1.2 (20% more throughput): 1 − 1 ÷ 1.2 = 0.167, so 16.7% of the fleet, not 20%"
/>

There is a second trap. The saving is real only if the freed GPUs are either released, so you stop paying for them,
or used to absorb growth you would otherwise have bought. If they sit idle, no money was saved.

Say it like this: "Twenty percent more throughput frees about seventeen percent of the fleet, and it only counts
because that capacity took growth we would otherwise have paid for."

### The loaded cost of an engineer

Treat the interviewer's $400K per engineer-year as a fully loaded figure, and accept it as given. For a
small-company answer you need your own figure, and you can derive one from public data: the US Bureau of Labor
Statistics (BLS) gives a median software developer wage and the share of total pay that is wages. That gives a
loaded cost of **about $194K a year**. Your company's figure will differ; say which one you use.

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the numbers (for a Staff-level follow-up)</summary>

- The May 2025 median wage for software developers was **$135,980**
  ([BLS occupation page](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/computer-and-information-technology/software-developers.htm),
  fetched 2026-10-11).
- Wages and salaries were **70.0%** of private-industry compensation in June 2026
  ([BLS employer costs](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/ecec.nr0.htm), fetched 2026-10-11).
- Loaded cost: 135,980 ÷ 0.70 ≈ **$194K a year**.

</details>

## Story structure

Use one structure for all four questions, and change only the middle. Here is the shape of the "was it worth it?"
answer as a flow you can sketch on a whiteboard:

```
Restate the cost  ──  "50 × $400K = $20M; I accept that."
      │
      ▼
Value lines  ──  each with how it was measured, against a baseline
      │
      ▼
Attribution  ──  how much would have arrived anyway (vendor, open source)
      │
      ▼
Ongoing cost  ──  people and compute to keep it running, every year
      │
      ▼
Payback  ──  build ÷ (claimed value − ongoing cost)
      │
      ▼
Verdict with a condition  ──  "yes, unless…" and what I would cut
```

The first box shows you will not fight the setup. The second is where most of your speaking time goes, because
each value line needs a measurement. The third is the Staff-level step: it is the counterfactual turned into a
number. The fourth stops you from claiming the gross gain. The fifth is one division. The last box is what the
interviewer remembers.

### For "was it worth it?" (infra-103)

1. **Restate and accept the framing.** "Fifty engineer-years at $400K is $20M; let me check it against what the
   project returned."
2. **Name the value lines** the project actually produced, each with its measurement: for example GPU-hours
   avoided, incidents avoided, a launch it enabled, or teams that adopted it. A **GPU-hour** is one GPU used for one
   hour, the usual unit for buying and counting compute.
3. **Do the arithmetic out loud** with labelled assumptions, including attribution and ongoing cost.
4. **Give a verdict with a condition.** "On direct savings it paid back in about N years. It is worth it if the
   platform keeps absorbing growth, and it would not have been if the vendor release had shipped on time."
5. **Say what you would cut** if you did it again for the same value. That shows you know which parts of the $20M
   were essential.

If it was not worth it, say so. A clear "no on direct return, yes on what it unblocked, and here is what I would
have scoped down" is a stronger Staff answer than an inflated yes.

### For compute allocation (infra-108)

This question is a political problem disguised as a technical one. Several teams want the same scarce GPUs. Your
job is to turn that fight into a rule other people can predict and accept.

1. **Clarify what is scarce.** GPU-hours in general, a specific GPU type, jobs that need GPUs wired closely
   together, or a monthly API budget measured in **tokens** (the small pieces of text, roughly ¾ of a word each,
   that AI providers bill by).
2. **List the demand.** For each project: what it needs, its deadline, and what it is worth if delivered on time
   versus late.
3. **Rank by value per unit of compute**, with deadlines as hard limits, not a first-come queue.
4. **Choose a mechanism** (the picture below).
5. **Make it reviewable.** Publish the allocation and the rule, measure how much of each team's share they really
   use, and re-allocate on a fixed schedule. Take back quota that sits idle.

The ranking rule fits in one line. It asks: how much value do we lose if this project is late, per GPU-hour it
needs?

<Formula
  title="Rank requests by value density"
  expr="priority ≈ (value if done by deadline − value if late) ÷ GPU-hours requested"
  vars={[
    { sym: 'value', meaning: 'an agreed estimate (revenue, a launch, a customer commitment), in any consistent unit' },
    { sym: 'GPU-hours requested', meaning: 'compute needed to deliver by the deadline' },
  ]}
  example="Assumed: project X loses 10 units if late and needs 2,000 GPU-hours (0.005 per hour); project Y loses 4 units and needs 400 GPU-hours (0.01 per hour). Y goes first despite the smaller headline value"
/>

The numbers are illustrative. What matters is that you have a rule other people can predict, and that you say how
you handle the case it gets wrong: an escalation path with a named person who decides.

The mechanism splits the GPU pool into three parts:

```
            Shared GPU pool
                  │
   ┌──────────────┼──────────────────┐
   ▼              ▼                  ▼
Guaranteed     Preemptible        Experiments
quotas         pool               slice
(committed     (anything that     (small, so research
deadlines)     can be paused)     is never starved)
   │              │                  │
   └──── weekly utilization report ──┘
                  │
                  ▼
        Re-allocate on a schedule; take back idle quota
```

**Guaranteed quotas** are reserved GPU shares, sized to the projects with committed deadlines. Nobody can take
them away mid-quarter. The **preemptible pool** is capacity anyone can use, but a higher-priority job may pause
yours to take it back. That is fine for work that saves its progress regularly (training jobs that write a
**checkpoint**, a saved snapshot they can resume from) or that can simply be retried. The **experiments slice**
is a small fixed share for research, so production work never starves it completely. The **utilization report**
shows, per team, how much of their share they actually used. That report is what makes the system fair, because
idle guaranteed quota flows back into the shared pool.

Say it like this: "I'd rank by value lost if late, per GPU-hour, give guaranteed quota only to committed deadlines,
and put everything else in a preemptible pool. Then I'd publish the rule and take back quota that sits idle."

### For conflicting feature requests (infra-109)

The NVIDIA version names **PyTorch**, the most widely used open-source library for building and training AI models.
Picture an internal platform team that maintains the company's PyTorch setup, with many teams asking for changes
at once.

1. **Find the need under each request.** Several "features" often share one underlying problem, such as a missing
   **operator** (one of the built-in math building blocks in PyTorch) or a performance regression.
2. **Size impact and urgency.** How many users are blocked, which revenue or deadlines are at risk, and whether a
   workaround exists.
3. **Decide and communicate.** What ships now, what ships later with a date, and what you won't do and why.
4. **Prefer upstream fixes.** "Upstream" means the original open-source project. If the change is general, get it
   accepted there instead of keeping a private **fork** (your own modified copy). A fork is a maintenance cost you
   pay on every new release, because you must re-apply your changes each time.
5. **Escalate outside with a timebox.** Once internal options are exhausted, go to the people who own the code:
   upstream maintainers and forums, a vendor's support or solutions team, or a paid expert. Say what you would
   prepare first (a minimal reproduction and the measured impact), so the outside expert's time is cheap.

Say it like this: "I'd group the requests by the problem underneath them. Usually two or three share a root cause,
and fixing that once unblocks several teams."

### For build versus buy (infra-110)

1. **State the need precisely**, including the requirement the existing options miss.
2. **Compare total cost over a horizon** (a fixed number of years), with labelled assumptions. The formula is below.
3. **Weigh the non-cost factors.** Is it core to your product? What lock-in and vendor risk do you take on? Who
   maintains it when the person who built it leaves?
4. **Say what you did to test buying first.** A trial, a benchmark, a conversation with the vendor.
5. **Name the exit.** The condition under which you would switch to the bought option later.

The cost comparison adds up each option over the horizon. Building has a big one-time cost and then yearly
upkeep. Buying has a small one-time integration cost and then a yearly licence.

<Formula
  title="Total cost over a horizon"
  expr="build = B + H × (M + R)     buy = I + H × L"
  vars={[
    { sym: 'B', meaning: 'one-time build cost (engineer-years × loaded cost)' },
    { sym: 'M, R', meaning: 'yearly maintenance people and run cost for the built system' },
    { sym: 'I', meaning: 'one-time integration cost for the bought system' },
    { sym: 'L', meaning: 'yearly licence or usage cost' },
    { sym: 'H', meaning: 'horizon in years' },
  ]}
  example={[
    "Assumed: B = 0.5 engineer-years × $194K = $97K; M = 15% of an engineer = $29.1K/yr; R = $18K/yr",
    "Assumed: L = $30 per seat per month × 300 seats × 12 = $108K/yr; I = 0.1 engineer-years = $19.4K",
    "3 years: build 97 + 3 × 47.1 = $238.3K; buy 19.4 + 3 × 108 = $343.4K",
    "Break-even horizon: 97 + 47.1H = 19.4 + 108H, so H = 77.6 ÷ 60.9 ≈ 1.3 years",
  ]}
/>

In words: on these assumptions, building costs about **$238K** over three years and buying about **$343K**, and
building starts to win after about **1.3 years**. All the inputs are assumptions, chosen only to show the method.
The result is sensitive to them: double the maintenance share and the gap shrinks fast. And the formula leaves out
the opportunity cost of the half engineer-year, which is often the real argument for buying.

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the numbers (for a Staff-level follow-up)</summary>

- **Build B:** 0.5 × $194K = $97K.
- **Maintenance M:** 0.15 × $194K ≈ $29.1K a year. **Run R:** $18K a year (assumed). M + R = $47.1K a year.
- **Licence L:** $30 × 300 seats × 12 months = $108K a year. **Integration I:** 0.1 × $194K = $19.4K.
- **Three years:** build 97 + 3 × 47.1 = $238.3K; buy 19.4 + 3 × 108 = $343.4K.
- **Break-even:** 97 + 47.1H = 19.4 + 108H, so 77.6 = 60.9H and H ≈ 1.27, about 1.3 years.

</details>

Say it like this: "On my assumptions the build pays off after about a year and a quarter, but that ignores what
those engineers could have built instead, so I'd only build the part the product can't do."

## Example story outlines

The outlines below are **illustrative structures with invented placeholder numbers**, not anyone's real project.
Replace every number with your own and keep the shape. Outline 1 is the running example from this page.

### Outline 1: "Was the $20M worth it?" for an inference platform

- **Context.** A team built an internal serving platform over several years. The interviewer prices it at 50
  engineer-years × $400K = $20M.
- **Value lines.** (a) Throughput per GPU rose by an assumed factor of 1.2 at the same response-time target, on an
  assumed $60M-a-year fleet: 1 − 1 ÷ 1.2 = 16.7% of the fleet, about $10M a year. It counts only because the
  capacity absorbed growth that would otherwise have been bought. (b) The time to deploy a new model fell, which
  enabled launches (say how many, and how you measured it).
- **Costs.** $20M to build, plus an assumed 5 engineers maintaining it at $400K = $2M a year.
- **Arithmetic.** With full attribution: 20 ÷ (10 − 2) = 2.5-year payback. If an open-source serving engine's
  release would have delivered half the gain within a year, a = 0.5: 20 ÷ (5 − 2) ≈ 6.7 years.
- **Verdict with condition.** Worth it on the launch value and in the 2.5-year case. Doubtful on savings alone if
  the attribution is closer to half.
- **What you'd change.** Adopt the open-source engine earlier, and spend the team on the parts nobody else provides
  (scheduling, sharing GPUs between customers, and observability), lowering C for most of the same value.

### Outline 2: allocating a scarce GPU pool

- **Situation.** More requests than GPUs for a quarter: three teams with deadlines and a backlog of experiments.
- **Action.** Collected each request's deadline, value if late, and GPU-hours. Gave guaranteed quotas to the two
  commitments with external deadlines, put the rest in a preemptible pool, and kept a small slice for experiments.
  Published the rule and a weekly utilization report.
- **Conflict.** A team with a large headline project objected to being second. Showed the value-per-GPU-hour
  ranking and offered a date to re-check.
- **Result.** State what you measured: utilization, deadlines met, idle quota taken back.
- **Reflection.** What the rule got wrong and how you changed it.

### Outline 3: conflicting framework feature requests

- **Situation.** Several internal teams demanded different changes to the training setup at once.
- **Action.** Grouped requests by underlying need, found that two shared one root cause, and fixed that first.
  Deferred one with a date and a workaround. Declined one that only one team needed, with a reason.
- **Outside help.** After a timeboxed internal attempt on a hard performance bug, prepared a minimal reproduction
  and went to the upstream maintainers or the vendor's support channel.
- **Result and reflection.** Which teams were unblocked, and when; what you would triage differently.

### Outline 4: justifying a build

- **Situation.** An existing product covered most of the need, but one requirement was missing: say, a latency
  target, a rule that data must stay in one country, or an integration.
- **Action.** Trialled the product against that requirement, priced both options over three years, and chose to
  build a narrow component instead of the whole system.
- **Result.** The cost and time actually spent, compared with your estimate.
- **Reflection and exit.** The signal that would make you switch to the product, and whether it has happened.

## Small-team version

At a company of 50 to 500 people nobody asks about $20M. But the CEO asks a smaller version of the same question
every quarter: is this AI feature worth what it costs? The answer uses the same template. Engineer-time is usually
the largest cost. The second is the bill for the **model API**: a paid web service (Anthropic, OpenAI and others)
that you send text to and get the model's reply back from, billed per token.

### A worked one-pager

Take a support assistant that answers customer questions before a human sees them. Every row below is an
assumption except the loaded cost and the API price.

| Line | Assumption | Value |
|---|---|---|
| Build | 0.5 engineer-years × $194K loaded (BLS-derived above) | **$97K** |
| Run: model API | Assumed 20,000 conversations a month × 4 calls × $0.0064 per call | $512 a month ≈ **$6.1K a year** |
| Maintain | Assumed 15% of one engineer | **$29.1K a year** |
| Value | Assumed 20,000 tickets a month × 20% resolved without a human × $6 per human-handled ticket | $24K a month = **$288K a year** |
| Payback | 97 ÷ (288 − 6.1 − 29.1) | about **0.38 years**, roughly 4.6 months |

The $0.0064 per call is the per-request example from the
[project deep-dive walkthrough](/system-design-labs/interviews/walkthroughs/project-deep-dive/#core-concepts),
using Claude Sonnet 5 list prices fetched 2026-10-11
([pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing)). The tickets, rates and per-ticket cost are
assumptions.

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the numbers (for a Staff-level follow-up)</summary>

- **One call:** 1,000 new input tokens at $2 per million, 2,000 cached input tokens at $0.20 per million, and 400
  output tokens at $10 per million: 0.002 + 0.0004 + 0.004 = **$0.0064**. "Cached" means the provider has already
  seen that part of the prompt (a fixed set of instructions, for example) and charges less to reuse it.
- **API per month:** 20,000 × 4 × $0.0064 = $512; × 12 = $6,144 ≈ $6.1K a year.
- **Maintenance:** 0.15 × $194K ≈ $29.1K a year.
- **Value:** 20,000 × 0.20 = 4,000 resolved tickets a month × $6 = $24K a month, $288K a year.
- **Payback:** 97 ÷ (288 − 6.1 − 29.1) = 97 ÷ 252.8 ≈ 0.38 years ≈ 4.6 months.
- **At a 5% resolution rate:** 20,000 × 0.05 × $6 × 12 = $72K a year; net 72 − 6.1 − 29.1 = $36.8K; payback
  97 ÷ 36.8 ≈ 2.6 years.
- **Cost per resolved ticket:** $512 ÷ 4,000 ≈ $0.13.

</details>

The useful part is how sensitive the answer is. If only 5% of tickets are resolved without a human, value falls to
$72K a year and payback stretches to **about 2.6 years**. So the number to measure first is the resolution rate,
not the API bill. Define it strictly: the ticket was not reopened within a few days, and a sample passed a quality
check. A wrong but confident answer also has a cost: Air Canada was held responsible for its chatbot's wrong fare
advice in 2024 ([Law360](https://www.law360.ca/ca/articles/1804075)).

Say it like this: "The API bill is about six thousand dollars a year. The whole case rests on the resolution rate,
so that is what I'd measure first."

### How the decision actually gets made

A small company rarely has a finance team modelling AI projects. The decision is usually a short conversation
between the AI owner, the person who owns the outcome (head of support, sales or product), and a founder or CTO.
That makes your one-pager the model. Three habits make it hold up:

- **Agree the outcome metric with its owner before building.** The head of support decides what counts as a
  resolved ticket, not you. If the definition changes after launch, the ROI changes with it.
- **Set a kill threshold up front.** For example: if the resolution rate stays below the level that pays back within
  a year, the feature is cut or rescoped at the next review. Writing that down early makes the later decision a
  check, not an argument.
- **Report cost per outcome, not total spend.** In the one-pager above, $512 a month of API calls over 4,000
  resolved tickets is about $0.13 per resolved ticket. A founder can compare that with a $6 human-handled ticket.
  "$512 a month on tokens" alone tells them nothing.

In an interview for a small-company role, describing this loop (agree the metric, price it, set the threshold,
review) answers the business-case question better than any single ROI figure. It shows you will own the decision
after launch, which is what a company without a large management layer is hiring for.

### Build, buy, skip

- **Buy** model access through a managed API and, usually, **tracing** (a tool that records every model call, its
  inputs, outputs and cost, so you can debug and audit). **Self-hosting**, running an open model on GPUs you rent,
  rarely wins at small-team volumes. One published analysis puts a rented machine with 2 H100 GPUs (an NVIDIA
  data-center GPU) at about **$6,117 a month**. It finds that machine cannot break even against the same
  open model on a **serverless endpoint** (a provider runs the model and bills you per token) when traffic follows
  business hours ([Cohorte](https://cohorte.co/blog/self-hosted-llm-cost-vs-api-break-even), 2026-09-20; labour
  excluded).
- **Build** what is specific to you: the **eval set** (a collection of real examples with known good answers, used
  to score the system), search over your own documents, the cost and rate limits, and the integration into your
  product.
- **Skip** anything whose value line you can't name. A feature with no measurable outcome is the first one to cut
  when the bill grows.

### Scarce compute at a small company

Your scarce resource is usually an API budget and one engineer's time, not a GPU pool. The allocation answer
becomes: a budget per feature, provider spend caps and workspace limits set before launch, and a monthly review of
cost per outcome. Anthropic's API documents monthly spend caps by usage tier and says requests over the cap get an
HTTP 429 until the next month ([rate limits](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/api/rate-limits), fetched
2026-10-11). Know your tier before a launch, or the launch can stall on billing rather than code.

### Revisit when

Revisit a buy when the vendor bill passes the cost of the build plus maintenance over your horizon, or when a
requirement (data location, latency, an integration) is not met. Revisit an API-only setup when measured volume is
steady at hundreds of millions of tokens a month, or a compliance rule forces it. Revisit a feature when its
measured outcome stays below the payback threshold for two review cycles.

<AINative title="Agents change the cost line, not the decision">
Coding agents (AI tools that write and edit code with little supervision) lower the build cost B, which tilts
some decisions toward building. They do not lower maintenance much: someone still owns the on-call, the upgrades
and the evals. When you price a build that agents will mostly write, keep M at its usual share, add the token spend
for the agents themselves, and budget your own time for checking their work. An agent can also draft the one-pager
above from your billing export and ticket data. You still check the counterfactual and the resolution-rate
definition yourself, because those are where the answer is decided.
</AINative>

## Senior vs Staff

The difference is not the arithmetic. A Senior answer prices its own project correctly. A Staff answer also says
what the project should be compared against, designs a rule other people can use, and says when the answer would
be no.

## Common mistakes

- **Rejecting the framing.** Arguing that $400K is the wrong number wastes the round. In a different round
  (inference design), an Anthropic Staff candidate wrote, in hindsight, that they "should have accepted the setup
  faster" (<Cite id="s-4e04a1689c" />). Accept the setup, then add your own assumptions.
- **No counterfactual.** Counting every gain as yours ignores what a vendor or an open-source release would have
  delivered anyway.
- **Throughput as savings.** 20% more throughput frees 16.7% of capacity, and only if you release or reuse it.
- **Leaving out maintenance and opportunity cost.** A build looks cheap only until you count the people who keep it
  running and what they could have built instead.
- **A yes for everything.** If every project was worth it and every request got served, the interviewer stops
  believing the rest; say what you refused and why.
- **Escalating with nothing in hand.** Going to outside experts without a reproduction or a measured impact wastes
  their time and yours.

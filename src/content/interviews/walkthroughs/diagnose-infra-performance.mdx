## The prompt

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "Before I touch anything, I want to pin down what 'slow' means: which number got worse, for whom, since
> when, and what changed. Then I'll go resource by resource: CPU, memory, disk, network, and the software
> limits like thread pools, queues and container quotas. For each one I ask three things: how busy is it, is
> work queuing for it, and is it throwing errors. I'll run the cheapest command that can rule each guess
> out, and I'll say what I expect to see before I run it. Once I find the cause, I stabilize first, then
> fix, then prove it with the same measurement I started with."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer, so you can say it with understanding.

You already debug production systems. You have looked at a slow endpoint, found a missing index, and
watched the p99 drop. This round looks like that, but three things are different, and the answer is built
around them.

1. **You get a symptom, not a bug report, and no dashboards.** The interviewer says "the server is
   overwhelmed" or "the notebook is slow" and waits. You have no graphs and no history. You ask for a
   number or a command's output, and the interviewer tells you what it shows. The path you take is what
   gets graded, not just where you end up.
2. **Many of the numbers lie, or rather, they don't mean what they seem to mean.** A disk at "100%
   utilization" may have plenty of room left. A machine with a high load number may have idle CPUs. A
   container may be stalled on a machine whose CPU graph is almost flat. Most of the follow-up questions
   in these rounds are "this number looks bad: is it?" You pass them by saying what each number counts.
3. **The layers stack up.** A request now passes through a Kubernetes pod, a container with its own CPU
   and memory budget, a Linux kernel, a disk or network, and often a GPU or a remote API. Each layer has
   its own way of being "full", and some of them look idle from the layer above.

Here are the symptoms these rounds have used. A server is overwhelmed and logs may be involved. Network
throughput inside containers is low. A Linux box is slow and CPU looks high. Storage I/O is suspected. A
Kubernetes cluster has high tail latency, timeouts and autoscaling that keeps flipping up and down. A pod
is stuck in Pending. An ML engineer says the notebook experiment behind Kafka is slow. A training data
pipeline doesn't run, and once fixed, it is slow. In every case the task is the same: find out why, say
what you'd run, and fix it.

## What is being tested

**A method when the problem is vague.** The NVIDIA prompt was vague on purpose, and the interviewers
steered by the path the candidate took. The Perplexity listing asks for five things in order: what to
inspect, the commands, the hypotheses, the immediate mitigation and the long-term fix. Both reward the same
visible loop. Define "slow". Write a short list of guesses. Run the cheapest command that can rule one
out. Repeat. Say it like this: "I'll test the cheapest guess first and tell you what I expect to see, so
you can stop me if I'm going the wrong way."

**Knowing what the numbers mean.** The Together AI follow-ups are all of one kind: this metric looks bad,
but is it? Load average against CPU busy. A disk's `%util` on a device that serves many requests at once.
A host that looks idle while its containers are held back. A deleted file that still takes up disk. You
can't pass these by listing tools. You pass them by saying what each number counts, and the core concepts
below exist for that.

**Staying calm on a clock.** The Anthropic candidate ran out of time. Yuan Meng's blog, describing
LeetCode-style rounds rather than this one, puts it bluntly: "If you panic or can't think of what to look
into, it's a strong signal for rejection" <Cite id="s-598c128e3b" />. That is one author's judgment. The
point of the method is that you always have a next thing to look at, so you never freeze.

**Fixing, then preventing.** The logging listing asks how you would "trace the cause, stabilize the
service, and prevent the problem from recurring". Stabilizing means making users stop hurting now: shed
some load, lower a log level, add a node. Fixing means removing the cause. Preventing means the defaults
and alerts that stop it coming back. They are three different steps, and the interviewer expects all
three.

## Clarify and scope

Before running anything, ask five questions. Each one cuts the search space.

- **What is slow, for whom, measured how?** The median request or the slowest 1% (the p99)? One endpoint
  or all of them? "The dashboard takes 40 s to show results" is a different problem from "the experiment
  takes 40 minutes".
- **Since when, and what changed?** A deploy, a config change, a traffic shift, a new tenant, a new model
  or prompt version, a node pool upgrade. Most incidents follow a change, so this is the cheapest question
  you will ever ask.
- **One machine or many?** If every replica is slow at once, look at what they share first: a database, a
  log collector, a network path.
- **Is it us or a dependency?** A provider's API, a database, a remote disk, a log collector.
- **What may I touch?** Read-only diagnosis is different from permission to restart, scale or change
  config. In production this matters a lot. In the interview, say which permission you would need.

Then write the answers as a one-line problem statement, and say it out loud: "p99 of `/generate` rose from
2 s to 9 s at 14:05, on all replicas, after the 14:00 deploy." A sentence like that tells the interviewer
you know what you are chasing. Leave out kernel tuning, rewriting the service and buying hardware until
the data points there.

## Core concepts

### Words you need first

Three words do most of the work in this round. They come from Brendan Gregg's **USE method**: "For every
resource, check utilization, saturation, and errors."

- **Utilization** is how busy a resource is. Gregg defines it as "the average time that the resource was
  busy servicing work". A CPU that was busy 70% of the last second has 70% utilization.
- **Saturation** is how much work is waiting for the resource. Gregg's words: "the degree to which the
  resource has extra work which it can't service, often queued". A CPU with ten threads waiting to run is
  saturated, whatever its utilization says.
- **Errors** are what the name says, "the count of error events": failed disk reads, dropped packets,
  rejected connections.

A **resource** here is anything that can run out. The physical ones are CPU, memory, disk and network
interfaces. The software ones matter just as often: thread pools, connection pools, file descriptors,
queues, locks, and the CPU budget the kernel gives a container. Gregg reports that the method "solves
about 80% of server issues with 5% of the effort", and is honest that it finds only bottlenecks and errors
([USE method](https://www.brendangregg.com/usemethod.html), last updated 2017-08-24, read 2026-10-12).

For a service that answers requests, add a second view called **RED**: for each endpoint, the **rate** of
requests, the **errors**, and the **duration** (latency). RED tells you whether users feel a problem. USE
tells you which resource causes it. Start from RED, because that is what users see, then go to USE, because
that is what the machine is doing. Say it like this: "RED tells me users are hurting; USE tells me which
resource is the reason."

### A picture of where the time can go

When a request is slow, the time is being spent in one of these layers. This picture is the map you walk
through, top to bottom:

```
User / client
   │  request
   ▼
Load balancer / Kubernetes Service ── picks a ready pod
   │
   ▼
Pod ── one or more containers, placed on a node by the scheduler
   │
   ▼
Container (cgroup) ── its own CPU and memory budget, enforced by the kernel
   │
   ▼
Process ── threads, pools, locks, queues inside your code
   │
   ▼
Node kernel ── CPU run queue · memory · disk queue · network stack
   │
   ▼
Outside dependencies ── database · log collector · remote disk · model provider
```

**Load balancer or Service.** A Kubernetes **Service** is a stable address that spreads requests over the
pods that report themselves ready. If pods keep dropping out of "ready", the rest get more load. That
loop shows up in case 5.

**Pod.** A **pod** is Kubernetes' unit of deployment: one or more containers that run together on one
machine, called a **node**. The **scheduler** decides which node a pod goes on. If it can't find one, the
pod waits in a state called Pending (case 6).

**Container and cgroup.** A container's CPU and memory budget is enforced by a Linux kernel feature called
a **cgroup** (control group). This is the layer that can make a container slow on a node that looks idle,
because the container has used up its own budget (case 2).

**Process.** Inside your code, requests wait for threads, connections and locks. A logging call that holds
a lock while it writes to a slow disk turns every request into a queue behind that lock (case 1).

**Node kernel.** The machine's shared physical resources: CPUs, memory, disks and network cards, each with
its own queue (cases 2, 3 and 4).

**Outside dependencies.** Anything over the network: a database, a log collector, a remote volume, a
model API. When every replica is slow at once, start here.

### Saturation is where latency comes from

A resource that is 70% busy with nothing waiting is fine. A resource with a queue adds wait time to every
request that joins it. That is why saturation, not utilization, predicts latency.

One rule ties busy-ness, waiting and latency together. It is called **Little's law**: the average number of
things in a system equals the rate at which they arrive times the average time each one spends inside
<Cite id="s-b9e76e614c" />. You already use it when you size a connection pool: for example, 200 requests per
second that each hold a connection for 50 ms keep about 10 connections busy. In this round it is a cross-check. If
the disk statistics say a device completes so many operations per second at such an average wait, the
queue length the tool reports should match their product.

(**IOPS** means I/O operations per second.) If the numbers don't agree, you are reading the wrong device,
the wrong time interval or the wrong column. Saying so out loud is a good Staff-level move.

### What Kubernetes requests and limits do

Each container in a pod can declare two numbers for CPU and memory.

- A **request** is what the container asks to be guaranteed. The scheduler uses requests, and only
  requests, to place pods: "the kube-scheduler uses this information to decide which node to place the Pod
  on".
- A **limit** is the most the container may use. CPU limits "are enforced by CPU throttling": when the
  container has used its share of CPU time, the kernel stops running it until the next time slice. Memory
  limits "are enforced by the kernel with out of memory (OOM) kills", and only reactively, under memory
  pressure ([Kubernetes resource management](https://kubernetes.io/docs/concepts/configuration/manage-resources-containers/),
  read 2026-10-12).

Two surprising results follow, and both are favourite follow-ups. A pod can be stuck waiting for a node
on a cluster that is mostly idle, because the *requests* already add up to each node's capacity even though
real *usage* is low. And a container can be slow on a node with spare CPU, because its own quota is spent
for this time slice. Say it like this: "The scheduler counts requests, not usage, and the kernel enforces
limits per container, not per node."

Under cgroup v2 (the current version of cgroups), the CPU limit lives in a file called `cpu.max`. It holds
two numbers, "$MAX $PERIOD": how many microseconds of CPU the container may use in each period of that many
microseconds. The default is "max 100000", which means no limit, with a 100 ms period. A second file,
`cpu.stat`, counts how often the limit bit: `nr_periods` (periods so far), `nr_throttled` (periods in which
the container was stopped) and `throttled_usec` (total time stopped)
([cgroup v2](https://docs.kernel.org/admin-guide/cgroup-v2.html), read 2026-10-12).

### What Pending means

Kubernetes' pod-lifecycle page defines **Pending** as: "The Pod has been accepted by the Kubernetes
cluster, but one or more of the containers has not been set up and made ready to run. This includes time
a Pod spends waiting to be scheduled as well as the time spent downloading container images over the
network" ([pod lifecycle](https://kubernetes.io/docs/concepts/workloads/pods/pod-lifecycle/), read
2026-10-12).

So two different problems share one word. If the pod has no node yet, the scheduler couldn't place it. If
it has a node, the scheduler did its job, and the delay is on that node: pulling the container image or
mounting a volume. The first command you run tells you which.

### Lag, not latency, for Kafka consumers

Kafka stores messages in an append-only log split into **partitions**. Each message has a position called
its **offset**. A **consumer group** is a set of readers that share the partitions, and it records how far
it has read by **committing** an offset. The gap between the newest offset in a partition and the group's
committed offset is called **lag**: the number of messages written but not yet processed.

`kafka-consumer-groups.sh --bootstrap-server … --describe --group my-group` prints, per partition,
`CURRENT-OFFSET`, `LOG-END-OFFSET` and `LAG`
([Kafka operations](https://kafka.apache.org/43/operations/basic-kafka-operations/), read 2026-10-12). Lag
that keeps growing means the consumers are slower than the producers. Lag at zero means the slowness is
somewhere else. Either way, one command rules a whole hop in or out.

## Worked examples

The method is the same in every case, so it is worth seeing it once as a loop:

```
 define "slow" ──► list guesses, cheapest to test first
       ▲                     │
       │                     ▼
 prove it with the     run one command, say what you
 same measurement      expect before you read it
       ▲                     │
       │                     ▼
 fix, then prevent ◄── stabilize (stop the user pain)
```

Case 1 follows that loop step by step, with real numbers. The other cases use the same loop more briefly.
The commands below are standard Linux and Kubernetes tools; no output is reproduced from a real incident.
Two examples (cases 1 and 8) were run on 2026-10-12 on an Apple M1 Ultra (Python 3.13.7) in a scratch
copy, and say so.

### 1. A server saturated by logging (Together AI): the full loop

This is the worked example to know well, because it touches every idea above. The setup used to run it:
a server with sixteen request threads. Each request does 1 ms of real work and writes five log lines. The
log handler takes 2 ms per line, which stands for a slow disk or a slow remote log collector (an assumed
figure). In Python, a logging handler takes a lock for each line it writes, so only one thread can write at
a time.

Here is the loop, one step at a time:

| Step | What you do | What you find | What to say in the interview |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Define slow | Ask for throughput and latency | About 79 requests/s, median (p50) 201 ms | "Each request does 1 ms of work but takes 200 ms. Something is making it wait." |
| 2. List guesses | Logging can exhaust three different resources | Disk space, disk bandwidth, or the request path itself | "Logs can fill the disk, clog the disk, or block the request. The fix is different for each." |
| 3. Cheapest test first | `df -h`, then `iostat`, then look at where threads wait | In this setup the slow part is the sink: every thread waits for the handler's lock | "The threads are all waiting to write a log line, one at a time." |
| 4. Explain the number | Little's law on the request path | 16 threads ÷ 79 req/s ≈ 200 ms each | "Sixteen threads at about 80 a second means each request spends about 200 ms inside. That matches the median." |
| 5. Stabilize | Lower the log level or sample the noisy line | Fewer lines per request, so the lock is held less | "First I make users stop hurting. Then I fix the design." |
| 6. Fix | Move logging off the request path with a queue | About 11,700 req/s, p50 1.3 ms | "Requests now drop the line into a queue and move on. A separate thread does the slow write." |
| 7. Prevent and prove | Bounded queue, drop counter, alert | Same measurement, before and after | "The queue doesn't make the sink faster, so I bound it and count what I drop." |

**The guesses, and the commands that test them.** Logging can saturate three different resources, and the
fix differs for each. It can fill the disk (space). It can make other disk writes queue behind log writes
(bandwidth). Or it can make each request wait for a log write to a slow sink (the request path). Run them
cheapest first:

```bash
df -h; df -i                                   # space and inodes
du -xh --max-depth=1 /var/log | sort -h        # which directory grew
sudo lsof +L1                                  # deleted files still held open (space not freed)
iostat -xz 1                                   # is the log disk busy and queued?
pidstat -d 1                                   # which process is writing
pidstat -u 1 -p $(pgrep -d, -f 'fluent|vector|promtail|journald')   # is the log shipper burning CPU?
journalctl --disk-usage                        # if journald is the sink
```

`df -i` checks **inodes**, the per-file records a filesystem can run out of even when bytes are free. The
last two lines check the **log shipper**, the agent (Fluent Bit, Vector, Promtail or journald) that reads
log files and sends them to a central store.

**Why `lsof +L1` is on the list.** Deleting a log file that a process still has open frees nothing. In
Linux, a file's name is just a link to its data. Per unlink(2), "If the name was the last link to a file
but any processes still have the file open the file will remain in existence" until the last descriptor
closes ([unlink(2)](https://linux.die.net/man/2/unlink), read 2026-10-12). `lsof +L1` lists open files with
no name left. That answers one of the listed follow-ups: truncate the file, or signal the process to reopen
it.

**What the run showed.** Here are both designs, measured three times each:

| Setup (run 2026-10-12, three runs) | Throughput | p50 | p99 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Handler in the request path | 79 req/s | 201 ms | 354–390 ms |
| `QueueHandler` + bounded queue (10,000) + listener thread | 11,684–11,946 req/s | 1.3 ms | 2.7–2.9 ms |

The first row has a simple explanation. One sink that takes 2 ms per line can write **500 lines a second**.
At five lines per request, the whole server tops out at **100 requests a second**, however many threads it
has. The 79 measured is just under that ceiling.

The second row uses Python's `QueueHandler`: the request thread puts the line into an in-memory queue and
returns, and a separate listener thread does the slow writes. This removes the sink from the request path.
But it does not create capacity. The sink still writes only 500 lines a second. With a smaller queue of
1,000 entries and a counter for dropped lines, the same burst kept p99 at 1.7 ms and **dropped 6,947 of
8,000 lines**.

That is the trade-off to say out loud: "When the sink is slower than the traffic, I have three choices.
Block the service, buffer without limit and risk running out of memory, or drop lines and count the
drops." Python's docs say `QueueHandler` uses `put_nowait()` and, when a bounded queue is full, calls
`handleError()`, which drops the record or prints to stderr
([logging.handlers](https://docs.python.org/3/library/logging.handlers.html), read 2026-10-12).

One surprise from the run: with the bounded queue still full, `QueueListener.stop()` itself raised
`queue.Full`, because it puts its own stop marker on the queue with `put_nowait()`. That happened in one
of two runs. It is the kind of detail that shows you have actually run the thing.

**Fix and prevent.** To stabilize: lower the log level or sample the noisy line, free space by truncating
(not deleting), and restart the shipper if it is stuck. To prevent: log asynchronously through a bounded
buffer, export the drop counter as a metric, rate-limit repeated error messages, rotate log files by size,
and alert on how long until the disk is full. At an assumed 3 MB/s of logs and 100 GB free, that is about
**9.3 hours**.

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the numbers (for a Staff-level follow-up)</summary>

- **Sink ceiling:** 1 line ÷ 2 ms = 500 lines/s. At 5 lines per request: 500 ÷ 5 = **100 requests/s**,
  however many threads.
- **Why the median is about 200 ms:** Little's law on the request path. 16 requests in flight ÷ 79
  requests/s ≈ 0.20 s ≈ **200 ms** each, against 1 ms of real work. The measured p50 was 201 ms.
- **Time until the disk is full:** 100 × 10⁹ bytes ÷ (3 × 10⁶ bytes/s) ≈ 33,000 s ≈ **9.3 hours** (both
  inputs assumed).

</details>

### 2. CPU high, or CPU throttled (Together AI)

"CPU looks high" has at least three meanings, and each needs a different fix. Run these, in this order:

```bash
uptime                       # load averages: runnable + uninterruptible tasks
vmstat 1                     # r (run queue) vs CPU count; b (blocked); wa; st (steal)
mpstat -P ALL 1              # one core at 100% while others idle = a single hot thread
pidstat -u 1; pidstat -t -p <pid> 1   # which process, which thread
perf top -p <pid>            # native code; py-spy top --pid <pid> for Python
cat /sys/fs/cgroup/cpu.max /sys/fs/cgroup/cpu.stat   # inside the container (cgroup v2)
```

A few columns need a word. In `vmstat`, `r` is the **run queue**, the number of threads waiting for a CPU;
compare it with the number of CPUs. `b` is the number blocked on I/O. `wa` is CPU time spent idle while
waiting for I/O. `st` is **steal**, time a virtual machine wanted the CPU but the hypervisor gave it to
another guest. `perf` and `py-spy` are **profilers**: they sample what code is running so you can see the
hot function.

**Load average is not CPU use.** The **load average** that `uptime` shows is the average number of tasks
that are either running, waiting for a CPU, or in "uninterruptible sleep". That last state usually means
waiting on a disk or a network filesystem. So a high load with low CPU busy points at I/O, not compute.
Check the `b` column and `wa` in `vmstat`. Say it like this: "On Linux, load average counts tasks stuck on
disk too, so high load and idle CPUs means I look at I/O next."

**One saturated thread.** If `mpstat` shows one core pinned at 100% while the rest idle, something is
single-threaded: an event loop, Python's global interpreter lock (which lets only one thread run Python
code at a time), or one thread holding a lock everyone needs. More cores won't help. More processes, or
less work per request, will.

**Throttled while the host looks idle.** This is the container case from the core concepts, with numbers.
A container limited to 2 CPUs has `cpu.max` = "200000 100000": 200 ms of CPU time in every 100 ms period.
Suppose 16 threads become runnable at once. Together they use up that 200 ms in **12.5 ms** of wall-clock
time. Then the kernel stops the whole container for the remaining **87.5 ms** of the period. The node's
average CPU looks low. The container's p99 grows by up to 87.5 ms every period it crosses. `nr_throttled`
rising against `nr_periods` confirms it. The fixes: raise or remove the CPU limit, cut the thread count to
match the quota, or move the work elsewhere.

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the numbers (for a Staff-level follow-up)</summary>

- **Quota:** 2 CPUs × 100 ms period = 200 ms of CPU time per period, written "200000 100000" in
  microseconds.
- **Time to spend it:** 16 threads running in parallel burn 16 ms of CPU per 1 ms of wall time, so
  200 ÷ 16 = **12.5 ms**.
- **Time stopped:** 100 − 12.5 = **87.5 ms** of the period with no CPU at all.

</details>

### 3. Storage I/O (Together AI)

```bash
iostat -xz 1        # per device: r/s w/s, rkB/s wkB/s, r_await w_await, aqu-sz, %util
pidstat -d 1        # per process read/write rates; or iotop
cat /proc/<pid>/io  # bytes this process actually caused to hit storage
strace -f -e trace=fsync,fdatasync -p <pid>    # is the app syncing on every write?
```

In `iostat`, `r/s` and `w/s` are reads and writes per second. `r_await` and `w_await` are the average time
each one took, including time waiting in the queue. `aqu-sz` is the average queue size. `%util` is the
column people misread. `strace` here watches for `fsync`, the call that forces data to disk; an app that
syncs on every write can be slow on any disk.

**`%util` is not saturation on every device.** `%util` is the fraction of time the device had *at least
one* request outstanding. For a single spinning disk that does one thing at a time, 100% means busy. An
SSD or a RAID set serves many requests in parallel, so it can show 100% while it still has room for more.
Use latency (`r_await`, `w_await`) and queue size (`aqu-sz`) instead, and cross-check them with Little's
law. Say it like this: "`%util` tells me the disk was never idle, not that it was full. I look at wait
time and queue size."

**Is the wait in the device or above it?** That is another listed follow-up. Compare the latency the
application measures around its own I/O calls with the device's `await`. If the application sees 50 ms and
the device says 2 ms, the time is being spent above the device: in the application, a filesystem lock or a
connection pool.

**Remote storage.** Network volumes have their own throughput and operation limits per volume, set by the
cloud provider. Check the volume's configured limits before blaming the disk. The symptom is latency that
rises sharply at a fixed throughput: the volume has hit its cap.

### 4. Slow network inside containers (Together AI)

```bash
iperf3 -s; iperf3 -c <host> -P 1; iperf3 -c <host> -P 8   # host-to-host, then pod-to-pod; 1 vs 8 streams
ss -ti dst <peer>                                         # per-connection rtt, cwnd, retransmits
ping -M do -s 1472 <peer>                                 # 1472 + 28 header bytes = 1500: does a full frame pass?
mpstat -P ALL 1                                           # %soft: one core spent on network interrupts
conntrack -S                                              # connection-tracking drops (kube-proxy iptables mode)
```

A few terms first. `iperf3` measures raw throughput between two machines. **RTT** is round-trip time. The
**congestion window** (`cwnd` in `ss`) is how much data TCP lets itself send before waiting for an
acknowledgement. **MTU** is the largest packet a link carries, usually 1,500 bytes. Containers often talk
over an **overlay network** that wraps each packet in another header, so the usable size shrinks. The
`ping` line sends a full-size packet that must not be split, to test whether one fits. `%soft` in `mpstat`
is time a core spends on deferred network-interrupt work. `conntrack` is the kernel's connection-tracking
table, which kube-proxy uses in its iptables mode; when it fills, new connections are dropped.

Compare layers before blaming the overlay: run the same test host-to-host, node-to-pod and pod-to-pod. If
one stream is slow and eight streams are fast, it is a limit per connection, not the network. One TCP
connection can never have more data in flight than its window, and it gets one window's worth through per
round trip:

<Formula
  title="One TCP connection's ceiling"
  expr="throughput ≤ window ÷ RTT"
  vars={[
    { sym: 'window', meaning: 'bytes in flight without acknowledgement (receive window or congestion window)' },
    { sym: 'RTT', meaning: 'round-trip time' },
  ]}
  example="An assumed 64 KiB window: at 1 ms RTT, 65,536 ÷ 0.001 ≈ 65.5 MB/s (524 Mbit/s); at 10 ms, 6.6 MB/s"
/>

So with an assumed 64 KiB window and 10 ms of RTT, one connection tops out near **6.6 MB/s**, however fast
the link. Say it like this: "One connection is capped at window divided by round-trip time. If eight
streams are fast and one is slow, the network is fine."

**A CPU problem can look like a network problem.** If the sender is throttled, or one core is saturated by
interrupts or by encryption (TLS), the link sits idle while throughput stays flat. Packet loss from an MTU
mismatch on an overlay shows up as retransmits in `ss -ti`.

### 5. An overloaded Kubernetes cluster (Perplexity)

The symptom: high p95 and p99 latency, timeouts, and autoscaling that keeps adding and removing pods. The
autoscaler here is the **HPA** (Horizontal Pod Autoscaler), which adds or removes pod copies to keep a
metric such as average CPU near a target. "Thrash" means it flips up and down without settling.

```bash
kubectl top nodes; kubectl top pods -A --sort-by=cpu        # needs metrics-server
kubectl describe node <node>          # Conditions (MemoryPressure, DiskPressure, PIDPressure), Allocated resources
kubectl get events -A --sort-by=.lastTimestamp | tail -50
kubectl describe hpa <name>           # current vs target metric, recent scaling events
kubectl get pods -o wide | grep -v Running
```

**Guesses to test one at a time.**

- **Throttling plus CPU-based autoscaling.** Pods hit their CPU limit and latency rises. The HPA adds pods.
  New pods take time to become ready. Load drops, the HPA removes them, and the cycle repeats. Kubernetes'
  HPA page says its stabilization window "is used to restrict the flapping of replica count", with a
  default of 300 seconds for scale-down
  ([HPA](https://kubernetes.io/docs/concepts/workloads/autoscaling/horizontal-pod-autoscale/), read
  2026-10-12). Put scaling events and latency on one graph and see whether they move together.
- **A readiness cascade.** A **readiness probe** is the check Kubernetes runs to decide whether a pod
  should receive traffic. Under load the probe times out, the pod leaves the Service, and the remaining
  pods get more load and fail their probes too. Probes that call a database make this worse.
- **Retry amplification.** If each layer makes up to three attempts, two such layers can send the slowest
  layer up to **nine times** the traffic (3 × 3). Retries turn a slowdown into an outage.
- **Overcommitted nodes.** If requests are set far below real usage, the scheduler packs too many pods on
  a node, and they then fight over CPU or memory.

**Mitigate first:** raise the HPA minimum so it stops removing pods, shed load at the edge, turn off
retries in the inner layers, and cap how many requests each pod handles at once. **Then fix:** set requests
from measured usage, autoscale on a signal closer to the user (queue depth, requests in flight, latency)
rather than CPU alone, keep liveness probes cheap and readiness probes independent of downstreams, and add
a **retry budget** (a cap on retries as a share of total traffic). Say it like this: "Stop the feedback
loop first, then find which loop it was."

### 6. A pod stuck in Pending (CoreWeave)

```bash
kubectl get pod <pod> -o wide           # NODE column empty: unscheduled. Set: pulling images or mounting volumes
kubectl describe pod <pod>              # Events at the bottom: FailedScheduling and its reasons
kubectl describe node <node> | sed -n '/Capacity/,/Allocated resources/p'   # nvidia.com/gpu capacity vs allocatable
kubectl get nodes -L <gpu-label>        # which nodes carry the GPU label your selector asks for
kubectl describe node <node> | grep -i taint
kubectl get pvc; kubectl describe pvc <claim>          # an unbound claim, or one bound in another zone
kubectl get pods -n kube-system | grep -i -E 'nvidia|device-plugin'   # is the GPU device plugin running?
```

The first line splits the problem in two, as the core concepts explained. If the pod has a node, look at
image pulls and volume mounts there. If not, read why the scheduler said no.

Kubernetes' debugging page says a Pending pod "can not be scheduled onto a node", usually from
insufficient resources, and points at the scheduler's messages in `kubectl describe`
([debug pods](https://kubernetes.io/docs/tasks/debug/debug-application/debug-pods/), read 2026-10-12).
The FailedScheduling event lists how many nodes each reason ruled out; the exact wording varies by
version. The usual reasons are these:

- **Not enough requested CPU, memory or GPU** on any single node.
- **A taint the pod doesn't tolerate.** A **taint** is a mark on a node that repels pods unless they carry
  a matching **toleration**. GPU nodes are often tainted so ordinary pods stay off them.
- **A `nodeSelector` or affinity rule** (a rule saying which nodes the pod may run on) that no node matches.
- **An unbound volume claim.** A **PVC** (PersistentVolumeClaim) is a pod's request for storage. If it
  isn't bound, or is bound to a disk in another zone, the pod can't start where the disk isn't.
- **A `hostPort` conflict**, where two pods want the same port on the node.

**GPU specifics.** Kubernetes doesn't know about GPUs by itself. A vendor's **device plugin**, a small
agent on each node, tells Kubernetes how many GPUs the node has. The docs: "your cluster exposes a custom
schedulable resource such as `amd.com/gpu` or `nvidia.com/gpu`", and GPUs "are only supposed to be
specified in the `limits` section"
([scheduling GPUs](https://kubernetes.io/docs/tasks/manage-gpus/scheduling-gpus/), read 2026-10-12). If
the device plugin isn't running on a node, that node advertises no GPUs. Every GPU pod then reports
insufficient GPUs, even on a GPU machine.

**Fragmentation.** Take four nodes with 8 GPUs each, and 7 in use on every node. There are 4 GPUs free in
total, yet a pod asking for 2 stays Pending, because no single node has 2 free. Say it like this: "Totals
across the cluster don't schedule pods. Free space on one node does." If a **cluster autoscaler** (the
component that adds nodes) is installed, its events on the pod say whether it tried to add a node and why
it didn't: no node group fits, a quota, or no capacity.

### 7. The slow notebook behind Kafka (NVIDIA)

Here the system is the problem, so draw it first. A plausible reading of the setup, to confirm with the
interviewer:

```
Jupyter notebook (runs the experiment)
   │  publishes results or metrics
   ▼
Kafka topic
   │
   ▼
Consumer ── reads results, writes them to a store
   │
   ▼
Results store
   │  queried by
   ▼
Experiment dashboard
```

**The notebook** is an interactive Python session where the ML engineer runs the experiment. It can be
slow on its own: slow Python, or a GPU that sits idle. **Kafka** carries the results. **The consumer**
reads them and writes them down; if it falls behind, lag grows. **The store** keeps the results. **The
dashboard** queries the store; an unindexed query over every experiment ever run can be the whole problem.

Pull the path apart first, as the reporting candidate did. Ask which part the ML engineer means by
"slow", then time each hop:

```bash
kafka-consumer-groups.sh --bootstrap-server <b> --describe --group <dashboard-consumer>   # LAG per partition
py-spy dump --pid <kernel-pid>         # what the notebook's Python is doing right now
nvidia-smi dmon -s u                   # is the GPU busy at all while the experiment "runs"?
```

**Guesses, each ruled in or out by one measurement above.** Consumer lag is growing, because there are too
few consumers for the partitions or each message takes a slow write. The notebook rereads the whole topic
on every run: a new consumer group with no committed offset starts from the beginning in kafka-lite, whose
`FetchOffset` returns 0 for a group with no commit; real clients follow their offset-reset setting. The
dashboard runs an unindexed query over all results. Or the experiment itself is CPU-bound in Python, or
copies data off the GPU every step. The breadcrumbs the candidate describes are the interviewer reacting
to which hop you measure first. Say it like this: "Lag takes seconds to check, so I check it first. If it's
zero, Kafka is not the problem."

### 8. A training data pipeline: fix it, then make it fast (Anthropic)

A **data pipeline** for training reads raw examples, turns words into numbers the model can use, groups
them into **batches** (here, 64 examples each), and feeds them to the model. One full pass over the data is
an **epoch**. Turning words into numbers needs a **vocabulary**, a table from each word to an id.

The report doesn't say what the bugs were, so here is a practice version, built for this page and run on
2026-10-12: 20,000 JSON lines of 40 words each, batches of 64, with two correctness bugs and one hot spot.

```python
def encode(text, texts):
    vocab = build_vocab(texts)              # HOT SPOT: rebuilt for every example
    return [vocab[w] for w in text.split()]

def batches(path, batch_size=64, seed=0):
    texts, labels = load(path)
    random.Random(seed).shuffle(texts)      # BUG 1: labels not shuffled with texts
    random.Random(seed + 1).shuffle(labels)
    for i in range(0, len(texts) - batch_size, batch_size):   # BUG 2: drops the tail
        xs = [encode(t, texts) for t in texts[i:i + batch_size]]
        yield xs, labels[i:i + batch_size]
```

**Correctness first.** Bug 1 shuffles texts and labels separately, so the model trains on random labels.
The loss won't fall below chance, and nothing crashes. You catch it by asserting that a few examples still
carry their original labels after batching. Bug 2 stops the loop one batch early. It silently drops the
last 32 examples (312 batches instead of 313), and drops a full batch whenever the count is a multiple of
64. You catch it by checking that examples per epoch equal the dataset size. Say it like this: "The bugs
that cost the round are the ones that don't crash, so I check invariants, not just exceptions."

**Then measure the input pipeline alone,** before blaming the model or the GPU. `cProfile` is Python's
built-in profiler. Its trimmed output over two batches:

```text
slow: 5 batches in 26.02s
   ncalls  tottime  cumtime  filename:lineno(function)
      128   0.001   42.637  pipeline_slow.py:24(encode)
      128  20.916   42.636  pipeline_slow.py:17(build_vocab)
102400000  13.548   13.548  {method 'setdefault' of 'dict' objects}
fixed: all 313 batches (one epoch, 20,000 examples) in 0.18s
```

Read it like this: `encode` was called 128 times (two batches of 64), and almost all its time is inside
`build_vocab`, which made over 100 million dictionary calls. Profiling slowed it about fourfold here (21 s
per batch profiled against 5.2 s without). Without the profiler, five batches took 26 s, about **81 ms per
example**, so one epoch would take about **27 minutes**. The fixed version builds the vocabulary and
encodes once, and does the whole epoch in **0.18 s**. Cache the encoded data to disk so the next epoch and
the next run skip it.

**Then the loader.** In PyTorch, the `DataLoader` that feeds batches to the model has a `num_workers`
setting. It defaults to 0, and "0 means that the data will be loaded in the main process".
`pin_memory=True` copies batches into page-locked ("pinned") memory for faster host-to-GPU transfer
([torch.utils.data](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/2.14/data.html), read 2026-10-12). Here is the sign to
look for: GPU utilization drops between training steps while one CPU core is pinned at 100%. The GPU is
waiting on input. More workers, prefetching and pre-tokenized data fix it; a bigger GPU doesn't.

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the numbers (for a Staff-level follow-up)</summary>

- **Batches per epoch:** 20,000 ÷ 64 = 312.5, so 313 batches with the last one holding 32 examples. The
  buggy `range(0, 20,000 − 64, 64)` stops before index 19,968, giving 312 batches and dropping those 32.
- **Per example:** 26.02 s ÷ 5 batches ≈ 5.2 s per batch; 5.2 s ÷ 64 ≈ **81 ms per example**.
- **Per epoch:** 20,000 × 0.081 s ≈ 1,620 s ≈ **27 minutes**, against 0.18 s once fixed.

</details>

## Common follow-ups in depth

### "I deleted the log file and the disk is still full"

The process still holds the file open, so the space stays allocated. `lsof +L1` lists deleted-but-open
files. Truncate through the descriptor (`: > /proc/<pid>/fd/<n>`) or make the process reopen its logs.
Log rotation by `copytruncate` (copy the file, then empty it in place) avoids this, at the cost of possibly
losing lines written during the copy.

### "What happens if the remote log sink slows down?"

It depends on whether logging blocks. Synchronous logging turns sink latency into request latency: that is
the 79 requests/s row in case 1. An unbounded buffer turns it into memory growth and, eventually, an OOM
kill. A bounded buffer turns it into dropped lines. That is usually the right choice, as long as drops are
counted and alerted on. Decide which logs may never drop, such as audit logs, and send those through a
separate, durable path.

### "High load average, low CPU utilization"

Tasks in uninterruptible sleep count toward load. Look for blocked processes (`vmstat`'s `b` column, or
`ps` showing state `D`), slow disks or a network filesystem, and `wa` time.

### "How do you know the fix worked?"

Take the same measurement before and after, under the same load. Take the user-facing number (p99, time
per epoch, lag) and the resource number that explained it (throttled periods, queue length, dropped
lines). Then add the alert that would have caught it earlier.

### "Why is the GPU pod Pending when the cluster has free GPUs?"

Per-node fragmentation, a taint the pod doesn't tolerate, a selector for a GPU type that is full, or a node
whose device plugin isn't advertising GPUs. Each is visible in the pod's events and the node's allocatable
resources.

<AINative title="Agents during an incident: fast readers, confident guessers">
An agent is useful for the reading: summarizing a thousand lines of events, explaining an unfamiliar
`iostat` column, writing the `kubectl` or PromQL (Prometheus metrics) query you half remember, diffing two configs. It is
dangerous in two ways. It will produce a root cause from one symptom with full confidence. And it will
suggest commands that change things (restart, delete the pod, raise the limit), which destroy the evidence
or widen the damage. Give it read-only credentials, as this guide's small-team defaults recommend for agent
tools. Ask it for a ranked list of guesses and, for each, the command whose output would rule it out. Then
run the command yourself and read the output yourself. A flag it invents fails loudly. A plausible
misreading of `%util` or load average fails silently, and that is the one to check.
</AINative>

## Small-team version

At a 50–500 person company, the AI engineer is often the person paged when the GPU pod is Pending or the
ingestion job crawls. There is no separate SRE team. So the playbook has to fit one person and one hour.

### Where to look first, in order

1. **Is it us or the provider?** Look at error rate and latency **per provider and model** on your own
   dashboard, then at the provider's status page. Provider outages are a routine failure for small teams.
   OpenAI's 2024-12-11 incident, for example, lasted about 4 h 22 m and was triggered when a new telemetry
   service overloaded Kubernetes control planes
   ([status page](https://status.openai.com/incidents/ctrsv3lwd797)).
2. **What changed?** Deploys, prompt or model version, config, and traffic by tenant, in the last hour.
3. **RED per endpoint**, split into queue wait, time to first token (how long until the model's first word
   comes back), generation time and tool calls, plus **tokens** per request. A token is the unit models
   read and write, roughly three-quarters of a word. A latency jump with flat tokens is infrastructure. A
   jump with doubled tokens is a prompt or retrieval change.
4. **USE on the box or pod**: CPU throttling, memory, GPU utilization, disk, and the software resources
   (worker pools, connection pools, how many calls the provider lets you make at once).

### Cheap defaults to have before the first incident

- Structured JSON logs with a request id, at INFO level, through an asynchronous handler with a bounded
  buffer and a drop counter. Sample debug logs instead of turning them on everywhere.
- RED metrics per endpoint and per provider, plus a spend metric. Alert on p95 latency, error rate, the
  provider's rate-limit rejections (HTTP 429) and daily spend.
- Traces from day one, with tokens and cost per request (OpenTelemetry, or an LLM tracing tool such as
  Langfuse, which is open source and can be self-hosted).
- **Quality checks, not just error rates.** Anthropic's September 2025 postmortem describes three
  infrastructure bugs that degraded output quality while standard benchmarks, safety evals, spot checks and
  canaries missed them ([postmortem](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/a-postmortem-of-three-recent-issues),
  2025-09-17). A small team's version: a handful of fixed prompts with checked answers, run on a schedule
  against production.

### Build, buy, skip

- **Buy hosted metrics and logs** while volume is small. You have no time to run a logging cluster. Size
  the bill from volume first: at an assumed 2M requests a day, 20 lines each, 300 bytes a line, that is
  **12 GB of logs a day**, times your vendor's price per GB (check it; it varies widely).
- **Skip running your own GPU Kubernetes cluster** for one or two models. A managed endpoint or a
  per-second serverless GPU platform removes the whole Pending-pod and device-plugin class of problems.
  For example, Modal listed an H100 at $0.001097 per second (about **$3.95 an hour**) as of 2026-10-11
  ([pricing](https://modal.com/pricing)). Revisit when your GPUs run near full all day, or when data
  residency rules require your own nodes.
- **Build** the runbook: the four steps above, the commands on this page that apply to your stack, and who
  can approve a restart or a rollback.

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the numbers (for a Staff-level follow-up)</summary>

- **Log volume:** GB/day = requests/day × lines per request × bytes per line ÷ 10⁹. With the assumed
  inputs: 2 × 10⁶ × 20 × 300 = 1.2 × 10¹⁰ bytes = **12 GB/day**.
- **Hourly GPU price:** $0.001097 × 3,600 s ≈ **$3.95 an hour**.

</details>

### What you still own

The alert thresholds, the decision to drop logs rather than block, the rollback, and the change after the
incident that would have caught it earlier. An agent can draft the postmortem; you decide what changes.

<FollowUps>
  <FollowUp q="Can deleting an open log file free the expected space immediately? (Listed on the PracHub page, Together AI)">
    No. The file's data stays allocated until the last process that has it open closes it. Find it with
    `lsof +L1`, truncate through `/proc/<pid>/fd`, or make the process reopen its log.
  </FollowUp>
  <FollowUp q="How can container CPU quotas affect a host that appears to have spare capacity? (Listed on the PracHub page, Together AI)">
    The quota is per period. A 2-CPU limit spent by 16 threads in 12.5 ms leaves the container stopped
    for the remaining 87.5 ms of each 100 ms period, while the host's average CPU looks low. `cpu.stat`'s
    `nr_throttled` shows it.
  </FollowUp>
  <FollowUp q="Why might a single connection underuse available bandwidth? (Listed on the PracHub page, Together AI)">
    It can't exceed window ÷ RTT. A 64 KiB window at 10 ms RTT caps it near 6.6 MB/s. Compare one stream
    with eight in `iperf3`, and check `ss -ti` for retransmits and window size.
  </FollowUp>
  <FollowUp q="Tail latency, timeouts and autoscaling thrash: what do you mitigate first? (Listed on the PracHub page, Perplexity)">
    Stop the thrash and the amplification: raise the HPA minimum, shed or rate-limit at the edge, disable
    inner-layer retries. Then find the cause (throttling, readiness cascade, overcommitted nodes) with
    events and HPA history on one timeline.
  </FollowUp>

  </FollowUp>

    Ask what "slow" means and for whom, draw the path (notebook, Kafka, consumer, store, dashboard), and

  </FollowUp>

## Common mistakes

- **Proposing a fix before defining "slow".** You end up fixing the wrong hop; the NVIDIA prompt was vague
  on purpose, and the reporting candidate pulled it apart first <Cite id="s-79598dd069" />.
- **Treating utilization as saturation.** `%util` at 100% on an SSD, or a host at 30% CPU with throttled
  containers, both mislead; queues and wait times are what predict latency.
- **Deleting the open log file.** It frees nothing while a process holds it open; truncate it or make the
  process reopen it.
- **Totals instead of per-node numbers.** Free GPUs spread across the cluster don't schedule a 2-GPU pod;
  only free space on one node does.
- **Blaming the GPU for an input-bound job.** A GPU waiting on a slow loader won't get faster by being
  bigger; measure the data pipeline alone first.
- **Changing production while diagnosing.** Restarting the pod destroys the evidence; capture events,
  profiles and logs first.
- **Running out of time on the fix.** The Anthropic candidate ran out of time on the debugging
  <Cite id="s-6f56a76a5b" />; fix the correctness bugs first, then state the slowness guess and the
  measurement you'd run next even if you can't finish it.

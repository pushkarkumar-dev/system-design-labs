## The prompt

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> For a personal question: "Here is one true, fairly small story. Two things I cared about pulled against each
> other. Here is what I did, including the part I'm not proud of, how it felt at the time, and what I do
> differently now."
> For a release or safety question: "I'd answer as the owner. I'd name who could get hurt and how, the tests that
> must pass before release, who can block it, how we roll out in stages, what makes us roll back, and which risks
> we accept on purpose, with a name next to each one."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer, so you can say it with understanding.

This family has two halves, and interviewers move between them in the same round.

The first half is **personal**. You are asked to describe a moral conflict you had with your work, a time you
built something that conflicted with your values, or a time delivery pressure ran against a security or safety
concern.

The second half is **judgment**. You are asked what criteria you would use to decide whether to release a new AI
model, or how you would approach safety in a consumer product that uses generative AI (**GenAI**: models that
produce text, images or other content, as opposed to models that only classify or rank).

### What makes this different from a normal behavioral round

You have probably answered "tell me about a conflict" before. In a normal behavioral round the interviewer wants
a result: what you did and how it turned out. The usual shape is **STAR** (situation, task, action, result), and
the best stories end with a win.

The values round reportedly wants something else. It cares less about the result and more about your thinking
before and after: what you noticed, what pulled at you, how it felt, and how you see it now. A story where you
were obviously right and won is weak here, because there was no real tension in it. A story with a compromise
you still think about is often stronger.

### What makes this different from a normal launch decision

The judgment half looks like a normal release question, and much of what you know carries over: staged rollouts,
feature flags, rollback plans, an on-call rotation. Three things are different with AI.

1. **The output is not fixed.** A **large language model (LLM)** is a program that reads text and writes text.
   Ask it the same question twice and you can get two different answers. So you cannot test it with exact
   expected outputs. You test it with an **eval**: a fixed set of test inputs plus a scoring rule, run against
   every new model or prompt version. Think of a regression suite whose results are percentages, not green or
   red.
2. **The failure is often behavior, not a crash.** The model can be polite, fast and wrong. It can give dangerous
   advice, leak private data, or flatter users into bad decisions. None of that shows up in an error rate.
3. **Good metrics can hide bad behavior.** Users can like an answer that is bad for them. The clearest public
   example, covered below, is a model update that users rated well and that was rolled back within days.

So the judgment half tests whether you can turn "safety matters" into a plan with gates, owners and a stop
button.

## What is being tested

### What the values round reportedly feels like

The **values round** is a dedicated interview at Anthropic about how you reason through ethical and safety
questions. A Staff infra candidate describes it as "explicitly about values, safety, and how I reason through
moral tension" (<Cite id="s-e9fee3985a" />).

The most detailed account comes from a candidate for Anthropic's Safeguards team (the team that works on
preventing misuse). Their round was run by non-technical interviewers and "felt almost like a therapy session
because they kept probing how I felt, not just what I did." The interviewer "kept pushing on how I felt then and
how I feel now" and seemed to want the candidate "to name the discomfort instead of rationalizing it away." The
candidate's own reading: "I think they were testing whether I was emotionally competent and actually reflective,
not just polished." The recruiter had warned them that "the company values round was the one where people usually
fail." At the time of writing the outcome was still pending (<Cite id="s-42f1c7f6dd" />).

A poster on Blind (an anonymous forum for tech employees), interviewing for Senior Staff, reports being asked "What kind of work do you dislike most?" and
"Have you ever done something morally wrong?" by a non-technical interviewer. He writes "I think the culture round
may have been the reason" for his rejection, and that his examples "probably contained too much engineering
detail." One commenter replies "Yeah, I think this is very common." (<Cite id="s-e98cbef9cd" />).

### Does this round decide outcomes?

Put plainly, the evidence that this round sinks candidates is thin: one recruiter warning, one candidate's own
belief, and one commenter. It all points the same way, and it matches how many prep sites treat the round. Treat
it as a round you can fail, and prepare for it like one.

One caution about an account people often cite. The Staff infra candidate quoted above describes the values
content, but says "I came out feeling good about everything except coding" and blames the coding round, not the
values round (<Cite id="s-e9fee3985a" />). Use that account for what the round asks, not as evidence that it
sinks people.

### Outside Anthropic

The evidence for OpenAI and Google is thinner. Hello Interview's OpenAI guide lists the release-criteria question
among its behavioral samples, next to "Tell me about a time you had to consider AI safety implications in your
work and how you addressed them". It suggests reading OpenAI's Charter and the team's recent work before the
loop, rather than preparing a speech (<Cite id="s-4d55d9b987" />).

The consumer-safety question (eng-109) appears on an aggregator list tagged OpenAI, Anthropic and Google, with no
detail about which round it came from (<Cite id="s-d7a8e88bf0" />). So prepare it as a judgment question that
could come up in a behavioral, hiring-manager or product-sense slot. Expect the interviewer to push on a concrete
product, not on principles.

### The signals, in plain terms

- **Honesty over polish.** The Aced list (the aggregator mentioned above) advises "honest, specific examples ready, not rehearsed mission-speak"
  (<Cite id="s-d7a8e88bf0" />). Hello Interview's OpenAI guide warns against "a rehearsed speech about AGI"
  (<Cite id="s-4d55d9b987" />), meaning artificial general intelligence. **Mission-speak** means repeating the company's stated mission back to it
  instead of telling a story.
- **Reflection.** Not only what you did, but how it felt then, how you see it now, and what you would do
  differently (<Cite id="s-42f1c7f6dd" />).
- **The right altitude.** The Blind poster suspects too much engineering detail hurt him
  (<Cite id="s-e98cbef9cd" />). This round is about the person, not the system. Compare the hiring-manager
  round in [Drive decisions](/system-design-labs/interviews/walkthroughs/bh-drive-decisions/), where the
  opposite is reported.
- **Judgment you can turn into practice.** For the release and consumer-safety questions: concrete gates, named
  owners, and what would make you stop. "Safety is important" earns nothing.

Say it like this when you sense the interviewer wants more feeling: "Honestly, I was frustrated, and I was also a
bit scared of being the person who slows the launch. That second feeling is the one I had to work on."

## What a strong story contains

### For the personal questions (infra-107, eng-108)

1. **A real tension.** Two things you valued pulled against each other: shipping for the team versus a risk to
   users; loyalty to a manager versus a concern you couldn't get heard; a customer's request versus how its
   output would be used. If one side was obviously right, there was no conflict.
2. **Stakes for someone other than you.** Users, customers, colleagues, the public.
3. **What you actually did, including what you didn't do.** Many honest stories include a compromise you are
   not fully proud of. That is acceptable, and often stronger, as long as you say so.
4. **The feeling, named.** Discomfort, frustration, fear of what would happen if you spoke up. One or two
   sentences, without performing it.
5. **How you see it now.** What you would repeat, what you would change, and the line you now hold that you
   didn't hold then.
6. **Proportion.** A small conflict told honestly beats an inflated one. You don't need to have been a
   whistle-blower.

### For the judgment questions (eng-109, eng-112)

A few more words first, because the judgment answers use them:

- **Harm**: a specific bad outcome for a person, such as dangerous advice, harassment, a privacy leak or fraud.
  Companies group harms into **policy areas** (for example "self-harm" or "weapons") and write rules per area.
- **Red-teaming**: people (inside or hired) deliberately trying to make the model misbehave before release, the
  way a penetration test attacks a web app.
- **Over-refusal**: the model refuses a legitimate request ("I can't help with that") because it looks a bit like
  a harmful one. It is the AI version of a false positive, and it costs you users.
- **Prompt injection**: instructions hidden inside content the model reads (an uploaded file, a web page, an
  email) that make it do something its owner didn't intend. It is like SQL injection, except there is no
  parameterized-query fix.

With those, a strong judgment answer has:

1. **A framework with gates, not adjectives.** What must be true before release, who checks it, what blocks it.
2. **Specific harms named.** For a consumer product: harmful content, dangerous advice, minors, privacy leaks,
   impersonation, prompt injection through user-supplied content, and over-reliance (users trusting a wrong
   answer).
3. **Measurement.** Evals per harm, red-team results, a threshold, a comparison with the previous version.
4. **Post-release.** Monitoring, abuse reporting, a rollback path, an incident process.
5. **Trade-offs said out loud.** Over-refusal is a cost too. Delay has a cost. Some risks are accepted, and should
   be written down as accepted.
6. **Humility about what evals miss.** A real example of evals missing something is the strongest material here
   (see the GPT-4o case below).

## Story structure

### Personal stories: the reflective arc

Use a five-part arc instead of plain STAR, because the round reportedly cares about the before and after of
your thinking (<Cite id="s-42f1c7f6dd" />):

1. **Context (two sentences).** Where you were, what was being built, the deadline.
2. **The tension (two or three sentences).** What pulled against what, and why both mattered.
3. **What you did (three or four sentences).** Who you raised it with, how, and what happened.
4. **How it felt then (one or two sentences).**
5. **How you see it now (two or three sentences).** What you'd keep, what you'd change, the principle you took
   from it.

Keep technical detail to what the listener needs to understand the stakes. If they want more, they'll ask.

### One story, start to finish

Here is one story told through the arc, with a running clock. The clock is a suggested pace for a first answer of
about two and a half minutes, not a rule from any source. The story is illustrative: every bracket is a fact only
you can supply.

| Clock | Part | What you say (illustrative) | Why it works |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0:00 | **Context** | "We were [two weeks] from launching [a feature that let customers connect their own data sources]. I was [the engineer on the ingestion path]." | Two sentences. The listener knows the deadline and your role. |
| 0:15 | **Tension** | "I found that [the connector stored customer credentials with broader access than it needed]. Fixing it properly meant [a week]. My manager had [a signed customer date]." | Both sides are real: a customer promise and a risk to customers' data. |
| 0:45 | **What you did** | "I wrote a one-page risk note with [the worst realistic outcome] and two options: ship a narrower version, or slip a week. I took it to [my manager and the security lead]. We shipped on time with the narrower version, and the full version shipped [later]." | You brought evidence and options, not just an objection. |
| 1:30 | **How it felt** | "I was worried about being the person who slowed the launch, and I nearly let it go." | One honest sentence. It admits the pull toward staying quiet. |
| 1:45 | **How you see it now** | "I'd raise it earlier, before the date was public. Since then I [add a security review to the launch checklist at design time], so nobody has to find this two weeks out." | Reflection plus a change to the process. The last clause is the Staff signal. |

Then stop and let the interviewer push. They will probably ask "how did you feel about your manager?" or "would
you have shipped the full version if they had insisted?". Those follow-ups are where the round happens.

### Finding your stories

Most engineers say they have no moral-conflict story, then find several once they look for the right shape. Go
through your last few roles with these prompts and write one line for each hit:

- A launch you were uneasy about, whether or not you said anything.
- Data used in a way users probably didn't expect.
- A shortcut on security, privacy, testing or accessibility taken to meet a date.
- A customer request you pushed back on because of how its output would be used.
- A metric you were asked to move, where the easiest way to move it was bad for users.
- A time you stayed quiet and later wished you hadn't.

The last one is often the best story, because the reflection is real. You don't need a story where you prevented
harm. You need one where you can describe the tension honestly and show that it changed how you act.

### How much to disclose

Personal does not mean confidential. Describe the situation at the level of "a fintech product", "a health
customer", "a manager two levels up". Never name the people involved or reveal your employer's confidential
details. An interviewer at a safety-focused company will notice if you do, and it undercuts the story. If a true
story can't be told without confidential detail, pick another.

Also calibrate the emotional part. The Safeguards account describes an interviewer probing feelings
(<Cite id="s-42f1c7f6dd" />), but that is a request for honesty, not for drama. One plain sentence is enough,
followed by what you did with it: "I was angry, and I was also afraid of being seen as the person who blocks
launches."

### Judgment questions: a decision procedure

For "would you release this model?" and "how do you approach consumer GenAI safety?", answer as an owner, not as
a commentator. A **release** here means any change to what the model does for users: a new model, a new model
version from your vendor, or a new **system prompt** (the instructions your product puts in front of every
conversation).

Picture the release as a pipeline with a gate between each stage:

```
New model or prompt version
   │  Gate 1: evals pass (quality no worse, harm checks no worse)
   │  Gate 2: red-team findings triaged; policy and legal sign-off
   ▼
Internal use ── employees only
   │  Gate 3: no unexplained reports from internal users
   ▼
Trusted testers ── a small outside group who expect rough edges
   │  Gate 4: tester concerns explained or fixed
   ▼
Percentage rollout ── e.g. 1% → 10% → 50% of users, behind a feature flag
   │  Gate 5: live monitoring within limits; rollback tested
   ▼
General availability
   │  after launch: sampling real outputs, abuse reports, on-call, kill switch
```

**Gate 1, the evals,** is the automated part. You compare the new version with the one in production on two
kinds of eval: task evals (does it still do the job?) and harm evals (does it misbehave more in any policy area?).

**Gate 2, red-team and sign-off,** is the human part before anyone outside sees it. High-severity findings are
fixed or mitigated. The rest are written down as accepted risk.

**Gates 3 and 4** exist because evals only measure what someone thought to measure. People using the model notice
things nobody wrote a test for. A report that no eval explains is a reason to stop.

**Gate 5** is ordinary canary practice: a percentage rollout behind a flag, with limits that trigger rollback. The
**kill switch** is the flag that turns the feature off entirely.

With the picture in place, the procedure is five steps:

1. **Scope the release.** Who gets it (internal, a percentage of users, everyone), through what surface, with what
   controls.
2. **List the gates.** Quality versus the previous version; harm-specific evals; red-team findings; policy and
   legal review; capacity and cost; a rollback plan.
3. **Name the thresholds and owners.** Who can block, and on what evidence.
4. **Say what you'd monitor after release** and what triggers a rollback.
5. **Say what you accept.** Every release accepts some leftover risk. Name it, and name who signed off.

The **frontier labs** (the companies building the most capable models, such as Anthropic, OpenAI and Google) publish their own versions of this. Anthropic's **Responsible Scaling Policy** (RSP) ties
**capability thresholds** (levels of dangerous capability a model might reach) to the safeguards required before
a model at that level can be released. The current version is 3.4, effective 2026-07-08
([Anthropic RSP page, fetched 2026-10-12](https://www.anthropic.com/responsible-scaling-policy)). Read the current
version of the interviewing company's framework before the round, and be ready to say where you agree and where
you'd push.

<AINative title="When the evals say yes and the people say it feels off">
The clearest recent case study for release criteria is OpenAI's April 2025 GPT-4o update, rolled back about
four days after release for **sycophancy**: the model told users what they wanted to hear, agreeing and
flattering instead of being accurate. Per a Georgetown Law brief quoting OpenAI's postmortem, OpenAI "didn't
have specific deployment evaluations tracking sycophancy", expert testers said the behavior "felt slightly
off", and the company launched "due to the positive signals from the users who tried out the model"
([Georgetown Law Tech Institute brief](https://www.law.georgetown.edu/tech-institute/insights/tech-brief-ai-sycophancy-openai/), postmortem 2025-05-02).
Two lessons carry over to any release you own. A qualitative "feels off" from a trusted tester is a blocking
signal until an eval explains it. And user approval metrics can reward the wrong behavior. If you direct
AI agents to build eval suites, they will build the evals you describe; the judgment about which behaviors need
an eval before launch is still yours.
</AINative>

Say it like this: "If a trusted tester says the new version feels off and none of our evals can explain why, I
treat that as a blocker until we've built an eval that measures it. That's the lesson from the GPT-4o rollback."

## Example story outlines

These are illustrative outlines, not real stories. Every bracket is a fact only you can supply. If you don't have
a true story that fits, use a smaller true one; never construct one.

### Outline A: delivery pressure against a security concern (infra-107)

*Illustrative outline.* This is the story from the table above, in the compact form you would keep in your notes.

- **Context.** "We were [two weeks] from launching [a feature that let customers connect their own data
  sources]. I was [the engineer on the ingestion path]."
- **Tension.** "I found that [the connector stored customer credentials with broader scope than needed]. Fixing
  it properly meant [a week]. My manager was under pressure from [a signed customer date]."
- **What I did.** "I wrote up the risk in a page with [the worst realistic outcome] and two options: ship with
  [a narrower scope and the old flow] or slip a week. I took it to [my manager and the security lead]. We
  shipped on time with [the narrower scope]; the full version shipped [later]."
- **Then.** "I was worried about being the person who slowed the launch, and I nearly let it go."
- **Now.** "I'd raise it earlier, before the date was public. I now [add a security review to the launch
  checklist at design time]."

### Outline B: building something that conflicted with your values (eng-108)

*Illustrative outline.*

- **Context.** "[Product] used [user data / engagement mechanics / a model output] in a way I wasn't
  comfortable with: [describe plainly]."
- **Tension.** "I agreed with the business goal. I didn't agree with [the specific mechanism], because [who it
  could hurt]."
- **What I did.** "I raised it with [person] and proposed [an alternative]. They kept [part of it]. I built the
  rest."
- **Then.** "Uncomfortable. I told myself [the rationalization]."
- **Now.** "I think [the rationalization] was partly true and partly an excuse. Today I would [hold this line /
  ask for this check / leave the project]."

Notice that this outline does not end with you winning. The round reportedly rewards naming discomfort rather than
rationalizing it away (<Cite id="s-42f1c7f6dd" />).

### Outline C: release criteria for a new model (eng-112)

*Illustrative structure for a judgment answer.* Here it is applied to one case, so you can hear how the gates
sound with values in them. Assume you own a homework-help chat assistant in a consumer app, and your vendor offers
a new model version. Every number in this table is an illustrative assumption.

| Gate | What you check | Illustrative result | What to say in the interview |
|---|---|---|---|
| Quality | 200 task-eval items, new vs current version | 86% vs 84% | "That gap is inside the noise for 200 items, so I'd call it 'no worse', not 'better'." |
| Harm evals | Each policy area, new vs current | Self-harm and privacy unchanged; one area slightly worse | "A regression in any area blocks until we know why. I set the thresholds before the run, not after." |
| Over-refusal | Legitimate questions the model now refuses | Up from a few to many on chemistry homework | "That's a cost too. Students asking about reactions are the product." |
| Red team | Findings by severity | One high-severity prompt that gets dangerous instructions out of it | "Fixed or mitigated before any outside user sees it. Lower ones go on the accepted-risk list." |
| Testers | Qualitative reports | Two testers say it "agrees too easily" | "Unexplained, so it blocks. We build an eval for it first." |
| Rollout | Flag, 1% → 10% → 50% → all | Rollback tested in staging | "If we haven't exercised the rollback, we don't have one." |
| Owner | Who signs | You, plus a named reviewer | "One person signs the release and the accepted risks." |

The compact version for your notes:

- **Scope.** "I'd separate a model swap behind an existing product from a new public model. Here's the second."
- **Gates.** "Quality at least matches the previous model on our task evals. No regression on harm evals per
  policy area. Red-team findings triaged, with high-severity ones fixed or mitigated. Capability evaluations
  against the company's published thresholds. Legal and policy sign-off. Capacity and rollback tested."
- **Staging.** "Internal, then trusted testers, then a percentage rollout with monitoring, then general
  availability."
- **Blocking signals.** "Any threshold missed; any credible tester report we can't explain, which is the GPT-4o
  lesson; a rollback path not exercised."
- **Owner.** "One named person signs the release and the accepted risks."

### Outline D: GenAI safety in a consumer product (eng-109)

*Illustrative structure for a judgment answer.* Safety in a consumer product is built in layers, the way you'd
put a WAF (web application firewall), authentication and rate limits in front of an API. No single layer catches everything.

```
User message (+ any uploaded file or web page)
   │
   ▼
Product limits ── rate limits, age checks, which features this user can reach
   │
   ▼
Input check ── a classifier flags harmful requests and hidden instructions
   │
   ▼
Model + system prompt ── chosen model, your product's own rules
   │
   ▼
Output check ── a classifier flags harmful replies before the user sees them
   │
   ▼
Action limits ── no writes to accounts without the user confirming
   │
   ▼
User ── sees an AI label, a "report this" button, crisis resources where relevant
   │
   ▼
Operations ── sampling real outputs, on-call for safety incidents, kill switch
```

**Product limits** come first because they are ordinary code and cheap. A **classifier** is a smaller model that
labels text by category (for example "self-harm" or "harassment"); a provider's **moderation endpoint** is an API
that does this for you. The **system prompt** sets your product's rules, and the choice of model sets the rules
the vendor trained in. **Action limits** matter as soon as the assistant can do things, not just say things.
The **operations** layer is how you learn about what the other layers missed.

- **Threat model first.** "Who uses it, including people under 18 and people in crisis; what they can make it
  do; what content enters from outside."
- **Layers.** "Model choice and system prompt; input and output classifiers; product limits such as rate
  limits and no autonomous writes; human escalation; clear disclosure that it's AI."
- **Measurement.** "An eval set per harm, refreshed from real reports; over-refusal tracked too."
- **Operations.** "Abuse reporting in the product, on-call for safety incidents, a kill switch per feature."
- **The trade-off.** "Every layer costs latency or usefulness. I'd say which ones I'd accept and why."

## Small-team version

At a 50–500 person company there is no safety team, no policy team and often no security team. The AI owner
decides whether the feature is safe to launch, and the interview for that job tests whether you will delay a
launch when you should.

### What to build

- **A one-page release checklist** for every AI feature and every model or prompt change: eval results versus
  the previous version, harm checks relevant to this feature, data-handling review, rollback tested, owner named.
- **A kill switch per AI feature** that a non-engineer on call can flip.
- **Production sampling.** Read a sample of real outputs every week. Vendor model changes can shift behavior
  without your code changing.
- **An incident log** that records the times you delayed or rolled back, with the reason. This becomes the
  evidence for your next values story.

### What it costs

Less than one delayed launch. With assumed numbers:

- **Reading outputs:** 50 production outputs a week at an assumed 2 minutes each is about **1.7 hours, roughly
  $155 a week** of engineer time.
- **Running evals:** a 200-item harm and regression suite, run 3 times on a feature that makes one model call, is
  about **$13 per run** at Claude Sonnet 5 list prices fetched 2026-10-11 (the
  [eval-system walkthrough](/system-design-labs/interviews/walkthroughs/eval-system/) has the full arithmetic).
  Model prices are per **token**, a chunk of text of roughly ¾ of a word.
- **The kill switch** is a feature flag you already run.

The real cost is the occasional slipped date, which is why the incident log should record what each delay cost.

### What to buy

Provider-side moderation and safety features, and an existing guardrail or classifier service if your volume
justifies it. Check each vendor's documentation for what it covers; don't assume it covers your domain's harms.

### What you still own

What the product says. Three public cases show that whoever runs the product, not the model vendor, answers for it:

- In 2024 a tribunal held Air Canada responsible for its chatbot's wrong fare advice
  ([Law360 Canada, 2024-02](https://www.law360.ca/ca/articles/1804075)).
- In 2025 Cursor's support bot invented a login policy and users cancelled
  ([Fortune, 2025-04](https://fortune.com/article/customer-support-ai-cursor-went-rogue)).
- In July 2025 a published demonstration showed a support ticket containing instructions making a Cursor
  agent, connected to a Supabase database through MCP (a protocol for giving a model tools) with a privileged
  key, copy a private tokens table into the ticket. It was a researchers' demonstration, not a reported
  breach, but it used an ordinary product setup
  ([Simon Willison, 2025-07-06](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Jul/6/supabase-mcp-lethal-trifecta/)).

In each case the responsibility sits with whoever runs the product, not with the model vendor.

### Explaining a delay to the business

The hard part at a small company is rarely knowing that something is unsafe. It is saying so to a CEO who
promised the feature to a customer. Use the same pattern as any hard executive conversation: show two or three
real failing examples rather than a percentage, offer a narrower launch that passes the checklist, and name the
number that unlocks the rest. The [Drive decisions](/system-design-labs/interviews/walkthroughs/bh-drive-decisions/)
walkthrough covers that conversation in detail.

Say it like this: "Here are three real answers it gave in testing. I can ship it this week to logged-in adults
only, and the rest when the harm eval for minors passes."

In a values round, a story where you delayed your own feature, and what it cost you, is strong evidence for
exactly this job.

### Revisit when

Add a dedicated safety or trust-and-safety owner when the product is open to the public at consumer scale, serves
minors, or operates in a regulated domain such as health, finance or hiring. Until then, the checklist and your
willingness to use it are the safety program.

## Senior vs Staff

  title="Values and safety judgment"
  senior={[

    "Lists the right release gates for a feature they own.",
  ]}
  staff={[

In plain terms: a Senior answer shows you did the right thing once. A Staff answer shows you made the right thing
easier for everyone after you. At Staff level the personal story should show you making it easier for others to
raise concerns, not only raising your own.

## Follow-ups

<FollowUps>
<FollowUp q="How did you feel then, and how do you feel about it now? (Reported, Anthropic)">
Answer directly and briefly, then say what changed. The Safeguards candidate describes the interviewer
pressing on exactly this (<Cite id="s-42f1c7f6dd" />).
</FollowUp>
<FollowUp q="Have you ever done something morally wrong? (Reported, Anthropic)">
Reported by the Blind poster (<Cite id="s-e98cbef9cd" />). Have a small, true answer: a corner you cut, a
person you treated unfairly, a concern you didn't raise. Say what you did about it afterwards. "No" is not a
credible answer.
</FollowUp>
<FollowUp q="What kind of work do you dislike most? (Reported, Anthropic)">
Also from the Blind account (<Cite id="s-e98cbef9cd" />). Be honest and specific, and connect it to how you
handle that work when it is needed, rather than naming something trivial.
</FollowUp>
<FollowUp q="What would make you block a release that every metric says is ready? (Not reported; a natural next push)">
A credible qualitative signal no eval explains, an untested rollback, or a harm area with no eval at all. Cite
the GPT-4o sycophancy rollback as the case where this mattered.
</FollowUp>
<FollowUp q="How do you weigh over-refusal against harm? (Not reported; a natural next push)">
Measure both. Track false refusals on legitimate requests alongside harmful completions, and set thresholds
for each per harm area. Say which side you would err toward for this product and why.
</FollowUp>
<FollowUp q="What if your manager overrules you? (Not reported; a natural next push)">
Write down the risk and the decision, ask who owns the accepted risk, and escalate once if the harm is to users
rather than to schedules. Know where your own line is before the interview.
</FollowUp>
</FollowUps>

## Common mistakes

- **Mission-speak instead of a story.** The Aced list warns against "rehearsed mission-speak"
  (<Cite id="s-d7a8e88bf0" />). Fix: a specific situation with roles, dates and stakes.
- **Too much engineering detail.** This is the Blind poster's own diagnosis (<Cite id="s-e98cbef9cd" />). Fix: the
  system only as context; the tension and your thinking as the body.
- **A conflict where you were the hero.** If nobody else had a point, there was no tension. Fix: include the
  compromise, the doubt, or the part you would redo.
- **Rationalizing instead of reflecting.** Explaining why it was fine sounds defensive. Fix: name the discomfort
  plainly, as the Safeguards interviewer reportedly wanted (<Cite id="s-42f1c7f6dd" />).
- **"Safety first" with no gate.** An adjective can't block a release. Fix: name the evals, thresholds, owner and
  rollback for the judgment questions.
- **Ignoring the cost of caution.** An answer that never ships isn't judgment. Fix: say what the delay or the
  refusal costs, and why you accept it.

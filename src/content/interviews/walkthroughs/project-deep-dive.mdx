## The prompt

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "Here is the problem, who had it and how big it was. I used an LLM because the simpler option we tried first
> failed on these cases. I made these decisions myself; a teammate owned that part. The hardest choice was A over
> B, and this is the evidence that decided it. On 200 real questions it got 85% right, give or take 5 points, and
> it costs under a cent a request. Here is what broke after launch, what I changed, and what I would do
> differently."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer, so you can build your own version of it.

In this round you present a system you built, usually for 30 to 45 minutes and often with slides. One or two
interviewers interrupt with questions. The content is yours. The shape of the questions is not, and it is
predictable: the same questions come back across the reports. This page prepares you for each one.

### What makes an AI project different to present

You have probably presented a backend project before: a new service, a migration, a queue that replaced a cron
job. Those stories have a familiar shape. You describe the design, and you prove it worked with tests that pass,
a latency target that was met and an outage count that went down. "Did it work?" has a yes-or-no answer.

A project built on a large language model (an **LLM**, the kind of model behind ChatGPT or Claude) is different
in three ways. A good presentation is built around them.

1. **"Did it work?" has no yes-or-no answer.** The same model gives a good answer to one question and a
   confident wrong answer to the next. So quality is a **rate**: the share of answers that were right on a fixed
   set of test questions. If you can't give that number, the interviewer assumes you never measured it.
2. **Every request has its own bill.** A normal service costs about the same whether it handles one request or a
   thousand, because you pay for servers. With a hosted LLM you pay per request, by the amount of text in and
   out. So "what did it cost?" has a precise answer per request, and you are expected to know it.
3. **"Why use an LLM at all?" is a real question.** A keyword search, a few rules or a small classifier is
   cheaper, faster and more predictable. The interviewer wants to see that you compared, not that you were
   excited by the technology.

A strong talk therefore has three things a normal backend talk often lacks: a quality number with its sample
size, a cost per request, and a comparison against a simpler option. Everything else (ownership, decisions,
failures, timeline) is the same as for any project, and the interviewer probes it just as hard.

### Words you need first

- **Token.** LLMs read and write text in small pieces called tokens, roughly ¾ of an English word each. Prices,
  limits and lengths are all counted in tokens. The text you send is the **prompt**; the text that comes back is
  the **output**.
- **Eval set.** A fixed list of test inputs with known good answers, used to score the system. "Eval" is short
  for evaluation. Running the same eval set before and after a change is how you know whether the change helped.
- **RAG.** Short for retrieval-augmented generation. Before asking the model, the system searches your own
  documents for the passages most likely to answer the question (**retrieval**), then puts those passages into
  the prompt so the model answers from them. Many AI projects people present are RAG systems.
- **Agent.** A system where the model decides the next step itself, in a loop: it chooses a tool (search, an API
  call, a database query), sees the result, and decides again until the task is done. A single model call is not
  an agent.

## What is being tested

Four signals show up in the sources, and a fifth comes from the Staff report. Each one is a question the
interviewer is silently asking while you talk.

### 1. Did you own it?

The interviewer needs to separate what you did from what the team did. interviewing.io tells candidates to be
ready for "your level of contribution" and "what everyone's responsibilities were" (<Cite id="s-74b419b5c0" />).
Hello Interview says Staff reports highlight "personal ownership, alternatives considered, and impact"
(<Cite id="s-4d55d9b987" />).

Say it like this: "I chose the retrieval design and wrote the eval set. A teammate owned the ingestion jobs. I
reviewed their chunking code because it decided what the model could see."

### 2. Can you explain a decision, not just a diagram?

A diagram shows what you built. A decision shows how you think. Hello Interview warns that a polished
architecture summary "won't cover why you rejected another approach" (<Cite id="s-4d55d9b987" />). The Anthropic
Staff report's first question was "why A over B" (<Cite id="s-2348518c66" />).

Say it like this: "We had two options. B was simpler. We chose A because of this measurement."

### 3. Do you have a number for quality?

This is the AI-specific signal from the section above: quality is a rate, so you need the rate. A prep guide puts
it bluntly: "If you can't name what "good retrieval" means as a number, the interviewer assumes you tuned yours
by feel" (techinterview.org prep guide, <Cite id="s-7d03a572e0" />). The same guide calls evals "the widest skill
gap in the field".

### 4. Do you know when an LLM fits?

Aced's Anthropic FDE guide says to be ready to show you can tell when an LLM is the right tool and when it isn't
(<Cite id="s-cb39678bbd" />). That is the "why an LLM" question. The best answers include a part of the system
where you chose *not* to use one.

### 5. For Staff: can you extend it?

The Staff report went on to multi-region, sharding and operability (<Cite id="s-2348518c66" />). The interviewer
is checking that you can grow the system you actually built, with its real data and write path, not recite a
textbook design.

### Cost comes up too

A LinkedIn Senior candidate's project discussion on an LLM integration framework went into token handling and
per-query versus per-token pricing (<Cite id="s-1980a339c0" />). If your project calls a model, have the cost per
request ready. The Core concepts section shows how to work it out in one line.

### What the round is not

It is not a test of how big the system was. Hello Interview notes that a project you helped define and lead can
give you more to discuss than a bigger, well-known system where you owned a small piece
(<Cite id="s-4d55d9b987" />). That is good news if your best work happened on a small team.

## Clarify and scope

Most of the clarifying happens before the day, with the recruiter. Then you choose the project.

### Ask the recruiter four things

**How long, and how much of it is questions?** The sources disagree. interviewing.io describes a 45-minute
presentation to a senior manager (<Cite id="s-74b419b5c0" />). Hello Interview's L5 guide says candidates describe
"a roughly 30-minute presentation followed by detailed questions", and mentions one account with 15 minutes of
questions inside a one-hour round (<Cite id="s-4d55d9b987" />). Its advice is to follow the duration in your
invitation and ask the recruiter. Treat both lengths as reported, not as a rule. Prepare a 20-minute core plus two
optional deep sections you can add or drop.

**Slides or no slides?** interviewing.io says "Prepare slides – this is expected, not just advisable"
(<Cite id="s-74b419b5c0" />). Hello Interview only says a discussion of past work "may require slides"
(<Cite id="s-4d55d9b987" />). If unsure, bring 8 to 12 slides and be able to talk without them.

**Who is in the room?** A senior manager probes ownership, timeline and impact. An engineer probes the design. A
hiring manager for a forward-deployed role probes fit and adoption.

**A past project or the take-home?** Hello Interview flags that some deep dives review the take-home instead
(<Cite id="s-4d55d9b987" />).

### Choose the project

Use these criteria, in order:

1. **You made the key decisions** and can explain every one. A smaller project you led beats a famous one you
   touched.
2. **It has a number**: an eval result, a latency or cost change, an adoption figure. If it has none, measure
   something now. Running an eval afterwards on saved traffic is still a measurement. Otherwise pick a different
   project.
3. **Something went wrong** and you changed the design because of it.
4. **It matches the team.** An Uber Senior machine-learning engineer candidate presented a GenAI agent project to
   a pricing and marketplace team. The interviewer said it "might not be a great match", and HR raised a possible
   down-level
   ([PracHub](https://prachub.com/interview-experiences/uber-seniorplus-machine-learning-engineer-interview-experience-withdrew-after-a-possible-l5a-to-l4-downlevel), Apr 2026).

The help-desk assistant passes all four for a product-engineering team: you made the retrieval and eval
decisions, it has a quality number, it broke in an instructive way, and the team you are interviewing with builds
customer-facing features.

### Say what you will abstract

Confidential details are out of scope, but vagueness without explanation sounds like hiding. Say it up front:
"I'll give relative numbers for revenue; the eval numbers are exact."

## Core concepts

### The spine of the talk

Every follow-up in the reports maps onto one part of this outline. Give each part one slide and one number. Then
you can answer almost any interruption by jumping to the right part.

| Part | What it answers | Typical follow-up |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Problem and stakes | Who had the problem, how much it hurt, how you knew | "How big was the problem before you started?" |
| 2. Why an LLM | The simpler option you tried or ruled out | "How did you know it fit?" (eng-102) |
| 3. Your role | What you decided, built and delegated | "What did *you* do?" (eng-105) |
| 4. Architecture and the rejected option | One diagram, one fork in the road | "Why A over B?" (infra-104) |
| 5. Evaluation | Metric, eval set, baseline, result with its error bar | "How did you measure it?" |
| 6. What broke | A production failure and the design change | "Stale docs? Uncovered questions?" (eng-099) |
| 7. Timeline and cost | Calendar time, where it went, cost per request | "Why was the timeline tight?" (eng-101) |
| 8. What you'd change, and how it scales | Your own critique, then the next order of magnitude | "Multi-region? Shard it?" (infra-104) |

The rest of this section explains the ideas behind parts 2, 3, 5 and 7, the parts candidates most often get
wrong. The Worked examples section then fills all eight for the help-desk assistant.

### Ownership language

Interviewers listen for who did what. "We" is right for team outcomes and wrong for your own decisions.

- Weak: "We built a RAG pipeline and improved accuracy."
- Strong: "I chose hybrid retrieval over dense-only after the eval showed keyword queries failing; a teammate
  owned ingestion; I wrote the eval set with two support leads and ran the comparison."

(Dense and hybrid retrieval are explained in the worked example below. For now, notice the shape: a decision, the
evidence, and who did the rest.)

Name what you delegated, including to **coding agents** (AI tools that write code from instructions), and how you
checked their work. "An agent wrote the ingestion scripts; I wrote the tests that check where it splits each document" is
stronger than hiding the agent.

### One number, with its error bar

Here is the idea before any arithmetic. Your eval set is a sample. If you scored the assistant on 50 questions
and got 85%, a different 50 questions might have given 80% or 90%. The **error bar** (or **confidence interval**)
says how far the true rate could plausibly be from the one you measured. The fewer questions, the wider the bar.

The width matters because interviewers compare numbers. If you say "the new version scored 3 points higher", the
next question is "how many questions?" If the error bar is wider than 3 points, the improvement might be noise.

For a pass rate near 85%, the 95% error bar is roughly:

| Questions in the eval set | Error bar | So "85%" really means |
|---|---|---|
| 50 | **± 10 points** | about 75% to 95% |
| 200 | **± 5 points** | about 80% to 90% |
| 1,000 | **± 2 points** | about 83% to 87% |

So "85% on 50 questions" means roughly 75 to 95%. If your before-and-after gap is smaller than the error bar, say
so, and say what you did about it. Two good responses: grow the set, or run a **paired comparison**. A paired
comparison runs both versions on the same questions and looks only at the questions where they disagree, which
removes much of the noise. The [eval-system walkthrough](/system-design-labs/interviews/walkthroughs/eval-system/)
covers paired tests and release gates in depth.

Say it like this: "85% on 200 real questions, so about plus or minus 5 points. The old version scored 78% on the
same 200, and the paired comparison says the gap is real."

### Why an LLM: the fit test

"How did you know it fit?" (eng-102) is answered by comparison, not enthusiasm. Four checks:

1. **A baseline.** A **baseline** is the simpler thing you compare against: rules, keyword search, a small
   classifier, or the existing human process. Say what it achieved and where it failed. If you never tried one,
   say what it would have scored and why you skipped it.
2. **Error tolerance.** What does a wrong answer cost, and who catches it? A draft that a human edits tolerates
   errors. An automatic refund doesn't.
3. **Input shape.** Open-ended language, many formats or long-tail questions favour an LLM. Fixed fields and
   stable categories favour a classifier or rules.
4. **Unit economics.** The cost per request against the value of one handled case (next section).

The honest version sometimes includes "part of it should not have been an LLM", for example a routing step you
later replaced with a classifier. That answer reads as judgment.

For the help-desk assistant: keyword search was the baseline. It found the right article when the customer used
the article's words, and failed when they paraphrased ("can't get in" versus "reset your password"). Wrong answers
are tolerable because every answer links to its source article and the customer can still open a ticket. Inputs
are free text. That is a clean fit.

Say it like this: "Keyword search was our baseline. It failed on paraphrased questions, which were most of the
tickets. A wrong answer was cheap, because every answer linked to its source."

### Cost per request

With a hosted model you pay per token, at different prices for three kinds of token:

- **Input tokens**: the prompt you send, including the passages retrieved from your documents.
- **Cached input tokens**: input the provider has seen recently and stored, such as a long fixed instruction
  block at the start of every prompt. These are billed at a fraction of the normal input price.
- **Output tokens**: the model's reply. These cost the most per token.

Assume the help-desk assistant sends 1,000 uncached input tokens (the question and retrieved passages), 2,000
cached input tokens (the fixed instructions) and gets 400 output tokens back. At Anthropic's list prices for
Claude Sonnet 5 ($2 per million input tokens, $0.20 per million cached, $10 per million output, fetched
2026-10-11, [pricing page](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing)), one request costs about
**$0.0064**, about two-thirds of a cent. The token counts are assumptions; the prices are the provider's.

Multiply by daily requests and 30 for the monthly bill. At the assumed 50,000 questions a day that is about
**$9,600 a month**. Having that one line ready answers most cost questions.

Say it like this: "About two-thirds of a cent per question, so roughly $9,600 a month at 50,000 questions a day.
The output tokens are most of it."

### The timeline

"How long to production-ready?" (eng-102) and "why was the timeline tight?" (eng-101) are planning questions in
disguise. Break the calendar into phases with durations:

1. **Prototype**: a working version on your own machine.
2. **Eval set**: collecting and labelling the test questions.
3. **Hardening**: timeouts, fallbacks when the model provider is down, **guardrails** (checks on what goes into
   and comes out of the model), rate limits.
4. **Review**: security, legal and privacy sign-off.
5. **Rollout**: a small group of users first, then everyone.

In LLM projects the eval and review phases are often the long ones, not the code. Honeycomb's account of shipping
its natural-language query feature in under a month includes a security audit of providers, new terms and
in-product disclosure on the critical path
([Honeycomb](https://www.honeycomb.io/blog/hard-stuff-nobody-talks-about-llm), page dated 2024-08-24).

For "why tight", the interviewer wants cause and response: where the deadline came from, what you cut, what you
refused to cut, and what that cost later.

## Worked examples

The three outlines below are **illustrative structures**, not real projects or anyone's story. Fill them with
your own facts. Where an outline uses a site lab, the numbers are the lab's measured results on a public
benchmark. That is a side project, not production evidence; say that plainly if you present one.

### Outline A: the help-desk assistant, start to finish (fits eng-099, eng-100, eng-105)

This is the running example, filled in part by part. First, the system.

#### The picture of the system

```
Help-center articles (about 3,000, edited daily by the support team)
   │  1. ingestion job, every hour
   ▼
Chunker ── splits each article into passages of a few hundred tokens
   │  2. turn each passage into a vector
   ▼
Embedding model ── text in, list of numbers out
   │  3. store
   ▼
Index ── vectors (for meaning) + words (for keyword search) + article id and version
   ▲
   │  4. search with the customer's question
Customer question ──► Retriever ── hybrid search, top 20 passages
                          │  5. re-order the 20, keep the best 3
                          ▼
                       Reranker
                          │  6. build the prompt: instructions + 3 passages + question
                          ▼
                       LLM (hosted API) ── answer with links, or "I don't know"
                          │  7. log question, passages, article versions, answer
                          ▼
                       Customer sees the answer
```

**Ingestion and the chunker.** Articles change every day, so a job re-reads them every hour. A long article
doesn't fit well into one prompt, so the **chunker** cuts each one into passages (**chunks**) of a few hundred
tokens. Chunk size is a real decision: too small and a passage loses its context, too large and the useful
sentence is buried.

**The embedding model.** An **embedding** is a list of a few hundred numbers that represents the meaning of a
piece of text. Texts with similar meaning get similar lists, even when they share no words. "Can't get in" and
"reset your password" end up close together. An **embedding model** is the small model that produces these
lists.

**The index.** It stores each chunk three ways: its embedding, its words, and the id and version of the article
it came from. The version is what later lets you detect stale answers.

**The retriever.** Searching by embedding is called **dense retrieval**; it finds passages with similar meaning.
Searching by words is keyword search; the standard scoring method is called **BM25**, and it is good at exact
terms such as error codes and product names. Using both and merging the results is **hybrid retrieval**. The
retriever returns the 20 most promising chunks.

**The reranker.** A **reranker** is a second, slower model that reads the question and each candidate chunk
together and scores how well they match. It is too slow to run over the whole index, but fast enough for 20
candidates. It keeps the best 3.

**The LLM call.** The prompt holds fixed instructions ("answer only from these passages; link each one; if they
don't answer the question, say so"), the 3 passages and the customer's question. The model writes the answer.

**The log.** Every answer is logged with the passages and article versions it used. This one line of design is
what makes the follow-up questions answerable.

#### The evaluation slide, with real measured numbers

If you built the site's <LabRef lab="rag" inline /> lab and extended it, the evaluation slide has measured
material. The lab ran three versions on two public test sets from the BEIR retrieval benchmark: SciFact
(scientific claims) and NFCorpus (nutrition and medical questions). The metric is **hit@3**: the share of
questions where a relevant document appears among the top 3 results.

<LabNumber lab="rag" metric="hit@3 on BEIR NFCorpus" />.

The point worth presenting is not that reranking "won". With about 300 questions, the error bar is wide, so the
lab compared each pair of versions on the same questions. Of the three shipped versions, only reranking cleared
its paired interval on SciFact. Hybrid added nothing with the lab's simple tokenizer (it split words on spaces, so
"error:" and "error" counted as different words). A tokenizer fix (an ablation in the lab's bench script, not its
shipped code) moved hybrid to <LabNumber lab="rag" metric="BM25 tokenizer" />, and that gain also cleared its
paired interval. On NFCorpus no single step cleared its interval.

That is a real "what I learned" slide: the expensive component helped less than a one-line tokenizer change. A
public benchmark also has no stale documents, so say what you would measure in production instead.

Say it like this: "Of the steps we shipped, only the reranker cleared its paired interval on SciFact. But a
one-line tokenizer fix to keyword search cleared its interval too, and did slightly more than the reranker."

### Outline B: an agentic workflow (fits eng-104)

"Have you built an end-to-end agentic system?" sounds like a yes-or-no question, but it has a trap. A chat window
over a single model call is not an agent. And an agent used where a fixed sequence of steps would do (a
**workflow**) is a design smell. A strong answer:

1. Says what "end to end" covered: what starts a run, the tool calls, where the run's state is kept, human
   approval, logging, evaluation and rollout.
2. Says why the task needed the model to choose its own steps, or admits that a fixed workflow handled most of it.
   The [agentic-workflow-design walkthrough](/system-design-labs/interviews/walkthroughs/agentic-workflow-design/)
   has the decision rule.
3. Gives the **stop conditions** (a step cap, a dollar cap, a timeout) and the permission model for each tool.
4. Gives a task-level metric: the share of runs completed without human correction, measured on a fixed set of
   tasks.
5. Names a failure: a loop, a wrong tool call, an action that needed a human gate.

For the help-desk assistant, a natural agentic extension is "check the customer's account before answering": the
model may look up the customer's plan and recent errors (read-only tools), and must ask a human before changing
anything. Saying that the read-only part is safe to automate and the write part is not shows the judgment the
question is after.

If your only agent work is the site's <LabRef lab="agent-framework" inline /> lab, say what is missing. Its LLM is
a mock, and 3 of its 15 tests fail: two because the parser can't read the nested tool-call JSON its own prompt
asks for, one because of the sandbox test setup. Fixing the parser, wiring a real model and measuring task success
is a legitimate project. Presenting the lab as it is, is not.

### Outline C: the Staff scale-out (fits infra-104)

After the design, the Staff report asks about multi-region, sharding and operability. Structure each answer the
same way: what the system does today, what breaks first at the next scale, what you'd change, what it costs.

```
            Global (one copy)                          Per region (one copy each)
  ┌──────────────────────────────────┐        ┌───────────────────────────────────┐
  │ Help-center articles (source)    │──────► │ Index replica (chunks + vectors)  │
  │ Eval set and release gate        │        │ Retriever + reranker              │
  │ Prompt and model version config  │──────► │ Gateway to the model provider's   │
  └──────────────────────────────────┘        │   regional endpoint               │
                                              │ Answer logs (stay in the region)  │
                                              └───────────────────────────────────┘
```

- **Multi-region.** Decide which state must be global and which can be regional. For the help-desk assistant the
  articles are written in one place, so writes stay global and each region gets a read-only index copy. For an
  LLM-backed system add: which regions the model provider serves, its data-residency terms, and whether each
  region has its own model quota (the provider's cap on tokens per minute).
- **Sharding.** Pick the shard key from the access pattern: tenant, document collection or user. Then say what
  goes wrong: hot tenants, queries that cross shards, rebalancing. For a vector index, sharding by tenant is
  natural when queries never cross tenants.
- **Operability.** The **SLOs** (service-level objectives: the reliability and latency targets you commit to) you'd set, the three dashboards you'd want, the runbook for the top failure, who is
  on call, and how a deploy is rolled back. For model-backed parts add a quality signal, not only an error rate.
  Anthropic's August–September 2025 postmortem describes quality bugs that standard evals, spot checks and
  canaries missed ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/a-postmortem-of-three-recent-issues),
  2025-09-17).

Say it like this: "Articles are written in one place, so writes stay global. Each region gets a read-only copy
of the index and calls the provider's regional endpoint. The first thing that breaks at 10× is the model quota,
not our servers."

## Common follow-ups in depth

### "How would you detect answers drawn from stale documents?" (eng-099)

A **stale** answer is one built from an old version of a document. In the help-desk example: the pricing article
changed on Monday, the index still holds last week's chunk, and the assistant quotes the old price. Make staleness
something you can see at three levels:

- **Index lag.** Store each chunk's source version and ingestion time. Alert on the gap between a document's last
  change and its last indexing, and on documents deleted at the source but still in the index.
- **Answer provenance.** Put the document id and version in every citation, and log them. Then "answered from an
  outdated version" is a query over logs, not a guess.
- **Canary questions.** When a key document changes, add a question whose answer changed, and run it against the
  live system on a schedule.

The [enterprise-rag-acl walkthrough](/system-design-labs/interviews/walkthroughs/enterprise-rag-acl/) treats
deletion lag the same way for permissions.

Say it like this: "Every chunk carries its article version, and every answer logs the versions it used. So a
stale answer is a log query, and index lag is a metric with an alert."

### "What happens when the docs don't cover the question?" (eng-099)

Say the target behaviour first: **abstain** (decline to answer), say what the documents do cover, and route to a
human or a search link. Then say how you enforce it: an instruction to answer only from the retrieved passages,
and a retrieval-score threshold below which the system abstains.

Most important, put **unanswerable questions in the eval set**, so you can measure two rates: how often the
system abstains when it should (the **abstain rate**) and how often it abstains when the answer was there (the
**false-abstain rate**). Without those items you can't tell whether the system abstains at all.

Say it like this: "Twenty of our eval questions have no answer in the help center. We track how often it says 'I
don't know' on those, and how often it says it on questions it should have answered."

### "Why did you use an LLM, and how long until production?" (eng-102)

Use the fit test from Core concepts, then the timeline phases. If adoption was slow, say why (trust, a change to
people's workflow, a missing integration) and what you did about it.

For the help-desk assistant, an illustrative timeline: prototype 1 week, eval set 2 weeks, hardening 2 weeks,
security and legal review 3 weeks, staged rollout 2 weeks. Ten weeks in total, and the longest phase was not the
code.

### "Why was the timeline tight?" (eng-101)

Name the source of the deadline (a customer commitment, a contract, a launch), what you traded (scope, not
evals), and the consequence you later paid. If the timeline was tight because of your own estimate, say that and
what you now do differently. Interviewers are probing planning and judgment, not looking for blame.

Say it like this: "The date came from a contract. I cut the second data source, kept the eval gate, and we paid
for it with two weeks of manual triage after launch."

### "Why A over B?" (infra-104)

Present the rejected option fairly, the criterion that decided it, and the evidence. A good shape: "B was simpler
and would have been fine below N; we expected to cross N within a year, and a load test showed B's p99 at X
there." If the evidence was thin, say what you would test now.

For the help-desk assistant the fork was dense-only retrieval (B) against hybrid (A). B was one component instead
of two. The deciding evidence was the eval: questions containing error codes and product names failed under B,
because meaning-based search tends to miss exact strings such as an error code like "E1042".

### "Was it worth it?"

This is the return-on-investment question. Use the template in the
[business-case walkthrough](/system-design-labs/interviews/walkthroughs/bh-business-case/): cost in engineer
time and compute, value against what would have happened without the project, payback period, and what would
have made it not worth doing.

## Small-team version

At a 50–500 person company you probably owned the whole thing: scoping with the business owner, the build, the
eval, the rollout, the bill and the pager. That is strong material if you frame it at Staff level instead of
apologizing for its size.

### Frame the scope

Staff scope at a small company is breadth plus consequences. Say how many teams or customers the system touched,
what decision it changed (support staffing, a sales process, a product feature), and what you decided not to
build. "I decided not to self-host and not to fine-tune, and here is the eval that showed prompting plus retrieval
was enough" is a Staff-level sentence. (**Self-hosting** means running the model on your own GPUs instead of
calling an API. **Fine-tuning** means further training a model on your own examples.)

### Bring four numbers

1. **The quality number** with its sample size, from an eval you built. Small teams usually have 50 to 300 items
   labelled with a domain expert.
2. **The cost per request and the monthly bill.** For the help-desk assistant: about $0.0064 a question and about
   **$9,600 a month** on Claude Sonnet 5 at the assumed 50,000 questions a day. Know the cheaper model you tested
   too: the same tokens on gpt-5-mini cost about $0.0011 a question, about **$1,650 a month**.
3. **An adoption or outcome number**: weekly users, tickets handled without a human, hours saved, and how you
   measured it.
4. **The calendar**: weeks to first user, weeks to production-ready, where the time went.

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the numbers (for a Staff-level follow-up)</summary>

- Claude Sonnet 5 (list prices fetched 2026-10-11): $0.0064 × 50,000 × 30 ≈ **$9,600 a month**.
- gpt-5-mini ($0.25 per million input, $0.025 cached, $2 output): 1,000 × 0.25 ÷ 10⁶ + 2,000 × 0.025 ÷ 10⁶ +
  400 × 2 ÷ 10⁶ = 0.00025 + 0.00005 + 0.0008 = $0.0011 a request; × 50,000 × 30 ≈ **$1,650 a month**.

</details>

Say it like this: "We tested the smaller model at about a sixth of the cost. It lost too many points on the eval,
so we kept the larger one and cached the instructions instead." (The outcome is illustrative; say what your own
eval showed.)

### Build, buy and skip, as part of the story

A small-team story is stronger when it names the choices that kept the system small. These are common small-team
defaults, offered as judgment, not as a reported interview rubric:

- **Bought**: model access through a managed API, often with a second provider as a fallback; tracing (a record
  of every model call and its inputs) from an open-source or hosted tool.
- **Built**: the eval set and the regression gate, the retrieval and prompt assembly, cost and rate limits.
- **Skipped**: fine-tuning, a dedicated vector database (a separate database just for embeddings) while Postgres was enough, an agent framework where a few
  hundred lines of plain code did the job.

A published break-even analysis puts
self-hosting a 2× H100 node (a server with two NVIDIA H100 GPUs) against frontier API output prices at roughly
245M to 612M output tokens a month before labor
([Cohorte](https://cohorte.co/blog/self-hosted-llm-cost-vs-api-break-even), 2026-09-20). The help-desk assistant
produces about 600M output tokens a month (400 × 50,000 × 30), the top of that range, and that is before paying
anyone to run the GPUs. If your project stayed on an API, that is a defensible reason.

### What you'd do differently

Pick one technical and one process change. Common honest ones: building the eval set before the prototype rather
than after the first complaint; adding article versions to citations on day one; setting a spend cap before
launch. Avoid "I'd use a better model", which says nothing about your judgment.

### Revisit when

Your story should say what would change the design: 10× traffic (cost and rate limits), a compliance requirement
(where data is stored, how long it is kept), a second team building on it (a shared gateway and prompt
versioning), or an eval score that stops improving (then fine-tuning becomes worth testing).

<AINative title="Telling the agent part of the story">
If coding agents wrote much of the code, say so and describe how you verified it: the tests you wrote first,
the eval that gated each change, the review you did on the parts that touch data or money. Hiding agent use
invites a follow-up you can't answer well, and claiming you wrote everything by hand is easy to probe. The
ownership the interviewer wants is over decisions and verification, not keystrokes. Be ready for "what did the
agent get wrong?" with a specific example, such as a retry loop with no cap or a test that asserted the bug.
</AINative>

Staff answer explains the decisions, the evidence and the limits, and extends the design before being asked.

## Common mistakes

- **Architecture tour, no decisions.** Hello Interview warns a polished summary "won't cover why you rejected
  another approach" (<Cite id="s-4d55d9b987" />); put one rejected option on a slide.
- **Underselling by omission.** The Meta candidate felt they did not make the project "sound impressive enough"
  (<Cite id="s-a7b843c47f" />); the fix is stakes, a number and a hard decision in the first three minutes, not
  adjectives.
- **No quality number.** Without one, a prep guide says the interviewer "assumes you tuned yours by feel"
  (<Cite id="s-7d03a572e0" />).
- **"We" everywhere, or "I" for everything.** Both fail the ownership probe, because the interviewer can't tell
  what you did (<Cite id="s-74b419b5c0" />).
- **Wrong project for the team.** A project far from the team's work can cost you the level, as in the Uber report
  under Clarify and scope.
- **Presenting a lab or tutorial as production.** Interviewers find out quickly; say what it is, what you
  measured, and on what data.
- **No answer for scale-out.** The Staff report asked about it even for a working system, so prepare it even if
  the project never needed it (<Cite id="s-2348518c66" />).

## The prompt

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "I use AI every day, but I treat what it gives me as a draft, never as the answer. For each kind of task I have
> a set way of handing it over and a set way of checking the result. I can tell you about a time it was
> confidently wrong, what caught it, and what I changed afterwards. There are also tasks where I don't use it at
> all, and I can say why. And I want this role for the work itself, not only for the company's name."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer, so you can say it with real examples of your own.

### Why this is not an ordinary behavioral question

A normal behavioral question asks about people and past events: a conflict, a missed deadline, a hard decision.
There is no right technical answer. The interviewer listens for how you think and how you treat others.

Questions about AI look similar, but the interviewer is checking something more specific. Three things make
them different:

1. **Everyone says yes.** Almost every engineer now says they use AI tools. So "I use it a lot" tells the
   interviewer nothing. What separates candidates is *how*: what you hand over, how you check it, and where you
   stop.
2. **The tool is wrong in a convincing way.** A junior colleague who is unsure usually says so. An AI tool
   produces wrong code with the same confidence as right code. The real skill is catching that, and the
   interviewer wants to hear that you have the habit.
3. **The company is betting on it.** Companies hiring AI engineers have opinions about AI, and some have
   published them. When they ask "why this role?" or "what would you critique about us?", they want to see that
   you have thought about the work and the company, not that you admire them.

So these questions reward the same thing a code review rewards: specific evidence, checked claims and honest
limits.

### Words you need first

You do not need any machine-learning background for this page. You need four terms.

- A **large language model (LLM)** is a program trained on a huge amount of text and code. Given some text, it
  predicts what text should come next. Chat tools and coding tools are built on top of one.
- An **AI coding assistant** is a tool that uses an LLM to suggest or write code: inside your editor, in a chat
  window, or in your terminal.
- A **coding agent** is an assistant that does more than suggest. It takes actions in a loop: it reads files,
  edits several of them, runs commands and tests, looks at the results, and keeps going until it thinks the task
  is done. Most "how do you use AI" answers today are about agents.
- A **hallucination** is a confident answer that is false. In code, the classic case is an **invented API**: a
  function or config option that looks real, fits the naming style, and does not exist in your library version.

Other terms (tokens, rule files, evals, forward-deployed engineers) are explained where they first come up.

### The example we will follow

## What is being tested

### Practice questions: checking, not enthusiasm

PracHub's write-up of the Amazon question sums up what strong answers contain: "Concrete, first-person examples
rather than general opinions about AI" and "A consistent verification habit, with AI output treated as a draft
to be checked." Its hint for the bad-output part is "Name what caught it." (<Cite id="s-56187f9727" />). That is
the write-up's view, not a reported rubric, but it matches what employers say elsewhere.

Canva, which expects candidates to use AI in interviews, wrote that "The most successful candidates didn't just
prompt AI and accept whatever it generated" (<Dated asOf="2025-06-11" src="s-88d61cb9fa">Canva Engineering</Dated>).
A startup tech lead told the Pragmatic Engineer "I'd rather hire someone who is 'behind' on AI, but has great
taste/judgment" (<Dated asOf="2026-07-07" src="s-b3dd400e1f">Pragmatic Engineer</Dated>).

For Sam, this means the migration story matters more than the list of tools. Say it like this: "The tool is
fast, but I'm the one who decides whether the change is right."

### Knowing how good your own measurements are

There is a second, quieter signal: do you know whether your own sense of speed is reliable? Usually it isn't.

The research group METR ran a study in 2025 with 16 experienced open-source developers working on real tasks in
their own projects. With AI tools they were 19% slower, while they believed they were about 20% faster
([METR, 2025-07-10](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/)). METR's 2026
follow-up with 57 developers found an 18% time reduction, but METR called it "only very weak evidence"
([METR, 2026-02-24](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/)).

The point is not that AI makes you slower. The point is that feeling faster and being faster are different
things. Quoting a self-reported speed-up as fact costs you a little credibility with an interviewer who knows
these studies. Sam has never measured, so Sam says so: "I haven't measured my speed-up properly, so I won't give
you a percentage. What I can tell you is what shipped and how I checked it."

### Knowing what over-reliance costs

A third signal is whether you know what leaning on the tool costs *you*. In an Anthropic trial with 52 mostly
junior engineers, the group using AI scored 50% on a follow-up quiz, against 67% for the group without AI. The
biggest gap was on debugging, and the AI group was not significantly faster
([Anthropic research, 2026-01-29](https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills)).

A candidate who can say how they keep their own skills sharp while delegating is answering a question the
interviewer may not have asked yet. Typical habits: writing the hard part by hand, reading every diff, and
debugging without the assistant first. Sam's version: "When something breaks, I try to find it myself for the
first twenty minutes before I ask the agent. Otherwise I stop learning the system."

### The assistant comparison: opinions from real use

PromptLayer's post, written from the interviewer's side, gives the sample "What AI coding assistants do you use?
Can you compare Cursor, Windsurf, Zed, and Claude Code?" It wants candidates with opinions, including on open
versus proprietary models and "specific use cases where they'd choose one model over another." (An **open
model** is one whose files you can download and run on your own machines; a **proprietary model** is only
available through the vendor's service.) The post names "Boring textbook answers" as a red flag and says "A
tinkerer will explain not just what worked, but what didn't." (<Cite id="s-1205b0cc50" />).

The post notes its sample questions "can age quickly", so the tool names are dated. The shape of the question is
not: they want comparisons that come from your own tasks, including where each tool failed.

### Why this role: a filter, not a formality

A **forward-deployed engineer (FDE)** is an engineer who works directly with a customer, often inside the
customer's own systems, to make the company's product work for them. It is a mix of coding, integration and
conversation, and it is a different job from building the core product.

In a first-hand OpenAI FDE account, the recruiter "spent a lot of time on why I wanted FDE specifically, not just
'work at OpenAI.'" The candidate treats it as a filter: "If you can't articulate why you want customer-facing
technical work versus pure engineering, the process stops here." (<Cite id="s-812dbc9385" />). Hello
Interview's OpenAI guide warns against "a rehearsed speech about AGI" (<Cite id="s-4d55d9b987" />), where
**AGI** (artificial general intelligence) means AI that matches people across most tasks.

So "why this role" is really "do you know what the work is, and do you want *that*?" An answer about the
company's fame fails it.

### The mission critique: candor with understanding

Some companies run a **values round** (also called a culture round): an interview about how you reason through
ethical tension and whether you share the company's goals. Both detailed accounts below describe the critique
question in Anthropic's values or culture round. One came from a candidate for an inference role (the team that
runs the models for users), the other from a candidate for the Safeguards team.

The inference candidate was asked "Do you have any critique or concerns about Anthropic's approach or
direction?" They answered that the safety-first positioning is "part of the brand", that "they probably regret
not being first on some launches", and "I buy the mission, but I also think they're still a competitive company
trying to win." That candidate was rejected; they don't say this answer was the reason (<Cite id="s-4e04a1689c" />).

The Safeguards candidate said "I am not totally confident anyone can reach the level of safety they say they
want", and that some of it "can look like overengineering when other players are willing to cut corners." Their
reading: "I got the sense they wanted to see whether I would push back instead of just flattering the mission."
Their outcome was still pending (<Cite id="s-42f1c7f6dd" />).

Neither account tells you what answer passes. What they show is that the question is sincere. Flattery is the
obvious wrong answer. A critique that shows you haven't read what the company publishes is the other.

The round also carries weight. The Safeguards candidate's recruiter said it "was the one where people usually
fail" (<Cite id="s-42f1c7f6dd" />). A Senior Staff candidate's Blind post (Blind is an anonymous forum for tech
employees) is titled "I think the culture round sank my Anthropic Sr Staff loop" (<Cite id="s-e98cbef9cd" />).
That is the candidate's own belief, not feedback the company gave.

## What a strong story contains

### The system you are describing

When you explain how you use an agent, you are describing a small system: a loop with inputs, a worker, checks
and a feedback path. Drawing it in your head keeps the answer in order. Here is Sam's:

```
Task (ticket + failing test)
   │  1. you hand over a clear task
   ▼
Agent proposes a plan ── you approve, edit or reject it
   │  2. the agent works
   ▼
Agent edits code and runs the tests
   │  3. you check the result
   ▼
Your review ── read the diff, race detector, staging run
   │  4. ship
   ▼
Merge
   │  5. a mistake got through, or nearly did?
   ▼
Rule file updated ── the agent reads it on every future task
```

**The task.** Sam starts with something the agent can be checked against: a failing test that shows the bug. A
vague request ("fix the timeout issue") produces vague work. Say: "I never hand over a task I can't check."

**The plan.** Before the agent edits anything, it writes a short plan: which files, what change. Sam reads it and
approves or corrects it. This is cheap, and it catches wrong directions before any code exists.

**The edit and test run.** The agent changes the code and runs the tests itself. A passing test suite is
necessary, but it is not enough, because the agent wrote code to make *those* tests pass.

**Your review.** Sam reads the whole diff, runs the Go race detector (which finds data races in concurrent code),
and for risky changes runs the change in staging against a copy of production data. This is the step most
candidates skip in their answer, and it is the step the interviewer is listening for.

**The rule file.** A **rule file** is a plain text file in the repository (often called `AGENTS.md`) that the
agent reads at the start of every task. It holds project instructions: conventions, commands, and "never do X"
lines. When a mistake gets through, Sam adds a line so the agent avoids it next time. Mitchell Hashimoto
describes turning each agent mistake into an AGENTS.md rule or a tool
([mitchellh.com, 2026-02-05](https://mitchellh.com/writing/my-ai-adoption-journey)).

### For "how do you use AI daily" (eng-106, r2b-006)

A strong answer has four parts. Here they are, filled in from Sam's table:
(One check in the table is an **eval run**. An **eval** is a fixed set of test inputs with known good answers that
you run an AI feature against, much like a regression suite.)

| Part | What it means | Sam's version (illustrative) | What to say in the interview |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Two or three named workflows** | What you hand the tool, in what form, and what comes back | Bug fixes from a failing test; explaining unfamiliar code | "I give the agent the failing test and the package, ask for a plan, approve it, then let it write the change." |
| **2. The check for each** | Tests written first, diff review, an eval run, a second reviewer, a manual reproduction | Diff review and race detector; reading the code to check two of its claims | "For explanations, I check two claims myself before I trust the rest." |
| **3. Where you don't use it, and why** | Data rules, security-sensitive code, decisions you want to own | No customer data in external tools; migrations by hand | "I write schema migrations myself. That rule came from a mistake I'll tell you about." |
| **4. An outcome, honestly measured** | What shipped faster or better, and how you know | Has not measured a speed-up | "I haven't measured it rigorously, so I won't give a percentage." |

"I use it for everything" is not a workflow. A workflow names the input, the action and the check. PracHub's
write-up also suggests saying where you deliberately avoid AI (<Cite id="s-56187f9727" />).

### For "a time AI gave you wrong or harmful output" (eng-106)

This is the story the interviewer remembers. It has four parts:

1. **The output, and why it looked right.** A wrong migration, an invented API, a subtle security hole, a
   confident summary of a document that said the opposite. Explain why a reasonable engineer could have missed
   it. That shows the error was a real risk, not carelessness.
2. **What caught it.** A test, a reviewer, a production metric, a customer. If it reached production, say so.
3. **The cost.** Time lost, a near miss, or a real incident.
4. **What you changed** so that kind of error is caught earlier next time: a line in the rule file, a new kind of
   test, a review step.

Sam's main failure, step by step:

- The agent wrote a migration that added a column with a default value. It passed every test, because the test
  database was tiny.
- A staging run against a copy of production data showed the migration locked the large orders table. In
  production, that would have blocked writes for the duration.
- It cost half a day and never reached production.
- Sam added a rule to the agent's instructions, a lock-duration check in CI for every migration, and the habit of
  writing migrations by hand.

Notice what the story is about. It is not "the AI was bad". It is "my checks had a gap, here is what filled it,
and here is the check I added". Say it like this: "The test suite passed because the test data was small. The
gap was mine, so I added a check that would catch it for anyone."

Security is a good place to look for your own story. Veracode's 2025 test of 100+ models on 80 tasks found a
vulnerability in 45% of generated code ([Veracode, 2025-07-30](https://www.veracode.com/press-release/ai-generated-code-poses-major-security-risks-in-nearly-half-of-all-development-tasks-veracode-research-reveals/)).

### For "compare the assistants you've used" (eng-098)

1. **At least two tools used on real work**, not tried once on a weekend.
2. **A comparison on things that matter to you:** how well it handles a lot of code at once, how it plans, how it
   fails, how easy its work is to check, what it costs, and whether your company's data rules allow it.
3. **A task where each was better**, and one where both failed.
4. **Your current default and what would make you switch.**

Sam has six months with Tool A and three weeks with Tool B. That is enough for an honest comparison, as long as
Sam says how much time went into each.

### For "why this role" (eng-111)

1. **What the work is, in the company's terms.** For forward-deployed work, one Pragmatic Engineer piece
   describes a split of about 25% coding, 50% integration and 25% customer-facing
   (<Dated asOf="2026-05-24" src="s-e4c26adb42">Pragmatic Engineer</Dated>).
2. **Evidence from your past that you like that mix**, especially the parts most engineers avoid.
3. **What you would give up**, and why you accept it.
4. **Why this company's version of the role.**

Sam's evidence is the week with the customer's operations team. It is the strongest thing in the table for this
question, and Sam should lead with it.

### For "what would you critique" (infra-106)

1. **Evidence you've read their own material.** Policies, research, product decisions, public statements.
2. **One or two specific critiques**, each with the strongest argument for the company's current choice. Stating
   the other side's best argument before you disagree is called **steelmanning**.
3. **What would change your mind.**
4. **Why you still want to work there.** Without this, a critique sounds like a reason not to hire you.

## Story structure

### The practice answer: workflow, check, failure, limit

For "how do you use AI", give a four-beat answer of about two minutes. Here is Sam's, with a rough clock:

| Clock | Beat | What Sam says (illustrative) |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00 | **Workflow** | "Most days I use a coding agent for bug fixes. I hand it the failing test and the package, and I approve its plan before it writes anything. I also use it to explain code I haven't seen before." |
| 0:40 | **Check** | "For fixes I read the full diff and run the race detector. For explanations, I check two of its claims against the code before I trust the rest." |
| 1:10 | **Failure** | "It once wrote a migration that passed all our tests but would have locked our biggest table. Staging caught it." |
| 1:30 | **Limit** | "Since then I write migrations myself, and I never put customer data into external tools, which is also our policy." |
| 2:00 | Stop | Let the interviewer pick what to dig into. |

That order puts the evidence first and ends on judgment. It also hands the interviewer the natural next question,
"tell me more about that migration", which is the story you prepared best.

### The motivation answer: past, present, specific

For "why this role" and "your view of AI", use three parts of two or three sentences each:

1. **Past.** Something from your past that pulled you toward this work: a real project.
2. **Present.** What you think now about where AI is useful and where it isn't.
3. **Specific.** Why this company's role is the next step.

The OpenAI guide's warning about AGI speeches applies (<Cite id="s-4d55d9b987" />): your view of AI should sound
like an engineer's, with failure modes, not like a keynote. Sam's present view, in three sentences: "It's good at
well-specified changes in code with strong tests, and at explaining unfamiliar code. It fails where correctness
depends on things it can't see, like the size of a production table. So the scarce skill is checking, and that's
the skill I've been building."

### The critique: steelman, critique, evidence, still in

1. **What they're doing, and the best reason for it.**
2. **Your concern, specifically.**
3. **What evidence would resolve it.**
4. **Why you want to work on it anyway**, or how you'd want to help with it.

Keep it to about ninety seconds. The follow-ups will test whether you read the material, so leave room for them.

<AINative title="Practice what you describe, and follow the candidate policy">
Interviewers who ask how you use AI are likely to test it elsewhere in the loop, in an AI-enabled coding round
or a take-home. Make sure the workflow you describe is the one you actually use. And check each company's
rules: Anthropic's guidance asks candidates to draft applications themselves before polishing with Claude,
allows Claude for prep, and says of live interviews "This is all you–no AI assistance unless we indicate
otherwise." (<Dated asOf="2025-07-10" src="s-b4d190c38f">Anthropic candidate AI guidance</Dated>). Using AI to
research the company's published positions before the critique question is fine and sensible. Reciting an
AI-written critique is not, because the follow-ups will go where the summary didn't.
</AINative>

## Small-team version

At a company of 50–500 people hiring its first AI engineer, this family is often the whole interview rather
than one round. The founder or CTO wants to know one thing: will you make the company's use of AI better, or
riskier? Some postings ask for evidence before the interview. One founding AI engineer posting asks applicants
for a short note on something they built with AI
([HN Who is hiring, Ambito, 2026-10-02](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49929368)).

### What to show

- **Workflows you would set up for the team,** not only your own: plan approval before an agent writes code,
  tests written first, and a rule file updated after each agent mistake. This is Sam's loop from the diagram,
  made a team habit.
- **A cost and data view.** Which tools may see which data, what the company spends, and how you would cap it.
- **The failure story,** because at a small company nobody else will catch your agent's mistakes.
- **Where you would not use AI** in the company's product, and why.

### What it costs, in money

Coding agents that run on a provider's API are billed by the **token**, a chunk of text of roughly ¾ of a word.
Input tokens (what the agent reads) and output tokens (what it writes) have different prices. When the agent
re-sends the same text again and again, such as the rule file and files it already read, the provider can
charge a much lower price for those repeated tokens. Those are called **cache reads**.

Assume one engineer's agent reads 2 million tokens a day, of which 1.6 million are cache reads, and writes
200,000 tokens. At Claude Sonnet 5 list prices fetched 2026-10-11, that is about **$3 a day, about $69 a month,
or about $690 a month for ten engineers**. These usage figures are assumptions; your team's will differ.

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the numbers (for a Staff-level follow-up)</summary>

- **Prices (Claude Sonnet 5 list, fetched 2026-10-11):** $2 per million input tokens, $0.20 per million cached
  input tokens, $10 per million output tokens.
- **Assumed daily use for one engineer:** 2M input tokens (1.6M of them cache reads, so 0.4M uncached) and
  0.2M output tokens.
- **One day:** 0.4M × $2 + 1.6M × $0.20 + 0.2M × $10 = $0.80 + $0.32 + $2.00 = **$3.12**.
- **One month:** $3.12 × 22 working days ≈ **$69**.
- **Ten engineers:** ≈ **$690 a month**.

</details>

Your default, said plainly: "One approved assistant, a spend cap set per workspace at the provider, and a
monthly look at spend per engineer. At about $69 a person a month, the money is small next to a salary; the cap
is there to catch a runaway agent, not to ration normal work."

The answer changes when usage is far heavier. The StrongDM team Simon Willison described works to a guideline of
at least $1,000 of tokens per engineer per day, which he calls "more of a business model exercise"
([Willison, 2026-02-07](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Feb/7/software-factory/)). At that level the budget is a
leadership decision, not an engineering default.

### Translating "AI-driven impact"

Large employers are starting to review engineers on how they use AI. Meta reportedly began judging employees in
2026 on how they use AI to "Enhance individual productivity", "Build tools that actively improve team
performance" and "Contribute to Meta's wider AI transformation efforts", with no usage metrics described
([Storyboard18, 2025-11-17](https://www.storyboard18.com/digital/meta-may-lower-appraisals-for-employees-who-dont-use-ai-enough-84313.htm);
also <Cite id="s-c8d8847c6b" />, snippet).

The same three levels work in a small-company interview: what you do yourself, what you built for the team, and
how you would change the company's use of AI. Lead with the second and third, because a small company is hiring
for leverage. For Sam: "I use an agent daily (level one). I wrote the migration lock check into CI, which
protects everyone, not only my changes (level two). Here I'd set up the same loop for the whole team (level
three)."

### The critique at a small company

You won't be asked to critique a mission statement, but a founder may ask what you would change about their AI
roadmap or product. The same structure works: say why they made the current choice, then your concern, the
evidence that would settle it, and why you still want the job.

### Revisit when

If the company already has an AI platform team, your answer shifts from "how I'd set up AI use" to "how I'd work
within the existing setup and improve it". Ask which it is before the interview.

In plain words: a Senior answer is about your own habits. A Staff answer moves from "how I use it" to "how I
changed how we use it", and from "I like the mission" to "here is where I think the mission is hard, and the
part of it I'd work on." Sam's CI lock check is the seed of a Staff answer, because it changed what everyone's
agent-written migrations must pass.

## Follow-ups

<FollowUps>
<FollowUp q="How do you review an AI-generated change differently from a colleague's change? (Listed on the PracHub page, Amazon)">
More skeptical about plausible-looking code and invented APIs, less about style. Run it, read the tests it
wrote, and check edge cases it is known to skip. A colleague can explain intent; an agent's explanation is
another output to verify (<Cite id="s-56187f9727" />).
</FollowUp>
<FollowUp q="What would make you stop using an AI tool for a task you currently use it for? (Listed on the PracHub page, Amazon)">
Name a measurable trigger: rising rework, a class of bug it keeps introducing, a data-policy change, or finding
that checking its output takes longer than doing the task (<Cite id="s-56187f9727" />). Sam stopped using it for
migrations after one near miss, which is an honest example of the last kind.
</FollowUp>
<FollowUp q="Why FDE specifically, not just this company? (Reported, OpenAI recruiter screen)">
The recruiter in the FDE account pressed on exactly this (<Cite id="s-812dbc9385" />). Answer with the
customer-facing work you have chosen before and why you would choose it again.
</FollowUp>
<FollowUp q="Do you have any critique or concerns about this company's approach or direction? (Reported, Anthropic)">
Asked in those words in one account (<Cite id="s-4e04a1689c" />). Use the steelman, critique, evidence, still-in
structure.
</FollowUp>
<FollowUp q="Which model would you pick for this task, and why? (PromptLayer sample)">
PromptLayer wants "specific use cases where they'd choose one model over another" (<Cite id="s-1205b0cc50" />).
Name the things you'd compare (quality on your own eval, speed, cost, data terms) and say you would decide on
your own eval, not on public rankings.
</FollowUp>
<FollowUp q="How do you know AI made you faster? (Not reported; a natural next push)">
Say how you measured, or that you didn't. Cite METR's finding that developers believed they were faster while
measuring slower as the reason you are careful about the claim.
</FollowUp>

## Common mistakes

- **Enthusiasm with no workflow.** PracHub's write-up asks for "Specific workflows, not enthusiasm."
  (<Cite id="s-56187f9727" />); name the task, the input and the check instead.
- **Usage counted as impact.** "AI writes 80% of my code" describes usage, not results; say what shipped and what
  you verified, as the [AI-native guide](/system-design-labs/interviews/ai-native/) argues.
- **A bad-output story where the tool is the villain.** It hides the lesson; make it about the gap in your checks
  and what you changed.
- **Textbook comparisons of assistants.** PromptLayer names them a red flag (<Cite id="s-1205b0cc50" />); talk
  about tasks you actually ran and where each tool failed.
- **"Why FDE" answered as "why this company".** It fails the filter the recruiter in the OpenAI account described
  (<Cite id="s-812dbc9385" />); answer about the work.
- **Flattering the mission, or critiquing it without reading it.** Both show you haven't thought about the
  company; give one specific concern, the company's best counter-argument, and why you still want in.

## What they're really asking

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "'Helping rather than hurting' is a claim about what would have happened without the assistant, so I'd
> measure it against a holdout: a small group of users who keep the old experience. Before launch I'd test
> the assistant offline on real past tickets, with checks for the harms that matter most, like inventing a
> policy or blocking access to a person. After launch I'd compare the two groups on time to resolution,
> recontacts, correct handoffs and harm signals, not on containment or thumbs-up alone. At 40,000
> conversations a day, a 5% holdout shows a 3-point change in about 2 days."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer.

The interviewer wants three things: outcome metrics compared with what would have happened without the
assistant, guard metrics that catch harm, and an honest view of what offline tests can and can't tell you.

### What makes this different from launching a normal feature

For a normal feature you check that it works, ship it, and watch usage. If usage goes up, it probably
helps.

A support assistant built on a large language model (**LLM**) can look successful while doing harm:

1. **It can "solve" problems by giving up on them.** If it stops handing hard cases to a person, the share it
   "solves" goes up, and customers are worse off.
2. **It can be confidently wrong.** A made-up policy sounds as fluent as a real one, and many users won't
   notice until later.
3. **Users can like the wrong thing.** Thumbs-up rates can rise while quality falls.

So usage and model scores aren't enough. You need to compare against a group without the change, and you
need guard metrics that only move when something bad happens.

The prompt is the techinterview.org applied-AI guide's own example for the system-design round, which it
describes as designing "an eval and serving path around a model you don't own" (<Cite id="s-7d03a572e0" />).

### Words you need first

- **Containment**: the share of conversations solved without handing off to a person.
- **Recontact**: the customer comes back about the same problem within a set window (this page assumes 7
  days). A high recontact rate means "solved" wasn't really solved.
- **Holdout**: a group of users kept on the previous experience, so you can compare. This is the
  **counterfactual**: what would have happened without the change.
- **Guard metric**: a number that must not get worse, such as policy violations. It doesn't measure success;
  it catches harm.

## Clarify first

- **Who are the users, and what is the job?** "Let's assume customers with order and billing questions."
- **What's the alternative today?** A human queue, a help centre, or nothing.
- **Which harms matter most?** Wrong policy statements, refunds promised, blocked access to a person.
- **Traffic?** "Let's assume 40,000 conversations a day."
- **Can we hold some users out?** "Can I keep a small group on the current experience for comparison?"

## Answer

### The design, briefly

```
Customer message
   │
   ▼
Assistant ── searches help content, calls tools (order lookup, refund within limits)
   │
   ├── confident and allowed → answers
   └── low confidence, or customer asks for a person → hands off to a human agent
   │
   ▼
Every conversation logged as a trace, tagged with its group (assistant or holdout)
```

The handoff path matters for this question. Without it, "contained" can just mean "the customer gave up".

### Offline, before launch

Build a **golden set**: a fixed list of real past tickets, each with what a good outcome is. Grade with:

- **code checks** where possible: right tool, refund within policy;
- **one pass/fail LLM judge per open-ended failure mode** (a second model call that grades the reply against
  one written rule), checked against human labels before you trust it;
- **must-pass items** for the harms: never invent a policy, never block a request for a human.

Offline tests tell you the assistant handles known cases. They can't tell you whether customers end up
better off. That needs live traffic.

### Online, the real answer

Keep a long-running holdout (say 5% of users, an assumption) on the previous experience. Compare the two
groups:

| Helps if | Hurts if |
|---|---|
| Time to resolution falls | Recontact within 7 days rises |
| Containment rises with a stable correct-escalation rate | Containment rises because escalations fall |
| Customer satisfaction (CSAT) on contained conversations holds | Complaints mention the bot, or users keep typing "agent" |
| Cost per resolved conversation falls | Policy violations or promised refunds appear in sampled review |

Read the table in pairs. Each "helps" signal has a "hurts" signal that would expose a fake win. Rising
containment is good news only if correct escalations hold steady.

Say: "I'd never report containment on its own. It goes up when the bot stops escalating cases it should."

### How long the comparison takes

Say containment is 60% and you want to catch a 3-point drop. You need **about 4,180 conversations in each
group** to have an 80% chance of catching it. A 5% holdout at 40,000 a day gets 2,000 a day, so it
reaches that in **about 2 days**.

### Why both layers: two incidents

User signals can mislead. OpenAI's April 2025 GPT-4o update shipped although expert testers said it "felt
slightly off", on positive signals from users (as quoted by a
[Georgetown Law brief](https://www.law.georgetown.edu/tech-institute/insights/tech-brief-ai-sycophancy-openai/)).

And a support bot can do real harm in ways no usage metric shows. Cursor's support bot invented a login
policy, prompting cancellations ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/article/customer-support-ai-cursor-went-rogue),
April 2025). An offline must-pass item "never state a policy that isn't in the help content", plus a sampled
human review, is how you catch that kind of failure.

Say: "Offline tests catch the failures I can name. The holdout catches the ones I didn't think of."

## Small-team version

- **Build** the holdout with a feature flag you already have, and the metrics from your support tool's data.
- **Buy** nothing new.
- **Read** a weekly sample of 30 contained conversations with a support lead. At an assumed 2 minutes each,
  that is about an hour a week, and it catches what metrics miss.

The holdout costs no tokens (chunks of text that providers bill for), because those users get the old path.
Its real cost is the help you withhold from 5% of users. Shrink it once the effects are established.

## Pitfalls

- **Declaring success on containment or thumbs-up alone.** Both can rise while customers are worse off.
- **No counterfactual.** Without a holdout, a seasonal dip in tickets looks like the assistant's impact.
- **No path to a human.** Then "contained" can mean "gave up".

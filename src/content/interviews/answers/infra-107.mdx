## What they're really asking

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "Two weeks before a launch, I found a security problem that would take a week to fix properly. I cared about the
> deadline, and I cared about our customers' data. I wrote a one-page note with two options, took it to my manager
> and the security lead, and we shipped a narrower version on time. I nearly didn't raise it, because I didn't want
> to be the one slowing things down. Now I raise that kind of risk at design time, and I added a check so nobody
> else has to find it two weeks out."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer. The brackets in the examples below are facts only you
> can supply; the shape is what matters.

The question has two parts: describe a moral conflict you had with your work, and describe a time delivery
pressure ran against a security or safety concern. The interviewer wants to know three things. Do you notice
ethical and safety tension in ordinary engineering work? Do you act on it when there is pressure not to? And can
you reflect on it honestly afterwards?

### What makes this different from a normal behavioral question

In a normal behavioral round, the interviewer mostly wants the result. The usual shape is **STAR** (situation,
task, action, result), and the best stories end with a win.

This round is reportedly different. It is Anthropic's **values round**, a dedicated interview about how you reason
through ethical and safety questions. It cares about your thinking before and after the event, not only the
outcome. A story where you were obviously right and won has no real tension in it. A story with a compromise you
still think about is often stronger.

Two first-hand accounts show what that means in practice. A Staff infra candidate describes the round as
"explicitly about values, safety, and how I reason through moral tension" (<Cite id="s-e9fee3985a" />); that
candidate blames the coding round, not this one, for the outcome. A separate candidate for the Safeguards team
(Anthropic's team that works on preventing misuse) says the recruiter warned that "the company values round was
the one where people usually fail", and that the interviewer kept asking how they felt then and how they feel now
(<Cite id="s-42f1c7f6dd" />).

So plan for a round you can fail, where your feelings and your reflection are part of the answer.

## Clarify first

You don't need much scoping in a behavioral question, but two short checks help:

- "Would you like one story covering both parts, or two stories?" Often one story does both. A deadline that ran
  against a security concern is itself a moral conflict.
- "Is a small-scale example fine?" It almost always is. Say so rather than inflating one.

Don't ask what "moral" means. Pick a situation where two things you value pulled apart.

## Answer

### Pick the story

Most engineers say they have no moral-conflict story, then find several once they look for the right shape. Look
for:

- a launch you were uneasy about, whether or not you said anything;
- a shortcut on security, privacy or testing taken to meet a date;
- a request whose output could be misused;
- a time you stayed quiet and later wished you hadn't.

The last kind is often the strongest, because the reflection is real. You don't need a story where you prevented
harm. You need one where the tension was real and it changed how you act.

### Tell it as a reflective arc

Use five parts instead of STAR. The extra parts (how it felt, how you see it now) are what this round listens for.

1. **Context, two sentences.** What was being built, by when, your role.
2. **The tension.** What pulled against what, and why both sides mattered. Name the stake for people other than
   you: users, customers, colleagues.
3. **What you did.** Who you told, how, what you proposed, what happened. Include what you didn't do.
4. **How it felt then.** One or two plain sentences.
5. **How you see it now.** What you'd keep, what you'd change, and the line you now hold.

### One story, start to finish

Here is the one-minute answer from the top of the page, laid out against a running clock. The clock is a
suggested pace for a first answer of about two and a half minutes, not a rule from any source. The story is
illustrative, not a real one.

| Clock | Part | What you say (illustrative) | What the interviewer hears |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0:00 | **Context** | "Two weeks before launching [a feature that let customers connect their own data sources], I was [the engineer on the ingestion path]." | A deadline and your role, fast. |
| 0:15 | **Tension** | "I found [credentials stored with more scope than needed]. A proper fix was [a week]. My manager had [a customer date]." | Both sides are real: a promise to a customer and a risk to customers' data. |
| 0:45 | **What you did** | "I wrote a one-page risk note with two options, a narrower launch or a one-week slip, and took it to [my manager and the security lead]. We shipped narrower." | Evidence and options, not just an objection. |
| 1:30 | **How it felt** | "I nearly didn't raise it, because I didn't want to be the one slowing things down." | Honesty about the pull toward staying quiet. |
| 1:45 | **How you see it now** | "Now I'd raise it at design time, and I [added a security review to our launch checklist]." | Reflection, plus a change to the process. |

Then stop and let the interviewer push. The follow-up questions are where this round really happens. Expect
"how did you feel about your manager then?", "how do you feel about it now?" and "what if they had said no?".

### How much feeling, and how much detail

**Feeling.** The Safeguards interviewer reportedly wanted the candidate to name discomfort rather than explain it
away (<Cite id="s-42f1c7f6dd" />). That is a request for honesty, not drama. One plain sentence is enough.

Say it like this: "I was frustrated, and I was also a bit scared of being seen as the person who blocks launches.
The second feeling is the one I had to work on."

**Technical detail.** Keep it to what the listener needs to understand the stakes. "Credentials with broader
access than they needed" is enough; the interviewer doesn't need the schema. A poster on Blind (an anonymous forum for tech employees) who thinks this round
"may have been the reason" his Senior Staff loop failed suspects his examples "probably contained too much
engineering detail" (<Cite id="s-e98cbef9cd" />).

**Confidential detail.** Describe people by role ("my manager", "the security lead") and companies by type ("a
fintech product"). Never name colleagues or reveal your employer's secrets. If a story can't be told without them,
pick another.

## Senior vs Staff

<LevelContrast
  title="Delivery versus safety"
  senior={["Raises the concern with evidence and options, accepts a reasonable compromise, and reflects honestly."]}
  staff={["Also changes the process (a design-time review, a checklist item, a named owner who can block) so the next engineer doesn't depend on courage alone."]}
/>

In plain terms: the Senior story shows you did the right thing once. The Staff story shows you made the right
thing easier for the next person. In the example above, that is the last clause: the checklist item you added.

## Small-team version

At a small company there is often no security or safety team. So this situation is your job, not an exception. A
strong story shows that you held a launch, or narrowed it, against your own schedule, and what that cost.

If you have a story where you delayed a feature you built yourself, lead with it, and put a number on the trade. A
one-week slip for two engineers costs about **$7,500** of loaded engineer time, plus whatever the customer date
was worth. Weigh that against the worst realistic outcome you avoided.

Say it like this: "The slip cost us about seven and a half thousand dollars of engineering time and an awkward
call with the customer. A leak of their credentials would have cost the account."

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the numbers (for a Staff-level follow-up)</summary>

- **Loaded cost per engineer-week:** US Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) median developer pay of $135,980 ÷ 0.70
  (salary as a share of total employment cost, an assumption) ÷ 52 weeks ≈ **$3,736 a week**, about $3,700.
- **Two engineers for one week:** 2 × $3,736 ≈ **$7,470**, about $7,500.

</details>

## Pitfalls

- **You were the hero.** If nobody else had a point, there was no tension, and the story sounds rehearsed.
- **Rationalizing instead of naming the discomfort.** Explaining why it was all fine sounds defensive, and this
  round reportedly listens for the opposite.
- **Confidential details or named colleagues.** It undercuts your judgment in the very round that tests it.
- **Too much architecture, too little thinking.** The system is context; your reasoning is the answer.

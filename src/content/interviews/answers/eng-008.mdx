## What they're really asking

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "Each part of a retrieval system trades something. Embeddings find passages by meaning, but they blur exact names and codes,
> so I pair them with keyword search and merge the two ranked lists by position. Chunks follow the document's
> headings and stay within what the embedding model can read. A reranker rereads the top candidates with the
> question and orders them better, but it costs time and can only reorder what search found. I pick each one by
> measuring it on questions with known answers, and I can show the table that justified each choice."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer.

This is a deep-dive round, not a design round. The interviewer wants the reasoning behind each choice, ideally
from something you built.

Some background. **RAG** (retrieval-augmented generation) means: search your documents for passages that answer
the question, then hand those passages to a large language model (**LLM**, such as Claude or GPT) to write the
answer. Three things make it different from the search features most backend engineers know:

1. **The model, not a person, reads the results.** A miss doesn't show up as a bad link; it shows up as a fluent,
   wrong answer.
2. **Each stage can fail on its own.** Parsing, chunking, search and ranking each lose information in different
   ways, so "why this, not that" has an answer per stage.
3. **Choices are measurable.** With a set of questions whose answers you know, every claim like "hybrid helps"
   becomes a number you can check.

A first-hand OpenAI forward-deployed engineer account (year not shown) describes a technical deep dive with "Deep
questions on RAG architecture (embedding selection, chunking, retrieval methods, reranking)". The same
candidate's take-home was probed with "why this chunking strategy, why not a different retrieval method" and
"what would I change if the dataset was 100x larger." (<Cite id="s-812dbc9385" />).

## Answer

### Words you need first

- **Token**: a piece of a word, about ¾ of a word. Models read and bill text in tokens.
- **Chunk**: one passage cut from a document; the unit you search and cite.
- **Embedding**: a list of numbers (a vector) computed from text by a small model, so that texts with similar
  meaning get vectors that point in similar directions.
- **recall@k**: the share of test questions where the passage holding the answer is among the top k results.

### The stages, and what each one decides

Here is the whole path. Each labelled box is explained, in order, below the picture.

```
Document ── parse (keep headings, tables)
   │
   ▼
Chunk ── split on headings; heading path on each chunk; within the embedder's limit
   │
   ▼
Embed (bi-encoder) ── one vector per chunk, computed once, in advance
   │
   ▼  (per question)
Keyword search (BM25) ─┐
                       ├─ merge by rank (RRF) ── top 20–50
Vector search ─────────┘
   │
   ▼
Rerank (cross-encoder) ── rereads each candidate with the question
   │
   ▼
Top k → LLM
```

**Embeddings.** The embedding model is a **bi-encoder**: it turns the question and each chunk into vectors
*separately*. That is why chunk vectors can be computed once, in advance, and search becomes a nearest-neighbour
lookup. Choose the model on your labelled questions (recall@k, compared on the same questions with a paired
test). Then weigh four things: vector size (600,000 chunks × 1,536 numbers × 4 bytes ≈ **3.69 GB**), the input
limit, the price, and whether the data may leave your environment.

Changing the model later means re-embedding everything. The embedding-pipeline lab compared two closely related
models, MiniLM-L6 and MiniLM-L12, and found that only
<LabNumber lab="embedding-pipeline" metric="Neighbour overlap@10" /> of each text's 10 nearest neighbours stayed
the same. A third of the neighbours move, even between sibling models. So swap through a **shadow index** (a
second index built alongside the first) and switch an alias once the new one passes.

**Chunking.** Split on the document's headings, keep tables intact, put the heading path on every chunk, size the
chunk for the question type (a paragraph for facts, a section for procedures), and stay within the embedder's
input limit. Justify it with a sweep on labelled questions.

**Retrieval methods.** Embedding search, also called **dense retrieval**, finds paraphrases. Keyword search with
**BM25** (the standard keyword-ranking formula) finds exact names and codes. Merge the two with **reciprocal rank
fusion** (RRF): each chunk scores 1 ÷ (60 + its rank) in each list, summed. Because it uses ranks, you never have
to put BM25 scores and vector similarities on the same scale.

The keyword side has to be decent, though. The rag lab measured **hit@3** (the share of questions with a correct
document in the top 3) on SciFact, a public benchmark of scientific claims. Hybrid scored no better than dense
alone while BM25 split text on spaces, so "cells," and "cells" counted as different words. With a tokenizer that
splits on word characters (`\w+`), hybrid reached <LabNumber lab="rag" metric="BM25 tokenizer (SciFact)" /> hit@3.
**Metadata filters** (date, product, document type) are often the cheapest quality win of all.

**Reranking.** A **cross-encoder** reads the question and one chunk *together* and outputs a relevance score. It
orders candidates better, at one model call per pair. It only reorders what search found: in the lab, hit@20 didn't
change, and the gains came from promoting relevant chunks into the top 3. And it costs latency:
<LabNumber lab="rag" metric="Cross-encoder rerank, 20 candidates" /> for 20 candidates (CPU / Apple GPU). Use it
when the right chunk is in the top 50 but not the top 5.

### Follow one question through it

Take an employee handbook (an invented example) and the question *"What is the notice period for contractors?"*

| Stage | What happens | The "why this, not that" answer |
|---|---|---|
| Chunk | The answer lives in "Contractors > Ending a contract", one ~300-token chunk with its heading path. | "A fixed window would have mixed it with the benefits section." |
| Keyword search | Ranks the general employee notice section 1st ("notice period" repeated); the contractor chunk 3rd. | "Keywords can't tell which notice period I mean." |
| Vector search | Ranks the contractor chunk 3rd, the employee section 10th. | "Meaning search catches 'ending a contract'." |
| RRF | Contractor chunk 0.0317 (3rd + 3rd) beats the employee section 0.0307 (1st + 10th). | "Agreement beats one search's favourite." |
| Rerank | Reads both with the question; contractor chunk goes to 1st. | "Worth about 0.4 s only if the eval shows chunks stuck at ranks 4–20." |

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the numbers (for a Staff-level follow-up)</summary>

- RRF with constant 60 (the rag lab's value, after Cormack et al. 2009): 1st + 10th → 1/61 + 1/70 ≈ **0.0307**;
  3rd + 3rd → 2/63 ≈ **0.0317**.
- Vector storage: 600,000 × 1,536 × 4 B = **3.69 GB** float32.

</details>

### 100 times the data

Ingestion and storage change; query cost doesn't. Each question still sends the same few chunks. What changes is a
vector store split across machines, vectors stored as 1-byte integers (int8) or half precision instead of 4-byte
floats, parallel re-embedding, re-indexing by content hash (only changed chunks), and fair ingestion queues so a
bulk import doesn't starve daily edits. Say it like this: "At 100× I'd redo the storage and ingestion envelope; the
per-question path stays almost the same."

## Small-team version

The best preparation is the thing you will do on the job anyway. Build a small RAG over real documents, keep a
labelled set, and record why each choice won: a table of recall@k per variant. That table answers every "why this,
not that" follow-up.

**The defaults** at a small company: a hosted embedding model, hybrid search in Postgres (full-text search plus
the pgvector extension), and no reranker until the eval asks for one.

**What it costs**, for the walkthrough's assumed corpus: about **$5** to embed 600,000 chunks and **3.7 GB** of
float32 vectors in the database you already run. The recurring bill is generation: about **$1,800 a month** at
5,000 questions a day on Claude Sonnet 5, or **$270** on gpt-5-mini (prices fetched 2026-10-11).

**When to change.** Add a reranker when the eval shows answers at ranks 4–20. Move off Postgres at millions of
vectors with heavy filtering.

## Pitfalls

- **Textbook definitions with no trade-off or number.** The round asks "why", and a definition doesn't answer it.
- **Claiming hybrid or reranking always helps.** The lab shows hybrid adding nothing with a weak tokenizer; measure
  it.
- **Forgetting that a new embedding model means re-embedding everything.** Vectors from two models can't share an
  index.

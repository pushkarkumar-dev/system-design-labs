## What they're really asking

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "I'd agree a small set of metrics with the owner before launch. One outcome metric tied to the user's job,
> like questions answered without the user searching again. A few quality metrics that explain it, like
> correctness and citation accuracy. Guard metrics that must not get worse: harmful outputs, latency, cost
> per task. Adoption last, because usage can rise while quality falls. Then I'd measure against a holdout
> group that doesn't get the feature, sized so it can actually detect the change, and write down the
> rollback thresholds before launch."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer.

The interviewer wants to see whether you can connect an AI feature to the outcome the business cares about,
and protect that outcome with guard metrics, rather than reporting usage or model scores. Expect follow-ups
on how you would measure it and what would make you roll back.

### What makes this different from metrics for a normal feature

For a normal feature, success metrics are often usage: clicks, active users, conversion. If the feature
works as coded, more usage usually means more value.

A feature built on a large language model (**LLM**) can be used a lot and still fail its users:

1. **It can be confidently wrong.** Users may not notice a wrong answer until much later, so usage looks fine.
2. **People can like the wrong thing.** A flattering or agreeable answer gets thumbs-up even when it's
   unhelpful.
3. **Quality and cost move separately.** A change can make answers better and twice as expensive, or cheaper
   and quietly worse.

So you need a hierarchy: an outcome on top, the quality numbers that explain it, and guards that catch harm
and cost.

Aced's question list tags "How would you define success metrics for an AI-oriented feature or product?" to
Perplexity; it is an aggregator listing with no further detail (<Cite id="s-d7a8e88bf0" />).

### Words you need first

- **Outcome metric**: the one number that says whether the user's job got done.
- **Guard metric**: a number that must not get worse, such as harmful outputs or cost. It catches harm
  rather than measuring success.
- **Holdout**: a group of users who don't get the feature (or keep the old version), so you can compare. It
  measures the **counterfactual**: what would have happened without the feature.
- **Golden set**: a fixed list of real test questions, each with checks for a good answer.

## Clarify first

- **What job does the feature do for the user, and what did they do before it existed?**
- **Who owns the outcome?** A product lead, support, sales.
- **How much traffic, and can we run a holdout?**
- **What harms are possible?** Wrong facts, unsafe content, cost overruns.

## Answer

Build a small hierarchy and agree it with the owner before launch.

### The hierarchy, top to bottom

```
1. Outcome      ── did the user's job get done?           (one metric)
      ▲ explained by
2. Quality      ── correctness, faithfulness, citations, handoff quality
      │ protected by
3. Guards       ── harmful outputs, latency p95, cost per successful task, complaints
      │ and last
4. Adoption     ── active users, repeat use               (interest, not success)
```

**1. One outcome metric**, tied to the user's job:

- for an answer engine: sessions where the user's question was answered without rephrasing it or leaving to
  search elsewhere;
- for a support bot: resolved without the customer coming back;
- for a drafting tool: drafts sent with light edits.

**2. Quality metrics** that explain the outcome: answer correctness and **faithfulness** (the answer says
only what its sources support), measured on a golden set and on sampled production review; citation
accuracy; and how well it refuses or hands off to a person.

**3. Guard metrics** that must not get worse: harmful or policy-violating outputs, latency at the 95th
percentile, cost per successful task, complaint rate.

**4. Adoption** last: active users and repeat use. They show interest but can rise while quality falls.
OpenAI's April 2025 GPT-4o update shipped on positive user signals although expert testers said it "felt
slightly off" (as quoted by a [Georgetown Law brief](https://www.law.georgetown.edu/tech-institute/insights/tech-brief-ai-sycophancy-openai/), 2025-07-30).

Say: "Usage tells me people are trying it. The outcome metric tells me it works. The guards tell me it isn't
working by doing harm."

### Follow one feature: an answer engine

Here is the hierarchy filled in for an answer engine, with the decision it drives. Rates are assumptions.

| Level | Metric | What would make me act | What to say in the interview |
|---|---|---|---|
| Outcome | Answered without rephrasing or leaving, baseline 60% (assumed) | A drop the holdout comparison can see | "This is the number the product lead signs." |
| Quality | Correctness and citation accuracy on the golden set and a weekly sample | A drop on the same items, outside the error bar | "Quality explains why the outcome moved." |
| Guard | Harmful outputs, p95 latency, cost per answered question | Crossing a threshold written before launch | "Rollback thresholds are agreed before launch, not fitted afterwards." |
| Adoption | Daily users, repeat use | Never on its own | "Rising usage with a falling outcome is a warning, not a win." |

### Measure against a holdout, sized for power

To claim the feature caused a change, compare with a holdout or an A/B test. Size it so it can see the
change you care about. **Power** is the chance the test catches a real change; 80% is the usual target.

With a 60% baseline, catching a 3-point change needs **about 4,180 users in each group**, and a 5-point change
about 1,500.

If traffic can't reach that, say so. The offline comparison on the golden set and human review become the
main evidence, and the live metrics act as a smoke alarm. Write down the rollback thresholds on the guard
metrics before launch.

## Small-team version

You will define these with the founder or product lead, often in one meeting.

- **Use** the analytics and feature flags you already have.
- **Add** per-request cost to your request logs from day one.
- **Start with** one outcome, three guards and a weekly transcript review.

The holdout adds no token cost (tokens are the chunks of text providers bill for), because those users get
the old path. Reading 30 transcripts a week at an assumed 2 minutes each is **about an hour** of someone's
time.

## Pitfalls

- **Usage or thumbs-up as the success metric.** Both can rise while the feature gets worse.
- **No guard metrics.** Cost or harm can grow for weeks before anyone looks.
- **Metrics defined after launch to fit the results.** Then the metrics prove nothing.

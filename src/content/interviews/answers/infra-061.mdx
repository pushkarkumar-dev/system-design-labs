## What they're really asking

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "GPU credits are money, so I'd build them like a payments system, not a counter. Every change is an append-only
> ledger entry: grants that expire, holds for work that is about to run, captures for metered use, releases for
> unused holds, and expiries. The balance is computed from the entries. Usage events carry a unique key so a retry
> can't charge twice, and an hourly job reconciles the ledger against what the GPU scheduler says actually ran."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer.

You may picture credits as a number in a column: the customer has 1,000, they use 24, now they have 976. That
works until the first hard question:

- **Which credits expired?** A customer has 100 promotional hours that expire next month and 1,000 purchased hours
  that expire next year. Which ones did yesterday's job use?
- **What if the usage event is retried?** The meter sends "used 8 GPU-minutes" twice after a network blip. Did
  you charge twice?
- **What about work that is still running?** A three-hour job has started. The balance looks fine now, but will it
  cover the job?
- **Does the bill match reality?** Did the GPUs actually run as long as you charged for?

A single balance column answers none of these. A ledger answers all of them. So the interviewer is really asking:
can you build a money-like system on top of a compute system?

A **GPU** is the chip that runs AI training and inference; it is scarce and expensive, so companies sell time on
it. A **GPU-hour** is one GPU for one hour, a common unit for credits.

(The prompt, "design a GPU credit management system", is tagged OpenAI on a company-tagged question list with no
further detail <Cite id="s-5fa409d1c2" />.)

## Clarify first

- **What unit?** "GPU-hours, dollars or tokens? I'll assume GPU-hours, with a conversion for inference."
- **Prepaid or billed afterwards?** "Assume prepaid grants, purchased and promotional, each with an expiry date."
- **What workloads?** "Both long jobs, like training a model further on customer data (fine-tuning), and short
  inference requests."
- **What happens at zero during a job?** "I'd ask. My default is a small overdraft, then save the job's progress
  and stop it."
- **How exact?** "Exact for money; jobs metered per minute."

## Answer

### The ledger entries

Keep append-only entries per organization. Follow one customer, Acme (made up), through one fine-tuning job
estimated at **8 GPUs for 3 hours**:

| Entry | Meaning | Acme's example | What to say in the interview |
|---|---|---|---|
| `grant` | Credit added, with an expiry | +100 promotional (expires 2026-11-01); +1,000 purchased (expires 2027-10-01) | "Each grant keeps its own expiry." |
| `hold` | Credit set aside for work about to run | −24 GPU-hours for the job (8 × 3) | "Admission checks available credit, not the balance." |
| `capture` | Credit actually used, metered | −0.133 GPU-hours each minute (8 GPU-minutes), drawn from the promotional grant first | "Spend the earliest-expiring grant first, and record which grant." |
| `release` | Unused part of a hold, given back | If the job ends after 2.5 hours, +4 GPU-hours | "Holds are estimates; releases correct them." |
| `expire` | What was left of a grant at its expiry | Any promotional hours left on 2026-11-01 | "A scheduled job writes these, after warning the customer." |

### The rules that make it correct

- **Available = grants − captures − open holds − expiries.** New work is admitted only if its hold fits in
  "available". The balance is always computed from entries, never stored as the only truth.
- **Earliest expiry first.** Acme's 100 promotional hours are used before its purchased hours, so the customer
  doesn't lose credit they could have used.
- **Holds in chunks for long jobs.** One hold at the start can be outrun by a job that runs longer than
  estimated. Take a fresh hold every so often as the job runs.
- **Idempotent metering.** Every usage event carries a unique key such as `(job id, minute)`. If the same event
  arrives twice, the second is ignored. Doing something twice with the same effect as once is called being
  **idempotent**.
- **Expiry** is a scheduled job that writes `expire` entries. Warn customers before it runs.
- **Reconciliation.** Every hour, compare captures with the GPU-seconds in the scheduler's own records. The ledger
  is the truth for money; the scheduler is the truth for time. Any gap gets an alert and an owner.

Say: "The ledger and the scheduler should agree. If they don't, someone owns finding out why."

### How it connects to the rest of the system

```
Customer submits a job
   │
   ▼
Gateway / admission ── available credit ≥ hold? if not, refuse with "balance", not "retry later"
   │ writes hold
   ▼
GPU scheduler ── runs the job on 8 GPUs
   │ every minute: usage event (job id, minute, 8 GPU-minutes)
   ▼
Meter ── ignores duplicates by (job id, minute); writes a capture
   ▼
Ledger ◄── hourly reconciliation against the scheduler's GPU-seconds
```

**Gateway and admission.** It refuses work whose hold exceeds available credit. The error must say "out of
balance", because a "try again later" error makes clients retry something that won't succeed.

**GPU scheduler.** It runs the job and is the source of truth for how long the GPUs were busy.

**Meter.** It turns scheduler events into captures, dropping duplicates by their key.

**Ledger.** The append-only entries above, plus the hourly comparison with the scheduler.

### Inference billed in GPU-hours

If short inference requests also draw on GPU-hour credits, you need a conversion. Inference is measured in
**tokens**, pieces of text roughly ¾ of a word each. The model reads the prompt's tokens (**input**) and writes
reply tokens (**output**). At peak, one 8-GPU node writes about 18,500 tokens a second and reads about 56,100
(from the family walkthrough's example). That gives about **0.12 GPU-hours per million output tokens** and **0.040
per million input tokens**. Say that these are floors at full use; a real rate adds headroom for idle capacity.

- **Hold:** 8 GPUs × 3 h = 24 GPU-hours. **Capture per minute:** 8 GPU-minutes = 8 ÷ 60 ≈ 0.133 GPU-hours.
- **Release in the example:** used 8 × 2.5 = 20 GPU-hours, so 24 − 20 = 4 released.

## Senior vs Staff

<LevelContrast
  title="GPU credits"
  senior={["Ledger entries with grants, holds, captures and expiry; earliest-expiry first; idempotent metering."]}
  staff={["Defines the zero-balance policy for running work, the reconciliation between ledger and scheduler with an owner for gaps, and why prepaid exhaustion fails closed while per-minute limits may fail open."]}
/>

In plain words: a Senior answer gets the ledger right. A Staff answer also decides what happens to running work
at zero, who owns a mismatch between ledger and scheduler, and why "out of credit" must block traffic even when a
rate limiter would let it through during an outage (failing closed rather than open).

## Small-team version

**Skip GPU credits unless you resell compute.** The everyday version is a per-customer monthly token or dollar
budget for an AI feature:

- One row per customer per month, with "reserved" and "used" columns.
- Update it in one database transaction when a request starts (reserve) and when it finishes (settle).
- A usage page, so customers aren't surprised by their bill.

**Price the budget from your cost per request.** Take the family walkthrough's assumed feature: about $0.0064
per request on Claude Sonnet 5 (list prices fetched 2026-10-11). A plan that includes 1,000 requests a month costs
you at most **$6.40 per customer** in model spend.

**When the full ledger becomes necessary.** Build the ledger above only if you start reselling GPU time or selling
prepaid credits. Then the money rules (fail closed, idempotent metering, reconciliation) are not optional.

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the numbers (for a Staff-level follow-up)</summary>

- **Cost per request** (assumed 3,000 input tokens with 2,000 cached, 400 output; $2 / $0.20 / $10 per million
  input / cached input / output tokens): $0.0020 + $0.0004 + $0.0040 = $0.0064.
- **Per customer:** 1,000 × $0.0064 = $6.40.

</details>

## Pitfalls

- **A single mutable balance column with no history.** You can't audit it, expire by grant, or undo a mistake.
- **Charging from a grant without recording which grant.** Expiry and refunds then can't be computed.
- **One hold at job start for a job that runs past it.** The job outruns its credit before anyone notices.
- **Non-idempotent usage events.** A retried event charges the customer twice.

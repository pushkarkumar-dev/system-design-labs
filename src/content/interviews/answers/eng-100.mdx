## What they're really asking

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "Customers were opening tickets for questions our help center already answered, because search couldn't find the
> article. I built an assistant that finds the right passages and has an LLM answer from them, with links. I owned
> the retrieval design and the eval. The hard call was adding keyword search to meaning-based search, because the
> eval showed error codes failing. It answers 85% of 200 real questions correctly, at under a cent each. I can go
> deeper on retrieval or on the eval."
>
> The rest of this page explains how to build your own version of that answer.

The interviewer is asking: can you explain a system you designed so that its stakes, your decisions and its
result are clear in a few minutes? In the reported Meta screen this was the opening section, and the candidate
wrote afterwards: "I probably did not make the project sound impressive enough" (<Cite id="s-a7b843c47f" />).

### Why AI projects are easy to undersell

When you describe a normal backend project, the result usually explains itself: "p99 latency went from 800 ms to
120 ms". An AI project built on a large language model (an **LLM**) has no such built-in number. Its output is
text, and "the answers got better" sounds vague. So candidates fall back on adjectives ("it was really powerful")
or on background, and run out of time before reaching anything the interviewer can remember.

The fix is not adjectives. It is structure: the problem's size, one hard decision, one number. You need three
plain ideas to do that:

- **Baseline**: the simpler thing you compared against (keyword search, rules, the old manual process). Without
  one, "it works well" has nothing to be better than.
- **Eval set**: a fixed list of test inputs with known good answers. Scoring the system on it gives you a
  **quality rate**, such as "85% correct on 200 questions". This is the AI project's equivalent of the p99.
- **Cost per request**: hosted models charge per **token** (a piece of text about ¾ of a word long), so each
  request has a price you can state, usually in fractions of a cent.

## Clarify first

- **How long is this section?** In a screen it may be 5 to 10 minutes before coding. Prepare a 3-minute version
  and a 10-minute version.
- **Breadth or depth?** Offer the 3-minute overview, then ask which part to go into.
- **Is a diagram expected?** Have one you can describe in words if there is no whiteboard.

Say it like this: "I'll give you a three-minute overview, then you can pick the part to go deeper on."

## Answer

### The 3-minute version, with a running clock

The example is illustrative: a help-desk assistant that answers customer questions from a product's help-center
articles. Replace every value with your own.

| Clock | Part | What it covers | What to say in the interview |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:30 | **1. Problem and stakes** | Who had the problem, how big it was, what it cost before. A number here (hours a week, tickets, error rate) does more than any adjective. | "A large share of tickets asked questions the help center already answered. Each one cost an agent several minutes." |
| 0:30–1:00 | **2. What you built, and why an LLM** | The approach in one sentence and the baseline it beat or replaced. | "Keyword search was the baseline. It failed whenever customers didn't use the article's words." |
| 1:00–1:20 | **3. Your role** | The decisions you made and the parts you built, with I-statements. What teammates or coding agents did, and what you verified. | "I owned retrieval and the eval. A teammate owned ingestion. An agent wrote the loaders; I wrote their tests." |
| 1:20–2:05 | **4. The hard decision** | One fork in the road: the option you rejected, the criterion, the evidence. | "Meaning-based search alone was simpler. The eval showed it missing error codes, so I added keyword search." |
| 2:05–2:35 | **5. The result** | The eval number with its sample size, and an outcome number (adoption, time saved, cost per request). | "85% on 200 real questions, plus or minus 5. About two-thirds of a cent per answer." |
| 2:35–3:00 | **6. What you'd change** | One technical and one process change. | "I'd store article versions from day one, and build the eval set before the prototype." |

Then stop and offer a choice: "I can go deeper on the retrieval design or the evaluation." Ending with a choice
keeps the interviewer in the conversation. That matters in a screen that continues into AI-assisted coding,
because the rapport carries over.

### Why this order works

Part 1 gives the interviewer a reason to care. Part 2 answers "why an LLM?" before they ask it. Part 3 lets them
separate your work from the team's. Part 4 is the part they will remember, because it shows how you think. Part 5
turns "it worked well" into a number. Part 6 shows you can criticise your own work, which reads as seniority.

### Explain the result number in plain words

An eval number needs its sample size, because an eval set is a sample. With 200 questions at 85%, the true rate
could plausibly be anywhere from about 80% to 90%; with 50 questions the range is about 75% to 95%. Say the
range, not just the point. It shows you know the number is an estimate.

Say it like this: "85% on 200 questions, so somewhere around 80 to 90. Good enough to beat search, which was at
about half that on the same questions." (The search figure is illustrative; use your own baseline.)

### Rehearse it

Practise it aloud and time it. Underselling usually comes from spending two minutes on context and running out
before the decision and the number. If your first run takes five minutes, cut background, not the decision.

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the numbers (for a Staff-level follow-up)</summary>

- 95% error bar: ± 1.96 × √(p × (1 − p) ÷ n). For p = 0.85: n = 200 gives ± 4.9 points; n = 50 gives ± 9.9.
- Cost per answer (Claude Sonnet 5 list prices fetched 2026-10-11; token counts assumed): 1,000 uncached input ×
  $2 per million + 2,000 cached input × $0.20 per million + 400 output × $10 per million = **$0.0064**.

</details>

## Senior vs Staff

A Senior candidate tells the story in order. A Staff candidate opens with the decision the system changed (for example, "we stopped hiring for tier-1 support") and puts the cost on the table before anyone asks.

## Small-team version

Small-company projects are easy to undersell because the system looks small. Reframe the scope: you owned
discovery with the business owner, the build, the eval, the rollout, the cost and the on-call.

Say what you chose not to build, and the evidence that let you skip it:

- **Self-hosting** (running the model on your own GPUs instead of calling an API);
- **fine-tuning** (further training the model on your own examples);
- a **dedicated vector store** (a separate database for embeddings) while Postgres was enough.

That is the same judgment a larger company pays Staff engineers for.

Quote the bill the same way every time: requests a day × cost per request × 30. The family walkthrough's example,
$0.0064 per request at 50,000 requests a day, is about **$9,600 a month** at Claude Sonnet 5 list prices fetched
2026-10-11 ($0.0064 × 50,000 × 30).

Say it like this: "I chose not to self-host or fine-tune. The eval showed retrieval plus a good prompt was enough,
and the whole thing costs about $9,600 a month."

## Pitfalls

- **Spending most of the time on background.** You run out before the result, which is the part they remember.
- **No number anywhere.** Then "it worked well" is all the interviewer can write down.
- **Describing the team's work as yours, or yours as the team's.** Either way the ownership probe fails.
- **Choosing a project that doesn't match the team.** The interviewer can't map your experience to their work.
- **Adjectives instead of evidence.** "Powerful" and "cutting-edge" don't make a project sound impressive; stakes
  and a hard decision do.

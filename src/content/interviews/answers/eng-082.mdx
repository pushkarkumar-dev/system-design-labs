## What they're really asking

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "Both are capable models, and for many tasks the difference is small, so I won't try to win a feature debate.
> Can I ask what you'd want the assistant to do, for whom, and what a wrong answer would cost? Then I'd suggest we
> run both on 50 of your real questions, graded by your people, with the pass bar agreed before anyone sees the
> results. Whichever wins, start with one team, and keep the model swappable."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer.

The interviewer plays a skeptical executive, here the chief technology officer (CTO). In the version behind this
question they say: "Honestly, I'm not sure Claude does anything ChatGPT can't already do — convince me."
(<Cite id="s-2f725b952e" />). Claude and ChatGPT are both **large language models** (LLMs): models that read text
and write text, rented through a paid API.

They want to see whether you stay calm, find out what the buyer needs, and replace a debate about brands with a way
to decide.

### Why this is different from a normal sales or design conversation

With most software, you can settle "can it do X?" by checking a feature list. With models, both sides can usually
"do X" in some form. What differs is how well they do it on this customer's own data, and that changes with each
model release. So any claim you make from memory is weak, and the executive will remember an overclaim.

The useful move is to change the question from "which brand is better?" to "which result on your data, at what
cost and risk?". That is a question you can answer with a test.

The source is a vendor blog with no sources (<Cite id="s-2f725b952e" />). A prep guide describes the round as
discovery without a code editor, run by engineers playing executives, and calls it "the gatekeeper"
(<Cite id="s-a982325743" />). Both are low confidence. The blog itself says the reflex is to pitch.

## Clarify first

- **The job.** What would the assistant do, and for whom? Assume employees asking policy and product questions.
- **The doubt.** What does "can't do" mean to them: quality, cost, security, integration? Assume they don't know yet.
- **History.** Have they tried ChatGPT or another tool already? What happened? Assume a pilot that stalled.
- **Data.** What data would it see, and who may see what? Assume permissions matter.
- **The decision.** How will they decide? Assume nobody has defined it. That is your opening.

Say it like this: "Before I compare anything, can I ask what you'd want it to do and how you'd decide it worked?"

## Answer

### Words you need first

- A **bake-off** is a side-by-side test: run both models on the same real questions and grade the answers.
- An **eval** (short for evaluation) is the test set used for that: real questions, each with an expert's judgment of
  a good answer, and a score.
- A **token** is the unit a model reads and writes, roughly ¾ of a word. Providers bill per token.

### Five moves, with what to say

| Move | What you do | What to say in the interview |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Don't argue features | Acknowledge both are capable and the gap is often small. You can't win a spec war from memory. | "For many tasks the difference is small, and I'd rather not make claims you'd have to take on trust." |
| 2. Ask what "doing" means for them | Run the questions above, then reflect back what you heard. | "So the real risk is people getting answers from documents they shouldn't see, and the real test is whether answers cite the right policy." |
| 3. Offer a decision rule | Propose a bake-off on their data. | "Let's run both on 50 of your real questions, graded by your people, with the pass criteria agreed before anyone sees the outputs." |
| 4. Name what really differs | List things to check in current documentation, not to assert. | "The differences that matter for you are contract terms, where it runs, and price on your text. Let's check those in writing." |
| 5. Lower the stakes | Start small, keep the model swappable. | "Start with one team and one document set, with the model behind a thin interface. Everything else we build carries over." |

**Move 2 in more detail.** To **reflect back** is to repeat the problem in your own words and check you heard it
right. It shows you listened, and it often reveals that the real concern isn't the model at all.

**Move 3 in more detail.** Compare the two models in pairs: same question, both answers, which one passed. This
**paired comparison** cancels out how hard each question is. Agree up front that a gap of a few points means "decide
on cost and terms", because small evals can't resolve small gaps. With 50 questions, each model's pass rate is
uncertain by roughly ±10 points, so a 3-point difference tells you nothing.

**Move 4 in more detail.** These are the differences that matter for a company, each to be checked in current
documentation:

- Data retention and whether their data is used for training.
- Which clouds and regions it runs in.
- How it behaves on their long documents.
- Connector and tool support.
- Rate limits.
- Price after **tokenization**: how text is split into tokens. Tokenizers differ between vendors, so the same text
  can be a different number of tokens, and the price per token alone misleads.

**Move 5 in more detail.** Propose "one team and one document set" first (<Cite id="s-a982325743" />), with the model
behind a thin interface of your own. The search over their documents, the permission checks and the eval all carry
over whichever model wins.

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the numbers (for a Staff-level follow-up)</summary>

- **Uncertainty with 50 questions:** half-width ≈ 1.96 × √(p(1 − p) ÷ n). At an 85% pass rate and n = 50:
  1.96 × √(0.85 × 0.15 ÷ 50) ≈ 0.099, so about ±10 points for each model.
- That is why the decision rule must be agreed before the results: a few points of difference is inside the noise.

</details>

If you are interviewing at the model vendor, advocacy is fine. But the credible form of it is confidence in the
bake-off.

### A short run of the conversation

Here is how the opening might go, as an illustration:

1. **CTO:** "Convince me." **You:** "I'd rather show you than convince you. What would people use it for?"
2. **CTO:** "Policy questions from staff. Last year's pilot gave people answers from HR files they shouldn't see."
   **You:** "So access control matters more than which model writes the answer."
3. **You:** "Then let's pick 50 real questions, have your HR lead grade both models' answers, and also check that
   neither ever cites a document the asker can't open."
4. **CTO:** "And if they tie?" **You:** "Then we pick on price, terms and where the data is stored."

## Senior vs Staff

<LevelContrast
  title="Skeptical executive"
  senior={["Avoids the pitch, runs discovery, proposes a fair bake-off and a narrow pilot."]}
  staff={["Changes the decision from 'which brand' to 'which result on our data at what cost and risk', gets the criteria agreed in the room, and keeps the architecture vendor-neutral where it costs little."]}
/>

In plain words: a Senior answer doesn't take the bait and suggests a fair test. A Staff answer changes what the
executive is deciding, and gets the rules of the test agreed before anyone sees a result.

## Small-team version

The in-house version is the CEO asking "why not just give everyone ChatGPT?". Sometimes that is the right answer
for general use.

Build only where company data, permissions or workflow integration matter. Settle the model choice with a
50-question bake-off on your own data rather than opinions.

The bake-off is cheap in model calls: about **$1.10** for 50 questions on two models. The real cost is a few hours
of the grader's time, so book that first.

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the numbers (for a Staff-level follow-up)</summary>

50 questions × 2 models = 100 calls. At about 4,000 input and 300 output tokens each on Claude Sonnet 5's list
price ($2 / $10 per million, fetched 2026-10-11), one call is about $0.011, so 100 calls ≈ $1.10. This assumes the other model's price is similar; check its
pricing page.

</details>

## Pitfalls

- **Reciting benchmark scores or feature lists from memory.** They go stale with each release, and one wrong claim
  costs you the room.
- **Being defensive about the competitor.** It turns a decision into a fight you can't win.
- **Proposing a company-wide rollout to win the argument.** It raises the stakes when your job is to lower them.

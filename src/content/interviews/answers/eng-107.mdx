## What they're really asking

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "I was sure that adding a reranker would fix our assistant's answer quality, because it had helped a lot on a
> public benchmark. I tested it on 50 questions I wrote myself, not on real traffic. In production, quality didn't
> move and every answer got slower. The real problem was out-of-date documents. I rolled it back, told the team I'd
> skipped testing on real questions, and since then every quality change runs on a sample of real questions first.
> It was embarrassing, and the habit it left is the useful part."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer, so you can build the same shape from your own work.
> (That story and its numbers are invented to show the level of detail. Use your own.)

The interviewer is asking whether you change your mind when the evidence says so, and whether you can talk about a
real mistake honestly, without dressing it up.

### What makes this question different

Most "failure" questions you have answered before let you tell a recovery story: something broke, you fixed it,
the system is better. This one is about a **belief**. You were confident, the confidence was misplaced, and the
question is what that taught you about how you decide.

Two things make it harder than it looks:

1. **The mistake must be real.** It has to have cost something: time, money, a customer's trust, a colleague's
   work. Interviewers hear disguised strengths as what they are.
2. **The reflection counts as much as the facts.** Especially in a **values round**, an interview about how you
   reason through honesty, safety and hard trade-offs, they will ask how it felt and what changed in you.

The prompt comes from an aggregator list tagged Anthropic (<Cite id="s-d7a8e88bf0" />), which advises showing how
you debugged and what you learned, with "honest, specific examples ready, not rehearsed mission-speak". One
Anthropic candidate describes a values interviewer who "kept pushing on how I felt then and how I feel now" and
seemed to want them "to name the discomfort instead of rationalizing it away" (<Cite id="s-42f1c7f6dd" />).

## Clarify first

- **What kind of belief.** "A technical belief, or a judgment about people or priorities?" Either is fine; a
  technical one is easier to make concrete.
- **How real.** "Should it be something that reached production?" A story with real cost is stronger, so ask
  whether a production example is preferred.
- **One story or a pattern.** Check whether they want one story or how you handle being wrong in general. Usually
  it is one story, then the pattern as a follow-up.

## Answer

### Words you need first

The example is about an AI assistant that answers questions from a company's documents.

- **Retrieval** is the step where the assistant searches the documents for passages relevant to a question and
  pastes them into the text sent to the AI model.
- A **reranker** is a second model that re-sorts those search results, putting the most relevant passages first,
  before the AI model reads them. It often improves answers, and it adds some delay to every request.
- A **public benchmark** is a standard test set that everyone uses to compare techniques. Doing well on one says
  little about your own documents and your own users' questions.

### Choose a real mistake

It has to be a belief you held with confidence that turned out false, and it has to have cost something. "I was
wrong that my design would only scale 10×; it scaled 100×" is a humble-brag, and interviewers hear it as one.

Good material is a belief that sounded sensible at the time, because it was. Your reasoning should not sound
foolish; the gap should be in what you checked.

### The story's shape

```
The belief ──────────── what you were sure of, and why it was reasonable
   ▼
Checked vs skipped ──── the honest gap in your evidence
   ▼
How you found out ───── the metric, customer or colleague that showed it
   ▼
The first hour ──────── rolled back, said it was your call
   ▼
How it felt ─────────── one or two plain sentences
   ▼
What changed ────────── a habit specific enough to observe
```

### One story, followed through

| Step | What happens in the story | What to say in the interview |
|---|---|---|
| **1. The belief** | A reranker would fix answer quality. It had helped a lot on a public benchmark. | "The reasoning was sound. The evidence was thin." |
| **2. Checked vs skipped** | I tested on 50 questions I wrote. I didn't test on real users' questions. | "My questions were the kind I expected. Users asked different ones." |
| **3. How you found out** | After two weeks in production, the weekly quality sample hadn't moved. Each answer was about 300 ms slower. | "The weekly sample was flat, and the latency chart wasn't." |
| **4. The first hour** | I rolled it back and told the team it was my call to skip the real-traffic test. | "I said it was my call before anyone asked." |
| **5. How it felt** | Embarrassing, because I had argued hard for it. | "I was embarrassed. I'd been the loudest voice for it." |
| **6. The real cause** | Many wrong answers came from out-of-date documents. Re-sorting stale passages couldn't fix them. | "Better sorting of the wrong documents is still the wrong documents." |
| **7. What changed** | Every quality change now runs on a sample of real production questions first. I write down the result I expect before each experiment. | "I now write the expected result down first, so I can't move the goalposts." |

**Step 2 is where honesty shows.** Name exactly what you didn't check. Vague gaps ("I should have tested more")
sound like you still haven't found the real one.

**Step 5 should be short and plain.** One or two sentences. Don't perform it, and don't skip it. If the
interviewer asks "and how do you feel about it now?", answer that too: "Mostly grateful it happened on a small
change."

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the numbers (for a Staff-level follow-up)</summary>

- **Why 50 self-written questions misled.** Fifty questions is a small sample, and questions you write yourself
  share your blind spots. Even a perfect score on them says nothing about question types you didn't think of.
- **What 300 ms cost.** If every answer gets about 300 ms slower and quality doesn't move, the change is pure loss.
  The 300 ms is an invented value; a real reranker's added delay depends on the model and how many passages it
  sorts.
- **Writing the expected result down.** If you had written "expect a 5-point rise on the weekly sample", a flat
  result after two weeks is a clear fail. Without the written number, it is easy to say "give it another week".
  The 5 points is an assumed example.

</details>

### The AI-era version

A good modern variant is a time you trusted AI output with confidence: a coding agent's migration that looked
right, a model's summary you forwarded without checking. Keep the story about your verification gap, not the
tool's flaws. Say it like this: "The tool did what tools do. The gap was that I didn't check."

## Senior vs Staff

<LevelContrast
  title="Confident and wrong"
  senior={["Owns the mistake, fixes it quickly, and adds a check."]}
  staff={["Names the reasoning habit that produced the mistake, changes a team-level practice, and says how they now invite disagreement before committing."]}
/>

In the example, the Senior version ends with "I rolled it back and now test on real questions". The Staff version
names the habit underneath (trusting a public benchmark over your own users), makes the real-traffic sample a team
rule, and asks a skeptic to review any change where you are the only believer.

## Small-team version

When you are the only AI owner, nobody reviews your confident calls. The useful habit to show is building that
review in on purpose:

- **A written expected result** before each change.
- **An eval run before rollout,** on real questions, not ones you wrote. (An **eval set** is a fixed list of real
  questions with known good answers, used to score changes the way a test suite does.)
- **A colleague asked to argue the other side,** even one outside engineering.

A small-team story where you caught your own mistake through a check you had set up for yourself is strong
evidence. It shows the habit works without anyone watching.

## Pitfalls

- **A disguised strength.** A "mistake" that cost nothing tells the interviewer you won't name a real one.
- **Blaming the data, the tool or a colleague.** The question is about your belief; blaming others dodges it.
- **Skipping or dwelling on the feelings.** Skipping it misses what values interviewers ask for; dwelling on it
  takes time from what you changed.
- **A lesson nobody could check.** "I'm more careful now" can't be observed; "I write the expected result down
  first" can.

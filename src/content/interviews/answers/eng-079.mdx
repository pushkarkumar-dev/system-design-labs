## What they're really asking

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "Before I design anything, I'd like to ask a few questions. Who will use this and what do they do with the
> output? What happens today and what does it cost? What would success look like, as a number? What data would it
> see, does it only answer or also take actions, and what must never go wrong? Then I'll propose one small first
> version, a test set of about 50 real examples, and a date when we decide whether to widen it."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer.

The interviewer plays a customer with a vague request, such as "we want to use AI on our knowledge base". They want
to see whether you find out what the customer needs before you design.

### Why this is not the requirements meeting you already know

You have gathered requirements for normal software. There, once you know what the system must do, a correct design
gives correct results every time.

A feature built on a **large language model** (LLM: a program like ChatGPT or Claude that reads text and writes text
back, usually rented through a paid API) is different in two ways:

1. **It is sometimes wrong, and it sounds sure of itself when it is.** So "who sees a wrong answer, and what does it
   cost them?" is a design question, not a detail.
2. **"Does it work?" is a measurement.** No unit test proves the replies are good. You need real examples graded by
   someone who knows the domain, and a number.

Because calling a model is the easy part, naming one early answers the wrong question. My reading is that the round
is largely decided early: whether you start from users and success, and whether your questions visibly change the
design.

In the first-hand account behind this question, an OpenAI forward deployed engineer (FDE) interview, the candidate
started on technical design and "The interviewer stopped me and asked "what questions would you ask the customer
before designing anything?"" (<Cite id="s-812dbc9385" />).

## Clarify first

Here, clarifying *is* the answer. Group your questions, and for each have an assumption ready in case the
role-player won't answer:

- **Users and job.** Who uses it, and what do they do with the output? Assume internal staff first, not end
  customers.
- **Today and its cost.** How is it done now, how long does it take, what does it cost? Assume you'll measure it.
- **Success.** What number would make this a win? Assume you'll propose one and get it agreed.
- **Shape.** How many requests, how fast must replies be, and does it only answer or also take actions? Assume it
  only answers (read-only) for phase one.
- **Data.** Where does the knowledge live, how much is there, how fresh is it, and who may see what? Assume
  permissions matter.
- **History and risk.** What was tried before, and what must never happen?

Say it like this when the customer won't commit: "I'll assume internal staff and read-only for now. Tell me if
that's wrong."

## Answer

### Words you need first

- An **eval** (short for evaluation) is a test set for a model feature: real examples, each with an expert's
  judgment of a good answer, plus a score. It does the job a test suite does for normal code.
- A **labelled example** is one input plus that expert judgment.
- **Retrieval** means searching the customer's documents for the passages relevant to a question and adding them to
  the text sent to the model (the **prompt**). You need it when the knowledge is too big to send every time.

### Run it as a conversation, not a questionnaire

Restate the ask, ask permission to dig, then go from outcome, to data, to risk. After each answer, say what it
changes. Here is one example customer, a software company whose head of support asks for "AI on our help centre".
The answers are illustrative.

| You ask | They answer | What it changes | What to say in the interview |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who uses the output? | "Our support agents." | Staff catch mistakes before customers see them. | "Since agents see these drafts before customers do, a wrong answer costs an edit, so we can start there." |
| What does it cost today? | "About 6 minutes a ticket." | Gives the baseline to beat. | "Then success is time saved per ticket, which we can measure." |
| What is success? | "Faster replies, I guess." | Becomes the eval and the decision to widen. | "Can we pick a number now and agree what result would make us stop?" |
| Where is the knowledge? | "400 help articles, some out of date." | Needs retrieval and a freshness check. | "Out-of-date articles produce confident wrong answers, so let's sample them first." |
| What was tried? | "A chatbot pilot. Customers hated it." | Rules out a customer-facing bot first. | "Then let's not start by having customers talk to a bot." |
| What must never happen? | "Promising refunds." | Defines the worst case. | "Anything about refunds goes to a human." |

Then reflect back the problem in one sentence and confirm it: "So the pain is agents' time on repeat questions, and
the worst case is a wrong promise. Is that right?"

### Only then design, and keep it narrow

1. **A first slice.** One team, one document set, one job, read-only or approved by a person. A **slice** is one
   small piece of the feature that works end to end.
2. **A success metric as an eval.** 50 labelled real examples, graded by the customer's domain expert, with the pass
   criteria agreed now. Fifty examples give roughly **±10 points** around the measured pass rate. That is enough to
   tell whether the idea works at all, not to pick between two close options.
3. **Phases.** First **shadow mode** (the feature runs on real traffic, but people only look at its output; nothing
   acts on it). Then a go/no-go meeting on the agreed number. Then widen one team or one action at a time.
4. **Components last.** Retrieval if the knowledge doesn't fit in the prompt, one model behind a thin interface of
   your own, a review step in the tool users already use, and a **trace** per request (a record of every step the
   request took, with timings).

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the numbers (for a Staff-level follow-up)</summary>

The ±10 points is a 95% confidence interval for a pass rate: the range the true rate very likely falls in.

- Formula: half-width ≈ 1.96 × √(p(1 − p) ÷ n).
- At an 85% pass rate and 50 examples: 1.96 × √(0.85 × 0.15 ÷ 50) ≈ 0.099, so about **±10 points**.
- At 200 examples it is about ±4.9 points. Four times the examples only halves the interval.

</details>

A prep guide's framing matches: "strong candidates start from the customer and the definition of success; weak ones
start naming models" (<Cite id="s-26f0cae3fe" />).

Say it like this to close: "So phase one is drafts for one team, measured against 50 tickets your lead grades, with a
go/no-go in four weeks. I'll name components once we agree on that."

## Senior vs Staff

<LevelContrast
  title="Open customer scenario"
  senior={["Asks the right questions in a sensible order and turns the answers into a narrow first slice with an eval."]}
  staff={["Also puts rough numbers on value versus cost, identifies who owns the labels and the decision, and states what result would stop the project."]}
/>

In plain words: a Senior answer asks good questions and lets them shape a small plan. A Staff answer also says what
the project is worth, who decides, and what result would end it.

## Small-team version

Inside a 50–500 person company, the customer is the head of support or the CEO. This conversation should happen
before every AI project.

Spend two days on it, including labelling 50 real examples with the owner. That costs about **$1,500** of engineer
time. Building the wrong thing for an assumed three weeks costs about **$11,200**.

Skip the formal round only when the owner already has a labelled set and an agreed metric.

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the numbers (for a Staff-level follow-up)</summary>

- Loaded engineer cost: $194K a year. That is the
  [BLS](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/computer-and-information-technology/software-developers.htm) median developer wage of
  $135,980 (May 2025) ÷ 0.70, because wages are about 70% of total compensation.
- One working day: $194K ÷ 260 ≈ $747. Two days ≈ $1,500.
- Three weeks (15 days) ≈ $11,200.

</details>

## Pitfalls

- **Naming the architecture in the first minute.** Mentioning retrieval, a vector database (a store built for meaning-based search) or a model before you know
  the users is exactly what got the eng-079 candidate stopped.
- **Asking, then ignoring the answers.** If your design would be the same whatever they said, the questions were for
  show.
- **A success metric nobody owns.** Without an expert who grades examples, the pilot ends in opinions.
- **The whole company as phase one.** Wide launches skip the measurement that tells you whether to widen.

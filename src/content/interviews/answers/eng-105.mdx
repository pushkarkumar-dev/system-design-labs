## What they're really asking

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "I'll spend about twenty minutes on one project and leave the rest for your questions. It's an assistant that
> answers customer questions from our help center. I'll show the problem and its size, why we used an LLM, exactly
> what I owned, the one design choice I'd defend hardest, how we measured quality, what broke after launch, and
> what it cost. Interrupt me any time; the questions are the useful part."
>
> The rest of this page explains how to build that presentation.

The interviewer is asking whether you can present a substantial project to a senior audience and defend its
trade-offs and your role under questioning. interviewing.io tells candidates to be ready to discuss "what
tradeoffs were made", "your level of contribution" and "what everyone's responsibilities were"
(<Cite id="s-74b419b5c0" />). Hello Interview says Staff reports highlight "personal ownership, alternatives
considered, and impact" (<Cite id="s-4d55d9b987" />).

### What makes an AI project presentation different

A presentation about a normal backend project can lean on numbers that explain themselves: requests per second,
p99 latency, incidents per quarter. A senior manager hears "p99 went from 800 ms to 120 ms" and knows it worked.

A project built on a large language model (an **LLM**) produces text, and "the answers got better" proves nothing.
So three slides that a backend talk can skip become essential:

1. **A quality number.** Score the system on an **eval set** (a fixed list of test inputs with known good
   answers) and give the result with its sample size: "85% on 200 questions".
2. **A comparison.** Show the **baseline**, the simpler option you tried first (keyword search, rules, the manual
   process), on the same eval set.
3. **A cost per request.** Hosted models charge per **token** (a piece of text about ¾ of a word long), so each
   request has a price, and a senior audience expects you to know it.

The rest is what any project presentation needs: stakes, ownership, one hard decision, a failure, and what you'd
change.

## Clarify first

The sources disagree on the format, so ask the recruiter.

- **Length.** interviewing.io describes a 45-minute presentation (<Cite id="s-74b419b5c0" />). Hello Interview
  says candidates describe "a roughly 30-minute presentation followed by detailed questions", with one account of
  15 minutes of questions inside a one-hour round, and advises following your invitation's duration
  (<Cite id="s-4d55d9b987" />).
- **Slides.** interviewing.io: "Prepare slides – this is expected, not just advisable." Hello Interview only says a
  discussion of past work "may require slides" and suggests asking.
- **Audience.** interviewing.io says a senior manager; ask whether engineers join.
- **Past project or take-home?** Hello Interview notes some deep dives review the take-home instead.

Say it like this, to the recruiter: "How long is the presentation, how much of it is questions, and are slides
expected? And will engineers be in the room as well as the manager?"

## Answer

### Plan a 20-minute core that stretches to 30

Use about 10 slides. The example column is illustrative (a help-desk assistant that answers customer questions
from a product's help-center articles); replace it with your own project.

| Slide | Content | Help-desk example | What to say in the interview |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Problem and stakes, with a number | Tickets that the help center already answered | "A large share of tickets asked things the help center already answered." |
| 2 | Why this approach (the baseline; why an LLM, if relevant) | Keyword search failed on paraphrased questions | "Search was the baseline. It failed when customers didn't use the article's words." |
| 3 | Your role: decisions made, parts built, who owned the rest | Retrieval and eval were yours; ingestion a teammate's | "I owned retrieval and the eval. A teammate owned ingestion." |
| 4–5 | Architecture, and the option you rejected with the evidence | Meaning-based search alone versus adding keyword search | "The simpler option missed error codes. The eval showed it, so I added keyword search." |
| 6 | Evaluation: metric, eval set size, baseline vs result, error bar | 85% on 200 real questions, ± 5 points | "85% on 200, so roughly 80 to 90." |
| 7 | What broke, and the change it caused | Answers quoted an old version of a changed article | "Now every passage carries its article version." |
| 8 | Timeline and cost (phases; cost per request or infra cost) | Ten weeks; about $0.0064 a question | "Review was the longest phase. Under a cent a question." |
| 9 | Impact: adoption, other teams that used it | Another team reused the retrieval service | "Another team now calls the same service." |
| 10 | What you'd do differently; what changes at 10× | Build the eval first; at 10× traffic the model quota binds first | "Next time the eval set comes before the prototype." |

### Why each slide is there

**Slides 1–3** answer "why should I care?" and "what did *you* do?" before the interviewer asks. **Slides 4–5**
show how you think: Hello Interview warns that a polished summary "won't cover why you rejected another approach"
(<Cite id="s-4d55d9b987" />), so give the rejected option its own space. **Slide 6** turns "it worked" into a
number. **Slide 7** proves the system ran in the real world. The same guide suggests including what went wrong in
delivery and whether other teams adopted the work, which **slides 7 and 9** cover. **Slide 10** shows you can
criticise your own work.

### The evaluation slide, in plain words

Show three things: how many questions, where they came from, and the result with its error bar. The error bar
matters because the eval set is a sample. With 200 questions at 85%, the true rate is plausibly anywhere from about
80% to 90%. With 50 questions it would be about 75% to 95%. Showing the range tells a senior audience you know the
number is an estimate.

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the numbers (for a Staff-level follow-up)</summary>

- 95% error bar: ± 1.96 × √(p × (1 − p) ÷ n). For p = 0.85: n = 200 gives ± 4.9 points; n = 50 gives ± 9.9.
- Cost per question (Claude Sonnet 5 list prices fetched 2026-10-11; token counts assumed): 1,000 uncached input ×
  $2 per million + 2,000 cached input × $0.20 per million + 400 output × $10 per million = **$0.0064**.

</details>

### Leave room for interruptions

The questions are the round. Plan 20 minutes of talk for a 30-minute slot, and rehearse with someone who
interrupts you. If you are interrupted on slide 4, answer, then skip ahead rather than rushing every remaining
slide.

### Pick the right project

Choose a project you helped define and lead over a bigger one where you owned a small piece. The Hello Interview
guide makes the same point (<Cite id="s-4d55d9b987" />).

## Small-team version

A small-company project often has more of what this round wants than a slice of a big system. You made the
decisions, ran the eval, watched the bill and handled the incidents.

- **Show the full lifecycle on one slide**: scoping, build, eval, rollout, cost, on-call.
- **Add the cost per request and the monthly bill.** For example, $0.0064 a request × 50,000 a day × 30 ≈
  **$9,600 a month** at Claude Sonnet 5 list prices fetched 2026-10-11 (the family walkthrough's example).
- **Say which components you bought and which you built**: for example, model access bought through an API, the
  eval set and retrieval built.
- **Name what coding agents did and how you verified it.** "An agent wrote the loaders; I wrote the tests that pin
  their output" is stronger than hiding it.

Say it like this: "I owned it end to end: scoping with the support lead, the build, the eval and the on-call. It
costs about $9,600 a month at fifty thousand questions a day."

## Pitfalls

- **Overrunning the talk.** No time is left for questions, which are the part being scored.
- **No slide on the rejected alternative.** The interviewer sees a diagram but not how you decide.
- **An eval number with no sample size or baseline.** It can't be judged, so it doesn't count.
- **Slides dense with text you read aloud.** The audience reads ahead and stops listening.
- **Hiding coding-agent use.** It invites a follow-up you can't answer well.

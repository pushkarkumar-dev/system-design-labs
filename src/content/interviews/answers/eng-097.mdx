## What they're really asking

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "A work trial is my first week compressed into a day. I'll spend the first hour building, running and reading
> the code, with the AI summarizing and me checking its summary. Then I'll pick something small that users would
> notice, check the choice with the team, and build it in small tested steps. I'll stop early enough to harden it
> and prepare a short presentation: what I built, why, the trade-offs, and what I'd do next. A small finished
> feature beats an ambitious unfinished one."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer, so you can say it with understanding.

A **work trial** replaces some or all of the interview loop with a day or two of real work. You get a real
codebase, access to the team and a task, or the freedom to choose one. It is very different from a coding
interview. Nobody hands you a clear problem with a known answer. Choosing *what* to build is part of the test,
and so is how you use the time.

It tests scoping and product judgment as much as code. Can you understand an unfamiliar system quickly? Can you
pick something worth doing that you can actually finish? Can you ask the team good questions without leaning on
them? And can you explain your choice at the end? An AI coding agent (a program built on a large language model
that reads your files and writes code from plain-language instructions) helps with the reading and the typing.
It does not help with the choosing.

This format was reported at Cursor. Reports describe it differently, so prepare for both versions:

- Cursor's CEO said the process ends with two days in the office, building with the team
  (<Cite id="s-e3eeb47f1f" />). A later Business Insider piece says candidates get "a frozen version of the
  company's codebase" ([BI via AOL, 2025-11-11](https://aol.com/news/cursor-courted-top-engineers-flying-070128578.html)).
- A new-grad (L3) account describes "an 8 to 9 hour remote onsite" with part of the codebase, a Slack channel to
  the team, and a presentation at the end (<Cite id="s-e378475dbf" />).
- Pay is disputed: a Jul 2026 repost of a Business Insider story describes "extensive unpaid trials"
  (<Cite id="s-453266e92b" />), and only prep sites say the trial is paid.

## Clarify first

- **Format and logistics:** one day or two, remote or on site, paid or not. Ask the recruiter.
- **Is the AI tool expected?** The first technical screens allow no AI beyond autocomplete. The new-grad account
  says AI tools were used in the trial, but no primary source confirms that for everyone.
- **Is the task assigned or yours to choose?** In the new-grad account, the candidate chose it.
- **Who can you ask, and about what?** Use the channel for questions specific to their codebase, not for things
  the docs answer.
- **How is the end presented?** A demo, a short talk, or a pull request.

Say it like this, to the recruiter before the day: "Is the trial one day or two, remote or in the office, is it
paid, and are AI tools expected?"

## Answer

### Plan the day in blocks

```
Orient (≈1 h) ── build it, run it, run the tests; AI summarizes, you check the summary
   │
   ▼
Choose (≈30 min) ── small, finishable, visible to users; write scope + out of scope; check with the team
   │
   ▼
Build (most of the day) ── small tested steps; commit often; ask only codebase-specific questions
   │
   ▼
Harden (≈1 h) ── edge cases, a test for your own first bug, clean up generated code
   │
   ▼
Present (prepare ≈30 min) ── what, why, trade-offs, next steps, what you left out
```

1. **Orient (about 1 hour).** Build it, run it, run the tests. Read the READMEs and the modules near your likely
   area. Have the agent summarize the code, and check its summary against the code by opening the files it
   names. An agent's summary of a codebase sounds confident whether it is right or not.
2. **Choose (30 minutes).** Pick something small enough to finish and demo, that users would notice. The new-grad
   candidate picked an add-on to an existing service. Write down the scope and what is out of it, and if possible
   check it with the team in the channel.
3. **Build in small, tested steps (most of the day).** Delegate the boilerplate and keep the design. Commit often.
   Ask the team only when an answer is specific to their codebase.
4. **Harden (1 hour).** Edge cases, a test that would have caught your own first bug, and a cleanup pass on
   generated code.
5. **Present (prepare 30 minutes).** Cover what you built, why you chose it, the trade-offs, what you would do
   next, and what you deliberately left out. The new-grad candidate presented scope, the reason for it and the
   expected product impact.

### One worked example: an 8-hour day

Here is one illustrative day, with a running clock. The times follow the blocks above; the feature is a made-up
example.

| Clock | Block | What happens | What to say (in the channel or at the end) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 09:00 | Orient | Build, run the tests, skim the README | — |
| 09:30 | Orient | Agent summarizes the service you'll touch; you open three files to check | "I've read the request path; is this the right area for a small add-on?" |
| 10:00 | Choose | Pick one add-on to an existing service; write scope and out-of-scope | "I'm planning X, not Y, because I can finish and demo X today." |
| 10:30 | Build | First test, first small diff, commit | — |
| 14:00 | Build | Feature works end to end on the happy path | "One question about how you handle errors in this module." |
| 15:30 | Harden | Edge cases; a test for the bug you hit at 11:00; clean up names | — |
| 16:30 | Present | Write five slides or a short doc | "Here's what I built, why, what I left out, and what I'd do next." |

The key decision is at 10:00. If you are still choosing at noon, the day is in trouble.

A finished small feature beats an unfinished ambitious one. One prep article reports that the candidates who do
well ship "something smaller but solid and defensible" (<Cite id="s-c29d429c3c" />).

## Senior vs Staff

  staff={["Picks the feature by product impact, checks the choice with the team early, leaves the codebase better (tests, notes), and names the follow-on work and its risks."]}
/>

In plain words: a Senior candidate ships something solid and explains it. A Staff candidate also chooses by
impact, brings the team in early, and leaves a plan for what comes next.

## Small-team version

Small companies use work trials too. Foxglove runs them for every role (<Cite id="s-08351ee000" />). If you hire,
the same questions apply from the other side:

- What will the candidate build in a day?
- Do they get a **sandbox**, a separate environment with no production access?
- How will you judge AI-assisted work? Judge it on scope, tests and the explanation, not on lines written.

**Budget it honestly.** PostHog's handbook pays a flat $1,000 for its one-day paid SuperDay
([PostHog hiring process](https://posthog.com/handbook/people/hiring-process), fetched 2026-10-11). Add a day of
your own engineer's time to host and review, about **$750** at an assumed loaded cost. Five finalists a quarter is
then roughly **$8,750**, cheap next to a bad hire.

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the numbers (for a Staff-level follow-up)</summary>

- One engineer-day: an assumed $194K loaded yearly cost ÷ 260 working days ≈ **$750**.
- Per finalist: $1,000 trial pay + $750 hosting ≈ $1,750.
- Five finalists a quarter: 5 × ($1,000 + $750) ≈ **$8,750**.

</details>

As a candidate, treat the day like your first week: read, pick something small and finish it.

## Pitfalls

- **Spending half the day choosing** something too ambitious. The choice should be made in the first two hours.
- **Asking the team what the README answers**, or not asking when you are really stuck. Both waste the day.
- **Presenting code you can't explain line by line.** The presentation is where AI-written code you didn't
  review shows up.
- **Assuming the trial is paid, or that AI is allowed, without asking.** Reports disagree on both.
- **Trusting the agent's summary of the codebase.** Check it against two or three real files before you build on it.

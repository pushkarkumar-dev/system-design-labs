## What they're really asking

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "Under a burst, this design turns overload into an outage, and a dead GPU loses work silently. Three changes fix
> most of it. Bound the queue by how long a request would wait, and reject early with a 'retry later'. Retry at one
> layer only, inside a budget. And lease each batch, so a dead GPU's work goes back on the queue. For each one I'd
> attach a test: replay at twice peak, inject GPU errors, kill a worker mid-batch."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer.

The interviewer hands you someone else's design for a service that runs a model on GPUs (graphics processors
used for a model's heavy math), and asks what breaks. The first few issues are the warm-up. The real question is
how deep you can go on **overload** (more traffic than the system can serve) and **failure** (a part dies).

Why is this harder than overload in a normal web service? A web request is cheap, and when traffic grows you add
servers in seconds. Here each request is expensive and the GPUs can't be added quickly, so the design must decide
who waits and who is turned away. Also, the service groups requests into a **batch** (a group the GPU processes in
one go, here 32 requests every 100 ms). That's efficient, but if a GPU dies mid-batch, every request in the
batch goes down together.

A Staff candidate at Anthropic reported a phone-screen round that "asked me to review an existing design doc and
give feedback" on a "Batch Sampling Service". After the first issues, "it becomes a typical deep dive session on
failure handling, overload protection". The candidate's advice: "Have a deep understanding of the LLM inference
stack and the failure scenarios" (<Cite id="s-eab681003c" />). (An LLM is a large language model; inference is
running it to get an answer; sampling is generating its output.)

## Clarify first

- **Who calls the service, and do they wait?** Interactive callers need a deadline; assume a 1 s timeout if the
  doc doesn't say.
- **Traffic, peak and tenant mix?** A **tenant** is one customer sharing the service. These decide how much the
  overload problem matters.
- **Latency target, and is the batch time inside it?**
- **Is a request safe to retry?** If it has side effects, retries and leases need more care.
- **Several models on the fleet?**

Missing answers in the doc are findings in their own right. Say it like this: "The doc doesn't say how long a
caller waits. That means nobody has designed the timeout, so I'll assume 1 second and note it."

## Answer

Picture the design as submitted (an invented example with the usual flaws):

```
Clients (1 s timeout)
   │
   ▼
Load balancer ──── up to 3 attempts per call
   │
   ▼
API pods ───────── also up to 3 attempts
   │
   ▼
In-memory queue ── no size limit, inside one dispatcher process
   │  every 100 ms, up to 32
   ▼
GPU workers ────── fleet sized for about 10,000 requests/s
```

**Walk the flow** once for a request that succeeds and once for a request that fails at each hop, naming each
timeout. Then replay it on a bad day. That is where the findings come from.

### Follow one burst through it

Suppose traffic suddenly doubles to 20,000 requests per second. The fleet can serve about 10,000, so roughly
10,000 extra requests a second pile into the queue. Wait time is queue length divided by how fast the queue
drains (about 10,000 a second). Illustrative numbers for the invented doc:

| Time into the burst | Queue length | How long a new request waits | What callers see |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 s | about 0 | about 100 ms | Normal |
| 0.5 s | about 5,000 | about 0.5 s | Slow but working |
| 1 s | about 10,000 | about 1 s | New requests hit the 1 s timeout |
| 2 s | about 20,000 | about 2 s | Every caller times out; GPUs compute answers nobody waits for |

Then the retries start, and each timeout comes back as more traffic. That's the outage.

### Overload

Four flaws compound in that table:

- **Unbounded queue.** "Absorbs bursts" really means "turns a burst into latency, then into timeouts". Fix: bound
  the queue by *estimated wait* (queue length ÷ drain rate), not by count. Past the budget, reject at once with
  `503` and a `Retry-After` header telling the caller when to come back. A 200 ms budget at this drain rate is
  about 2,000 queued requests.
- **Retries at several layers.** Three attempts at the load balancer times three at the API tier is up to **9**
  attempts,
  exactly when GPUs are overloaded. Fix: retry at one layer, inside a **retry budget** (a cap on retries as a share
  of all traffic). The load-balancer lab caps retries at 20% of traffic, and Google's SRE book recommends retrying
  only at the layer immediately above ([Handling Overload](https://sre.google/sre-book/handling-overload/)). With
  a 20% budget, total load stays at most 1.2× the base instead of up to 9×.
- **No admission control.** **Admission control** means deciding at the door whether to accept a request. Fix:
  per-tenant **token buckets** (a counter per tenant that refills at their agreed rate; empty means reject),
  priority classes for interactive work, and shed the lowest class first.
- **No deadlines.** Fix: carry each request's deadline with it, and drop expired work before it reaches a GPU.

Say it like this: "The queue doesn't absorb bursts; it converts them into timeouts, and then the stacked retries
multiply the load by up to nine."

### Failure

Now a normal day, but the GPU running our batch of 32 dies halfway through.

- **GPU dies mid-batch.** Without leases, the batch's 32 callers simply time out. A **lease** gives the worker the
  batch for a limited time; if it doesn't report success in time, the batch returns to the queue. Fix: lease
  batches, requeue with an attempt count, and retry once if the deadline allows.
- **Poison input.** One input that crashes the worker will crash worker after worker as the batch is retried.
  Fix: if a batch fails twice, split it in half and retry each half (**bisect**) until the bad input is alone, then
  move it to a **dead-letter queue** (a side queue for messages that keep failing).
- **Single dispatcher with an in-memory queue.** One crash loses everything queued. Fix: replicate the queue, or
  state the loss contract explicitly so callers know to retry.

Say it like this: "Today a dead GPU loses 32 requests with no trace. With leases, they go back on the queue and
run once more if their deadline allows."

### Ranked summary

Close with the outcome, not a list: "Under a burst this design turns overload into an outage. Three changes fix
most of it: a time-bounded queue, one retry layer with a budget, and leased batches." Attach a test to each:

- **Time-bounded queue:** replay traffic at 2× peak; accepted requests keep a bounded p99, and rejections rise.
- **One retry layer:** inject GPU errors; attempts per request stay within the budget.
- **Leases:** kill a worker mid-batch in staging; no accepted request is lost.

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the numbers (for a Staff-level follow-up)</summary>

- **Burst growth:** arrivals 20,000/s − service about 10,000/s = about 10,000 extra per second. After t seconds
  the queue holds about 10,000 × t requests, and a new arrival waits about (10,000 × t) ÷ 10,000 = t seconds.
- **Queue bound from a time budget:** 0.2 s × 10,000/s = about **2,000** requests.
- **Retry amplification:** 3 attempts at the load balancer × 3 at the API tier = **9** attempts. With one layer
  and a 20% retry budget, load is at most 1 + 0.2 = **1.2×**.

</details>

## Senior vs Staff

<LevelContrast
  title="Batch Sampling Service review"
  senior={["Finds the unbounded queue, compound retries and missing leases, and proposes concrete fixes."]}
  staff={["Ranks findings by incident risk, ties each fix to a verification test, and asks what the author knows that the doc doesn't say."]}
/>

In plain terms: a Senior reviewer finds the right problems. A Staff reviewer also decides which ones matter most,
proves each fix with a test, and treats the author as someone who may know a constraint the doc left out.

## Small-team version

At a small company the documents differ but the review is the same. A teammate's plan to run a model on your own
GPUs, a vendor's architecture or an agent pipeline gets the same lenses: timeouts, retries, queue bounds, what
happens when a part dies.

Add one finding small teams often miss: the provider's **spend cap**, a monthly limit on what your account can
spend. Anthropic's docs say that at the monthly cap the API returns 429 (too many requests) without a
`retry-after` header, so an SDK's automatic retries keep failing until the month resets. This guide's small-team
research lists caps of $500 a month on the Start tier and $1,000 on Build
([rate-limits docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/api/rate-limits), fetched 2026-10-11).

Say it like this: "What happens on the day we hit the provider's spend cap? Our retries won't help, so we need
an alert well before it and a clear error for users."

## Pitfalls

- **Redesigning from scratch instead of reviewing.** The round asks you to improve the author's design, not
  replace it.
- **A flat list of twenty findings with no ranking.** It hides your judgment; lead with three.
- **Missing that retries multiply across layers.** Three times three is nine attempts, at the worst moment.
- **Pushing back on the interviewer rather than the design.** Ask what they know before asserting they're wrong.

## What they're really asking

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "Here's what we had. A test set of about 200 cases from real traffic and bug reports. Code checks for
> format and citations, and one AI grader for faithfulness that we compared with 100 labels from our domain
> expert. Every prompt change ran against the current version on the same cases. One change it caught, one
> it justified, and one thing we never measured. If the honest answer is that it was mostly vibes, I say so,
> and say what I'd build first."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer.

Asked inside a project deep dive, this is a credibility check: did you measure the thing you built, or did
you tune it by feel? The answer has to be specific to your project and honest about gaps.

### What makes this different from "did you test it?"

For a normal project, "did you test it?" has a simple answer: unit tests, integration tests, coverage.

For a project built on a large language model (**LLM**), tests alone don't settle it:

1. **The output varies and is open-ended**, so "it passed" needs a grader that can judge meaning.
2. **The grader itself can be wrong.** If an AI graded your AI, the interviewer will ask who checked the
   grader.
3. **Every score is an estimate.** "85%" on a small set is a wide range, and the interviewer knows it.

So "framework" means evidence you can name: how many test cases, where they came from, who or what graded
them, how well the grader agreed with people, and one decision the numbers changed.

The report is low confidence: a candidate account of OpenAI interviews on Exponent's Medium, seen only
second-hand, in which the interviewer asked whether there was an actual eval framework or it was
"vibes-based" (<Cite id="s-9ffe6685c2" />).

### Words you need first

- **Eval** (evaluation): a test that scores the model's output. An **eval set** is the fixed list of test
  cases (**items**).
- **Trace**: the full log of one request: input, what was looked up, every model call, and the output.
- **LLM judge**: a second model call that grades an output against one written rule and returns pass or fail.
- **Held-out**: labels kept aside and never used to tune the judge, so its agreement with people is measured
  fairly.

## Clarify first

You can't clarify much here; it's your project. Before the interview, check:

- how many eval items you had and where they came from;
- which grader decided each failure mode, and whether any judge was checked against people;
- one concrete change the eval caught or justified;
- what you never measured.

## Answer

### Answer with evidence, in about two minutes

Here is an illustrative project, not a real one. Use its shape with your own facts.

| Part | What to say | Why it convinces |
|---|---|---|
| **1. The set** | "About 200 items: 120 sampled from production traces, 80 from bug reports. Each has the input, frozen context and a pass/fail check per failure mode." | Real sources, a count, and recorded context so reruns are comparable. |
| **2. The graders** | "Code checks for citations and output format; one LLM judge for faithfulness, compared with 100 labels from our domain expert. It caught most labelled failures and rarely flagged good answers; here are its rates on the held-out part." | Shows the grader was itself tested. |
| **3. The comparison** | "Every prompt change ran on the same items against the current version. A reranker improved the pass rate by a few points but its paired interval crossed zero on one dataset, so we shipped it only where it clearly helped." | A decision the numbers changed. |
| **4. Production** | "We tracked escalations and thumbs-down, and read 30 traces a week; two new failure modes came from that." | The loop from production back to tests. |
| **5. The gap** | "We never ran an A/B test; traffic was too low to see a 3-point change in under two weeks." | Honesty about limits earns trust. |

A few terms in that table. **Frozen context** means the documents and tool responses were recorded, so a
rerun sees exactly the same inputs. A **reranker** is a second model that re-sorts search results before the
answer is written. A **paired interval** is the error bar on the difference between two versions run on the
same items; "crossed zero" means the data couldn't rule out no improvement.

For the judge's "rates": the **true-positive rate** is the share of answers the expert marked as failures that
the judge also failed. The **true-negative rate** is the share the expert marked as good that the judge also
passed. Have both numbers ready.

Say: "I'll give you the counts, the graders, how the grader agreed with people, and one decision the eval
changed."

### If it was mostly vibes

Say so, and say what you would build first:

1. 50 items from real failures, with code checks;
2. then one judge, checked against human labels;
3. then a paired comparison in CI on every prompt change.

Saying how small a suite is matters too. At an 85% pass rate on 50 items, the 95% error bar is **about ±9.9
points**, so the true rate is somewhere between about 75% and 95%.

Say: "Our 85% was on 50 items, so it's really somewhere between 75 and 95. That was enough to find failures,
not to compare close versions."

## Small-team version

Expect a small-company interviewer to probe this, since there was no eval team to credit (my reading, not a
reported pattern).

- **Prepare** a one-page note on your project's eval: counts, graders, one caught regression, cost.
- **If you're building it now,** a week of work (traces, 50 items, code checks in CI) is enough to have a
  real answer.
- **The tokens are cheap.** Tokens are the chunks of text providers bill for, roughly ¾ of a word each. 50
  items × 3 trials on a single-call feature at about 4,400 tokens a run is **well under $5** at Claude Sonnet
  5 list prices ($2 / $10 per million, fetched 2026-10-11).

## Pitfalls

- **"We used LLM-as-judge" with no check against people.** An unchecked grader is an untested test.
- **Reporting one number with no item count.** Without the count, nobody can tell how precise it is.
- **Inventing rigor you didn't have.** The follow-ups expose it quickly.

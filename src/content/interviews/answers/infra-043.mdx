## What they're really asking

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "Route by model first, then by free memory; batch only within one model; and for allocation, memory isn't the
> conflict, coupling is. The small model fits beside the big one, but every big-model step then waits for the
> shared GPU, and that GPU's lost memory roughly halves the big model's batch. So I'd either let the node take
> turns between the models with a guaranteed share for the big one, shrink the big model, or ask for more
> hardware. If one GPU of the eight fails, the whole big replica is down; I'd restart it on four healthy GPUs, never
> seven."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer.

The interviewer wants to see whether you can keep the books straight when one model needs all eight GPUs of a
machine and another needs one.

In a normal cluster, a job that needs eight CPUs can usually take any eight. Here it can't. A large model is split
across eight GPUs that work together on every step, so those eight GPUs behave like one big GPU. If any one of them
is busy, or full, or broken, the whole group is held back. That is the idea the whole question turns on.

Anthropic's tech screen on PracHub (posted 2026-09-08) has three parts (routing, batch formation, GPU allocation)
and two follow-ups: a GPU in the eight-GPU group fails, and small-model traffic delays the eight-GPU work
indefinitely (<Cite id="s-62f6a22994" />). The follow-ups decide it.

### Words you need first

- **Weights.** The model's learned numbers. At 2 bytes each, a 70-billion-parameter model (70B) is about 140 GB and
  an 8B model about 16 GB. Each GPU here has 80 GB of memory.
- **Tensor parallelism (TP).** Splitting one model across several GPUs so they compute each step together.
  "TP = 8" means eight slices on eight GPUs. They exchange data on every layer of every step.
- **KV cache.** While the model writes a reply one token (about ¾ of a word) at a time, the GPUs keep a saved
  summary of every token so far. This memory sets how many requests (**sequences**) fit in a batch at once.

## Clarify first

- **Model sizes and context.** "I'll assume a 70B at TP = 8 and an 8B, on 80 GB GPUs, with sequences of up to 4,096
  tokens."
- **Do both models need low latency at the same time, or can one wait?** This decides whether taking turns is
  allowed.
- **Traffic mix and latency targets per model.**
- **May the two models share a GPU?**

## Answer

### The picture

```
Router ── by model and version, then by free KV memory
   │                         │
   ▼                         ▼
70B queue                 8B queue        (batches never mix models)
   │                         │
   ▼                         ▼
┌──── one node, 8 GPUs ─────────────────────────┐
│ GPU0 GPU1 GPU2 GPU3 GPU4 GPU5 GPU6 GPU7        │  70B: one slice on each GPU (TP = 8)
│  +8B?                                          │  8B: where does it go?
└────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

**Routing.** Route by model and version first. Then, among replicas that can serve it, pick by estimated work and
free KV memory, not by connection counts. Each model has admission control with bounded queues and a clear overload
answer: a queue position with an estimated wait, or a 503.

**Batching.** Requests share a batch only within one model and version. Cap each batch by KV memory and by how long
its oldest request has waited. Generation needs **continuous batching**: finished requests leave and waiting ones
join between steps, rather than fixed batches that wait for the slowest member.

### Allocation: memory isn't the conflict, coupling is

Follow the numbers on our one node.

| Step | What you find | What to say in the interview |
|---|---|---|
| **Weights per GPU** | The 70B takes 140 ÷ 8 = **17.5 GB** on each GPU. The 8B (16 GB) fits beside it. | "Memory fits. That's not the problem." |
| **Step-time coupling** | Every 70B step finishes only when its slowest slice finishes. Put the 8B on GPU 0, and every 70B step waits whenever GPU 0 runs 8B work. | "The 8B adds jitter to every 70B token." |
| **Memory coupling** | Each 70B sequence spreads its KV cache evenly over all eight GPUs, so the fullest GPU sets capacity. Alone, the replica holds **about 324 sequences**. With the 8B and an assumed 10 GB of its KV on GPU 0, it holds **about 169**. | "One small model on one GPU roughly halves the big model's batch." |

So the honest options are four:

1. **Take turns.** The node runs one model at a time, with a switch cost each way. With weights waiting in the
   server's ordinary memory (host DRAM), a switch takes about 0.9 s, and 30-second turns keep **97%** of the time
   useful.
2. **Share** a GPU, at the cost above.
3. **Shrink the big model**, for example to 8-bit weights on four GPUs (TP = 4), freeing four GPUs for small
   replicas. This needs a quality check, and the split must divide the model's 64 query heads and 8 key-value heads
   evenly.
4. **Add hardware**, if both models need low latency at once.

### Follow-up 1: one GPU in the group fails

The whole 70B replica is down, because every token needs every slice.

1. Stop routing to it. Retry requests that haven't started streaming.
2. For replies already streaming, send an error or **resume**: another replica reads the prompt plus the tokens
   already sent and carries on.
3. Mark the GPU bad.
4. Restore on another node, or relaunch at **TP = 4** on four of the seven healthy GPUs. That holds **about 110
   sequences**, a third of the full node. **TP = 7 isn't valid**, because 7 divides neither 64 nor 8.

### Follow-up 2: the small model starves the big one

If the node takes turns, a rule of "switch when the node is idle" never fires, because small-model traffic never
stops. Instead: once the big model's oldest request passes a wait bound, admit no new small work, let in-flight
small work finish up to a deadline, and switch. Report the big model's maximum wait as a metric.

Say: "Idle-triggered switching starves the big model under continuous traffic. I give it a bound, and I measure
its maximum wait."

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the numbers (for a Staff-level follow-up)</summary>

- **KV per token per GPU at TP = 8:** 2 × 80 layers × 8 KV heads × 128 × 2 bytes = 327,680 B per token, split
  over 8 GPUs = **40,960 B per token per GPU**.
- **Alone:** 80 − 17.5 − 8 (assumed overhead) = 54.5 GB per GPU → about 324 sequences of 4,096.
- **With the 8B on GPU 0:** 54.5 − 16 − 10 = 28.5 GB. 28.5 × 10⁹ ÷ 40,960 ≈ 696,000 tokens ≈ **169 sequences**.
- **Taking turns:** useful share = T ÷ (T + s). Weights in DRAM, s ≈ 0.9 s (140 GB at 8 × 20 GB/s, assumed): a
  30 s turn keeps 30 ÷ 30.9 ≈ **97%**.
- **TP = 4 after a failure:** 320 − 140 − 32 = 148 GB → 148 × 10⁹ ÷ 327,680 ≈ 451,000 tokens ≈ **110 sequences**.

</details>

The Staff move is saying which numbers would prove the choice right, and being willing to say "this needs another
node" instead of engineering around a hardware shortage.

## Small-team version

At a small company, don't put two models on one node. Run the large model on its own node or, more likely, call it
through an API. Put small models on separate GPUs or a per-token (serverless) endpoint. The co-location engineering
only pays when GPUs are scarce and both models are yours to serve.

**For scale:** a node of 8 H100s rented at about $3.99 per GPU-hour (Lambda's 8-GPU list price,
fetched 2026-10-11) costs about **$23,300 a month** (8 × 3.99 × 730). Check that the large model's traffic really
fills it before you rent one.

## Pitfalls

- **"The 8B fits in memory, so share the GPU."** It fits, but it slows every big-model step and halves its batch.
- **Proposing TP = 7 after a failure.** The model's heads don't divide by 7; use 4.
- **An idle-triggered switch.** Under continuous small traffic it never fires, and the big model starves.
- **Balancing on request counts.** Requests differ hugely in memory; balance on free KV memory.

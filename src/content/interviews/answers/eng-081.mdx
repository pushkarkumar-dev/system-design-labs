## What they're really asking

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "'AI on support tickets' is really three different projects: routing tickets, drafting replies for agents, and
> answering customers directly. I'd rank them by who sees a mistake, and start with routing or drafts, where a
> person catches errors. Then I'd agree a number for success, such as time saved per ticket, test it on 50 to 200
> real tickets graded by the support lead, and run a four-week pilot on one queue before deciding to widen."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer.

The interviewer plays an enterprise customer who says: "We want to use AI on our support tickets." They want to see
whether you can turn that into a scoped, measurable first project.

### Why this is different from a normal feature request

A normal feature has one shape. You build it, test it, and it either works or it doesn't. This ask hides three
projects with very different risks. A **large language model** (LLM: a model like ChatGPT or Claude, rented
through a paid API, that reads text and writes text) sometimes writes fluent, confident answers that are wrong. So
the key design question is: who sees a wrong answer before it does damage?

The second difference is how you know it works. You can't unit-test the quality of a written reply. You need real
tickets, a person who knows what a good reply is, and a number.

The wording comes from an OpenAI forward deployed engineer (FDE) prep guide that labels it "Representative" and
cites no candidates: "How would you scope an AI solution for an enterprise customer who says they want to use AI on
their support tickets?" (<Cite id="s-26f0cae3fe" />). It is low confidence as a report, but it is a typical example
of the vague asks this family is about.

## Clarify first

Ask, and carry an assumption if they won't answer. The numbers in the assumptions are used through the rest of the
page.

- **Who hurts today?** Customers waiting, agents overloaded, or managers without insight? Assume agent load.
- **Volume and effort?** Tickets per month, channels, languages, and average **handle time** (how long an agent
  spends per ticket)? Assume 3,000 tickets a month at 6 minutes each.
- **Knowledge?** Is there a help centre or knowledge base, and is it current? Assume partly.
- **Risk?** What must the AI never do? Assume no promises about refunds or policy without a human.
- **Success?** What would make this a win in 30 days? Assume handle time and how often agents accept the drafts.
- **Owner?** Who will grade examples? Assume the support lead.

Say it like this: "I'll assume 3,000 tickets a month at about 6 minutes each, and that agent time is the pain. Tell
me if that's off."

## Answer

### Split the ask into three slices and rank them by risk

A **slice** is one small piece of the feature that works end to end. Rank the three by who sees a mistake:

| Slice | What the model does | Who sees a mistake | What to measure | What to say in the interview |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Triage and routing | Reads each ticket, tags it, sends it to the right queue | Nobody outside; it costs a re-route | Share routed correctly | "Lowest risk. Customers never see the model." |
| 2. Agent assist | Drafts a reply with links to the help articles it used; an agent edits and sends | The agent, who fixes it | Handle time and how much agents edit drafts | "The agents are a free safety net, and their edits show where drafts go wrong." |
| 3. Deflection | A bot answers customers directly | The customer | Share resolved without an agent, and complaints | "Highest value, highest risk. It comes later." |

Deflection's risk is public. A Canadian tribunal held Air Canada responsible for its chatbot's wrong advice
([Law360, 2024-02-14](https://www.law360.ca/ca/articles/1804075)).

Recommend 1 or 2 first. Gate deflection on measured **grounding**: whether answers stick to what the help articles
actually say.

### Put numbers on it

Every input here is an assumption you say out loud.

- Agent time today costs about **$10,500 a month**.
- If drafts cut handle time by 25%, which the pilot must measure, that saves about **$2,625 a month**.
- The model costs about **$0.011 per ticket**, or **$33 a month**, on Claude Sonnet 5 at list price.

The model bill is noise. The case depends on the measured saving and on quality. Say it like this: "The model costs
about $33 a month. Whether this is worth doing depends entirely on how much time the drafts save."

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the numbers (for a Staff-level follow-up)</summary>

A **token** is the unit a model reads and writes, roughly ¾ of a word. Providers charge per million tokens, more for
tokens the model writes (output) than for tokens you send (input).

- Agent time: 3,000 tickets × 6 minutes (0.1 h) × $35 an hour (assumed loaded cost) = $10,500 a month.
- Saving at an assumed 25% cut: $10,500 × 0.25 = $2,625 a month.
- Model cost per ticket: about 4,000 input and 300 output tokens on Claude Sonnet 5 ($2 / $10 per million, list price
  fetched 2026-10-11): 4,000 × $2 ÷ 10⁶ = $0.008, plus 300 × $10 ÷ 10⁶ = $0.003, so $0.011. × 3,000 = $33 a month.
- Build cost for comparison: an assumed three weeks of one engineer is about $11,200 (the family walkthrough derives it), a
  payback of about four months at 25%, or about 11 months at 10%.

</details>

### Define success as an eval before building

An **eval** (short for evaluation) is a test set for the model feature: real tickets, each with the support lead's
judgment of a good reply, and a pass rate. It does the job a test suite does for normal code.

- **Offline eval:** 50–200 real tickets, graded by the support lead, with pass criteria agreed in advance. Fifty
  tickets tell you whether the idea works at all. Around 200 narrow the uncertainty to about ±5 points.
- **Online signals,** from real use: acceptance of drafts, re-open rate, escalations, and **CSAT** (customer
  satisfaction score) on AI-assisted tickets.

### The plan

1. **Week 1:** interviews and labelling.
2. **Weeks 2–3:** **shadow mode** on one queue. The feature runs on real tickets, but nothing is sent automatically;
   people only look at its output.
3. **Week 4:** a go/no-go meeting on the agreed number.
4. **Then:** widen one queue at a time.

Mention the build-or-buy check: the ticketing tool's own AI add-on, priced into the same arithmetic as your build.

Say it like this to close: "Phase one is drafts on one queue for four weeks. If handle time falls by the amount we
agreed and re-opens don't rise, we add the next queue."

/>

In plain words: a Senior answer picks the safe slice and a way to measure it. A Staff answer also shows how the
business case changes if the saving is smaller, compares building with buying, and says what result ends the
project.

## Small-team version

At a small company this is the most likely first AI project, and the head of support is the customer.

- **Do the arithmetic with your real ticket counts.** If you handle 500 tickets a month, the saving is a sixth of the
  numbers above, and the build may not pay back.
- **Label 50 tickets together** with the head of support. They become your eval.
- **Price the add-on first.** Consider the AI add-on of the support tool you already pay for before building.
- **Own the eval either way.** A vendor's demo is not your data.

## Pitfalls

- **Promising deflection first.** It is the riskiest slice, with public examples of what goes wrong.
- **No labelled tickets, or no owner for them.** Without them there is no go/no-go, only opinions.
- **Arguing about models.** The business case depends on handle time; the model bill is about 1% of the saving.

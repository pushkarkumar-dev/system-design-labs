## What they're really asking

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "Before drawing anything, I'd ask what the documents are and what people ask about them. If it's one document
> that fits in the model's input, I'd send the whole thing with prompt caching and skip search. Otherwise I'd
> split the documents into passages along their headings, search them by keywords and by meaning, give the best
> few to the model with ids it must cite, check those citations in code, and say 'not found' when the evidence is
> weak. Every choice gets measured on 50 questions a domain expert has answered."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer.

The interviewer wants to know whether you can build an assistant that answers from **someone else's** documents
and can prove it is right.

You already know search: a query returns ten links and the user picks one. Here the user never sees the list. A
large language model (**LLM**, such as Claude or GPT) reads the passages you hand it and writes one answer. That
changes three things:

1. **A search miss turns into a confident wrong answer.** The model writes something fluent from whatever it was
   given, and the user can't tell.
2. **Every passage you send costs money.** LLMs are billed per **token** (a piece of a word, about ¾ of a word),
   and the input has a size limit, the **context window**.
3. **You can't judge quality by looking.** Right and wrong answers read the same, so you need questions with
   known answers and a number.

In a forward-deployed engineer (FDE) loop, where you build on site with a customer, "a customer's private data"
is the whole point. Find out what the documents and questions are before you draw boxes. In a first-hand account
of OpenAI's FDE loop, the interviewer stopped an early architecture dive with "what questions would you ask the
customer before designing anything?" (<Cite id="s-812dbc9385" />). The prompt itself is listed in a
candidate-submitted bank for that loop and also tagged Google; only the title is known (<Cite id="s-ee91b6c25d" />).

## Clarify first

Say an assumption out loud for each question the interviewer won't answer:

- **What are the documents?** Formats, count, growth, and whether PDFs are scanned images or made by software.
  "Let's assume 50,000 mixed files averaging 4,000 tokens."
- **What do users ask?** Single facts, procedures, or questions about everything at once? "Mostly facts and
  procedures."
- **Who may see what?** "One permission level for now; per-user permissions are a separate design."
- **Can the data leave the customer's environment or region?** "Assume a managed API is allowed."
- **What counts as success, and who can grade answers?** "A support lead can write down 50 questions with the
  right answer and where it lives."

## Answer

### Words you need first

- **RAG** (retrieval-augmented generation): search your documents first, then put only the best passages in the
  LLM's prompt.
- **Chunk**: one passage cut from a document, a paragraph to a section long. You index, retrieve and cite chunks.
- **Embedding**: a list of numbers (a vector) computed from text by a small model, so that texts with similar
  meaning get similar vectors. Searching by embeddings finds paraphrases.
- **Eval set**: the 50–200 questions with known answers and source locations. Every knob is measured on it.

### The plan, in the order to say it

```
Customer documents
   │  parse: keep headings, tables, page numbers
   ▼
Chunker ── split on headings, heading path on every chunk, hash each chunk
   │
   ▼
Keyword index (BM25) + vector index ── in Postgres at this size
   │
   ▼  (per question)
Rewrite follow-up → search both indexes → merge → (rerank) → pack k chunks
   │
   ▼
LLM answers with chunk ids ── code checks every cited id
   │
   ▼
Answer with sources, or "not found in the documents"
```

1. **Check whether retrieval is needed.** If the corpus is one document under about 200,000 tokens, put it in the
   prompt with **prompt caching** (the provider keeps the processed start of your prompt for a few minutes, so
   repeat requests pay about a tenth of the input price). A 200-page PDF is about 80,000 tokens. On a warm cache
   that costs about **$0.020 per question**, against **$0.012 for RAG**, on Claude Sonnet 5 list prices (fetched
   2026-10-11). Larger corpora need retrieval.
2. **Ingestion.** Parse with structure (headings, tables, pages). Chunk on that structure, with the heading path
   at the top of each chunk so it makes sense alone. Hash each chunk, so an edit re-embeds only what changed.
   Write with idempotent upserts.
3. **Query.** Rewrite follow-up questions into standalone ones. Run keyword search (**BM25**, the standard
   keyword-ranking formula) and embedding search side by side. Merge the two ranked lists with **reciprocal rank
   fusion** (RRF), which scores each chunk by its positions in both lists. Add a **reranker** (a slower model that
   rereads the top 20–50 together with the question) only if the eval shows the answer sitting at ranks 4–20.
   Pack **k** chunks, the number you send, into a token budget.
4. **Answer.** Cite chunk ids, verify them in code, and refuse when the evidence is missing.
5. **Measure.** On the eval set: **recall@k** (the share of questions where the answer's chunk is among the k you
   send) with a confidence interval, citation validity on every answer, and correct refusals on questions the
   documents can't answer.

### Follow one question through it

An employee asks a company assistant *"What is the notice period?"* and then *"What about for contractors?"*.

| Step | What happens | What to say in the interview |
|---|---|---|
| Rewrite | "What about for contractors?" becomes "notice period for contractors". | "Follow-ups are made standalone before search." |
| Search | Keywords rank the general notice section 1st; the contractor section is 3rd in both lists. | "Each search misses things the other catches." |
| Merge (RRF) | Being 3rd in both beats being 1st in one and 10th in the other. | "Agreement wins." |
| Pack | 8 chunks plus instructions, about 4,000 tokens in. | "k comes from a token budget, capped in code." |
| Answer | "Contractors get 14 days' written notice [c-412]." (an invented handbook) | "About 1.2 cents on Sonnet 5." |
| Check | Code confirms `c-412` was in the context. | "No valid citation, no answer." |

### Size and cost

The assumed 50,000 documents become **600,000 chunks**. Their vectors take about **3.7 GB**, and embedding them
all costs about **$4.80** once. That fits in Postgres with **pgvector** (an extension that adds vector columns
and indexes), so you don't need a separate vector database. The real cost is generation: about **$0.012 per
question** for 4,000 input and 400 output tokens on Sonnet 5.

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the numbers (for a Staff-level follow-up)</summary>

- Chunks = docs × ceil((T − o) ÷ (c − o)), with T = 4,000 tokens per document, c = 400 chunk size, o = 50 overlap:
  50,000 × ceil(3,950 ÷ 350) = 50,000 × 12 = **600,000 chunks**.
- Vectors at 1,536 dimensions, 4 bytes each: 600,000 × 1,536 × 4 B = **3.69 GB** float32.
- Embedding: 600,000 × 400 tokens = 240M tokens × $0.02 per 1M (text-embedding-3-small, fetched 2026-10-11) =
  **$4.80**.
- Sonnet 5 per question ($2 in, $10 out per 1M): 4,000 × $2 ÷ 1M + 400 × $10 ÷ 1M = $0.008 + $0.004 = **$0.012**.
- Whole 80,000-token PDF on a warm cache ($0.20 per 1M cached): 80,000 × $0.20 ÷ 1M + $0.004 = **$0.020**.

</details>

The failure to name is the **stale index**: a document changed, but the old chunks still answer. Re-index by
content hash, reconcile deletions on a schedule, and track how far behind each source is. Say it like this: "The
worst bug in RAG isn't a crash. It's a confident answer from last quarter's policy."

## Small-team version

At a 50–500 person company this is usually the first AI feature you ship. Build the pipeline in plain code on
Postgres full-text search plus pgvector. Buy the embeddings and the LLM as managed APIs. A plan of about two weeks
for one engineer is an assumption: week 1 is parsing and a baseline with 50 labelled questions; week 2 is chunks
split on headings, citation checks and an eval that runs in CI.

**What it costs**, at an assumed 5,000 questions a day: about **$1,800 a month** on Claude Sonnet 5, or about
**$270** on gpt-5-mini ($0.0018 per question), plus about **$5 once** to embed 600,000 chunks (prices fetched
2026-10-11). One line: 5,000 × $0.012 × 30 ≈ $1,800.

**Skip** a vector database and a reranker until the numbers ask for them. **The answer changes** when the
customer's data may not leave its region or network (self-host the embedder and model, or use a regional
endpoint), when the corpus passes a few million chunks, or when documents carry per-user permissions.

**What you own**: the eval set, the refusal behaviour, a monthly spend cap, and reading 20 traces a week.

## Pitfalls

- **Drawing before asking.** The documents and the questions decide every knob, and the OpenAI interviewer asked
  for them first.
- **Fixed 512-token chunks and an untested k.** Both are defaults, not decisions; tie them to the questions and
  measure them.
- **"We'll evaluate with an LLM."** With no human labels and no interval, you are measuring the grader, not the
  system.
- **Never asking where the data may go.** A residency rule changes which models you can use at all.

## What they're really asking

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "GPUs inside one machine talk about nine times faster than GPUs in different machines, and between machines,
> crossing switches costs more. So I treat placement as packing at two levels. An 8-GPU pod needs one whole node.
> A 64-GPU job needs eight whole nodes under one leaf switch. At both levels I use best-fit: the tightest node that
> fits, the smallest leaf that fits. Each job says whether one leaf is required or only preferred, with a wait
> limit, and inside each node I hand out GPUs together with the network card next to them."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer.

The interviewer wants to know whether you understand why network position matters for GPU jobs, and whether you
can turn that into a placement rule you can defend with numbers.

### What makes this different from placing web services

When you place web services, network position barely matters. Every server can reach every other server fast
enough, and a request touches a handful of them for a few milliseconds.

A big training job is different. It runs the same step thousands of times on many GPUs, and at the end of every
step all the GPUs exchange gigabytes of data. That exchange happens in lockstep: the step isn't done until the
slowest link finishes. So the slowest link the job crosses sets its speed for the whole run, and placement decides
which links it crosses.

A prep site says recent CoreWeave candidates were asked to design "a multi-tenant GPU cluster scheduler aware of
InfiniBand topology", and that interviewers are "less interested in textbook answers"; it names no candidate
(<Cite id="s-1c022c73a6" />). Expect to defend numbers, not vocabulary.

### Words you need first

- **Node**: one server with 8 GPUs (assume NVIDIA H100s).
- **NVLink**: NVIDIA's direct GPU-to-GPU link inside a node. It is the fastest path a GPU has.
- **InfiniBand**: the low-latency network between nodes. Each node reaches it through network cards (**NICs**).
- **Leaf and spine switches**: the network is a tree. A **leaf** switch connects a group of nodes; **spine**
  switches connect the leaves. Traffic between two nodes on the same leaf goes through one switch; traffic between
  leaves goes up to a spine and back down.
- **Oversubscribed uplink**: the links from a leaf up to the spine carry less than the nodes below it can send. So
  traffic that crosses leaves gets less bandwidth.
- **All-reduce**: the step where every GPU sends its piece of an array and ends up with the average of everyone's.
  Training jobs do this with their **gradients** (the array of adjustments each GPU computed) on every step.
- **Tensor parallelism**: splitting one model's layers across several GPUs. It talks constantly, so it needs the
  fastest link.

## Clarify first

- **Fabric shape.** "How many nodes per leaf switch, how many leaves per spine, and how oversubscribed are the
  uplinks?"
- **Job mix.** "Multi-node training, single-node inference, or both?"
- **Tenancy.** "May two tenants share a leaf? A node?"
- **Waiting.** "Is it acceptable to wait for a perfect placement, and for how long?"

## Answer

### Why topology matters, in numbers

Inside an H100 node, NVLink gives **450 GB/s each way** per GPU
([Hopper blog](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/nvidia-hopper-architecture-in-depth/)). Across nodes the network
card is the limit; assume **50 GB/s per GPU**. That is a 9× gap.

Now one training job. It trains an 8-billion-parameter model on **64 GPUs**, and its gradients are **16 GB** (2
bytes per number, a format called bf16). In a ring all-reduce, each GPU sends about **31.5 GB per step**:

- under one leaf, at 50 GB/s: about **0.63 s per step**;
- through an oversubscribed uplink at half that bandwidth: about **1.26 s per step**.

That extra 0.63 s is paid on every step, for the whole run.

NVLink: 18 links at 25 GB/s each way = 450 GB/s each way per GPU; NVIDIA's "900 GB/s" is both directions together.

Say it like this: "Tensor parallelism stays inside a node, and a multi-node job wants one leaf. Crossing an
oversubscribed uplink can double the time of every gradient exchange."

### Placement as packing at two levels

- **Level 1, GPUs inside a node.** An 8-GPU pod needs a whole node. Keep free GPUs per node, and pick the node with
  the fewest free GPUs that fits (best-fit).
- **Level 2, nodes inside a leaf.** A 64-GPU job needs eight whole nodes under one leaf. Keep a count of free whole
  nodes per leaf, and pick the leaf with the fewest free nodes that fits.

Measure fragmentation per leaf the way you would per node: a cluster with 40 free nodes spread five per leaf has no
room for an 8-node job.

### Follow one job

The leaf names and counts below are illustrative.

| Step | What happens | What to say in the interview |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Submit | A tenant submits a 64-GPU training job: 8 whole nodes. | "This is a gang: all eight nodes start together or none do." |
| 2. Candidates | Leaf L3 has 12 free whole nodes. Leaf L7 has 9. Leaf L9 has 5. | "L9 can't hold it. L3 and L7 can." |
| 3. Best-fit | Pick **L7**, the smallest leaf that fits. | "L3's 12 free nodes stay together for a bigger job." |
| 4. Inside each node | Hand out all 8 GPUs with the network card paired to each. | "A GPU far from its network card is slow even on a good leaf." |
| 5. Nothing fits | If no leaf had 8 free nodes and the job says **preferred**, wait up to its bound, then place across two leaves. If it says **required**, keep waiting. | "A job that waits an hour for one leaf may finish sooner on two." |

### Required versus preferred

Let each job choose. Kueue, a Kubernetes job-queueing add-on, does this in its Topology Aware Scheduling with
"required" and "preferred" annotations; a preferred placement that doesn't fit falls back to the next level up
([Kueue TAS](https://kueue.sigs.k8s.io/docs/concepts/topology_aware_scheduling/), beta since v0.14).

Add a wait bound. Topology as a hard rule with no bound means a big job can wait forever while the cluster has
plenty of free nodes in the wrong places.

### Inside the node: pair GPUs with their network cards

**GPUDirect RDMA** lets a network card read GPU memory directly, without a copy through the CPU. It needs the GPU
and the card under the same **PCIe root complex**, the part of the server's internal bus that a group of devices
hangs off ([GPUDirect RDMA docs](https://docs.nvidia.com/cuda/gpudirect-rdma/index.html)). So "8 free GPUs and 8
free NICs" is not enough. Allocate each GPU with its paired card.

### Multi-tenancy

Give each tenant quotas in GPUs **and** in leaves. Keep small single-GPU work off the nodes in leaves that large jobs
need, or let it borrow them only preemptibly (it can be evicted when a big job arrives).

## Small-team version

You won't see an InfiniBand fabric unless you rent a multi-node training cluster. If you do, ask the provider how
jobs are placed and use its scheduler. For single-node inference, topology means only one rule: keep tensor
parallelism inside one node.

The money is why placement matters when you do rent one. 64 reserved H100s at $3.19 an hour (Together, fetched
2026-10-11) cost about **$149,000 a month**. A placement that makes each training step 0.63 s slower, as in the
half-bandwidth example above, wastes a share of that bill equal to the share of step time it adds.

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the numbers (for a Staff-level follow-up)</summary>

64 × $3.19 × 730 hours ≈ $149,000 a month.

</details>

Default: the provider's scheduler, with a required-topology flag for multi-node jobs.

Say it like this: "If we rent a training cluster, I'd ask how it places multi-node jobs and set them to require
one network domain."

## Pitfalls

- **Placing a multi-node job on any free nodes.** The slowest link it crosses slows every step.
- **Topology as a hard rule with no wait bound.** Big jobs can starve while free nodes sit in the wrong leaves.
- **Ignoring GPU-to-NIC pairing inside a node.** The direct GPU-to-network path needs both on the same root complex.

## What they're really asking

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "Source-code depth means I can point to where each technique communicates and what it saves. Megatron's tensor
> parallelism hides its traffic in two small functions, f and g, at the start and end of each block: two all-reduces
> forward and two backward per layer. Sequence parallelism swaps each all-reduce for a reduce-scatter plus an
> all-gather, which moves the same bytes and saves activation memory. Pipeline parallelism is a schedule of
> point-to-point sends, and interleaving shrinks its idle time. ZeRO splits the 16 bytes per parameter of training
> state: the first two stages add no traffic, the third adds 1.5×."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer.

This isn't a recorded interview question. It is advice about how deep training-infrastructure interviews go. Treat it
as a hint about depth, not a format. "Source-code depth" most plausibly means three things: you know where the
communication calls sit, what each one moves, and which memory each technique removes.

### What makes this different from knowing the names

Most candidates can say "tensor parallelism, pipeline parallelism, ZeRO". That is like a backend engineer saying
"sharding, replication, caching" without knowing where the network calls happen or what each one costs. The depth
being asked for is the second kind: given a profiler trace from a training job, you can predict which exchange is
which and why it's there.

### Words you need first

- **Parameter**: one learned number in the model. Training also stores a **gradient** for each (which way to move
  it) and the optimizer's bookkeeping.
- **Activations**: the intermediate results each layer produces in the forward pass, kept for the backward pass.
  They are often the biggest memory cost.
- **Collective**: an exchange where every GPU in a group contributes and gets a combined result. The ones here are
  **all-reduce** (everyone gets the sum), **reduce-scatter** (everyone gets the sum of one slice) and **all-gather**
  (everyone collects all slices). An all-reduce is a reduce-scatter followed by an all-gather.
- **Tensor parallelism (TP)**: splitting each layer's matrix math across the GPUs of one machine.

The source is one Blind poster's advice that training- and inference-infra candidates need "Megatron-LM (TP/PP/SP),
ZeRO, FlashAttention" and that "if you can't go source-code deep, don't bother applying"; commenters suspected the
post was an ad (<Cite id="s-a129aa6451" />).

## Clarify first

If an interviewer opens this way, ask:

- **Which framework?** "Does the team use Megatron-Core, DeepSpeed or PyTorch FSDP?"
- **Which goal?** "Is the focus training throughput, memory, or long inputs?"
- **Which format?** "Are we reading code, or discussing a design?"

## Answer

Walk the four techniques in the order data flows through one transformer layer (the building block a language
model repeats dozens of times), then the optimizer.

### Tensor parallelism, at the level of the calls

In [Megatron-LM](https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.08053), two small functions plug into automatic differentiation and
carry all the communication:

- **f**: does nothing in the forward pass; all-reduces in the backward pass.
- **g**: all-reduces in the forward pass; does nothing in the backward pass.

Each block (the attention, and the feed-forward part called the MLP) starts with a **column-parallel** linear layer, with f at its input, and ends
with a **row-parallel** linear layer, with g at its output. The column split leaves each GPU with a slice of the
output that the row split can use directly, so nothing is exchanged in the middle. The paper counts **two
all-reduces forward and two backward per layer**.

Say it like this: "If I know where f and g sit, I can look at a trace and say which all-reduce belongs to which
block."

### Sequence parallelism

Between those blocks, a few cheap operations (layer normalization and dropout) run on the full data on every GPU of
the tensor-parallel group. That repeats work and wastes activation memory.

Korthikanti et al. ([2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.05198)) split those regions along the sequence instead: each
GPU handles a different part of the input text. They replace each all-reduce with a **reduce-scatter** on the way
into the sequence-split region and an **all-gather** on the way out. Because an all-reduce already is a
reduce-scatter plus an all-gather, they report **no extra communication**. Together with **selective
recomputation** (discarding some activations and recomputing them in the backward pass), they report **5× less
activation memory**.

Say it like this: "Sequence parallelism doesn't add traffic. It splits the same all-reduce into its two halves and
does useful work in between."

### Pipeline parallelism

Pipeline parallelism puts different layers on different GPUs, called **stages**. Stages exchange activations
point-to-point. The batch is cut into **micro-batches** so several stages can work at once. The schedule is the code
you'd be asked about:

- **1F1B** alternates one forward and one backward micro-batch, which caps how many micro-batches' activations are
  held at once.
- The **interleaved** schedule gives each GPU v smaller chunks of layers. It cuts the idle share (the **bubble**)
  from (p − 1) ÷ m to (p − 1) ÷ (v·m), for p stages and m micro-batches, at the cost of v times the communication
  ([Narayanan et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.04473)).

### ZeRO

Mixed-precision training with the Adam optimizer costs **16 bytes per parameter**: 2 for the 16-bit weight, 2 for
its gradient, and 12 for a 32-bit master copy and Adam's two running averages. In plain data parallelism every GPU
holds all of it. ZeRO ([Rajbhandari et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.02054)) splits it across the GPUs that
hold copies of the model:

| Stage | What is split | Memory saving (paper) | Extra traffic |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | optimizer state | 4× | none |
| 2 | + gradients | 8× | none |
| 3 | + the weights themselves | grows with the number of copies | **1.5×**, because weights are all-gathered before each layer's forward and backward pass |

Follow the paper's own example: a **7.5B model with 64-way data parallelism** needs **120 GB per GPU** unsplit and
**31.4 GB** with stage 1.

### FlashAttention, in one line

Attention compares every token with every other token, which normally builds a full table of scores (s × s for a
sequence of s tokens). FlashAttention computes attention in tiles without ever storing that table. In the
activation-memory formula Korthikanti et al. give, that table is the 5as/h term (a is the number of attention
heads, s the sequence length, h the hidden size).

## Small-team version

A small team doesn't need Megatron internals. It needs to recognize the FSDP and tensor-parallel options in a
fine-tuning or serving config, and to estimate memory (16 bytes per trained parameter) before renting GPUs. Spend
source-code depth on whatever framework you actually run.

## Pitfalls

- **Treating a suspected ad as a known interview format.** It is one poster's advice, not a reported question.
- **Saying sequence parallelism adds communication.** It moves the same bytes, split into two halves.
- **Confusing ZeRO-3's 1.5× traffic with stages 1 and 2.** Only splitting the weights adds traffic.
- **Reciting names with no bytes attached.** Depth means knowing what each call moves.

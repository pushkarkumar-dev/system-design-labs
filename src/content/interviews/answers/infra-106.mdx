## What they're really asking

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "Yes, I have one real concern. First, here's the best case for what you're doing, from your own published
> policy. My concern is the tension between that principle and competitive pressure, and here's why. I'd be less
> worried if I could see this kind of evidence from outside. And that tension is part of why I want to work here:
> it's the part of the problem I'd like to help with."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer, so you can fill it in from the company's own documents.

The interviewer wants to know one thing: can you be candid about the company you want to join, show you've
understood its reasoning, and still want in?

This is unlike most interview questions, where agreeing with the interviewer is safe. Here, agreeing is the
risk. Two answers fail. **Flattery** ("I love the mission, no concerns") tells them you either haven't thought
about it or won't say what you think. An **uninformed critique** (a complaint you could make about any AI
company) tells them you haven't read what this company publishes. The answer that works sits between them: a
specific concern, the company's best argument against it, and a reason you still want the job.

Some companies ask this in a **values round** (also called a culture round): an interview about how you reason
through ethical tension and whether you share the company's goals. Two first-hand accounts describe the question
in Anthropic's values or culture round (<Cite id="s-4e04a1689c" />, <Cite id="s-42f1c7f6dd" />).

### Words you need first

- To **steelman** an argument is to state the strongest version of the other side before you disagree. It is
  the opposite of a straw man.
- A **safety-first** AI company is one that says it will accept slower or narrower launches to reduce risks from
  its models. Anthropic presents itself this way.
- Anthropic's **Responsible Scaling Policy (RSP)** is its published policy that ties how capable its models get
  to the safeguards it must have in place first.

## Clarify first

- **Mission or approach?** "Do you mean the mission itself, or how the company pursues it?" The reported wording
  is "approach or direction", so the second is fair game.
- **Industry concerns.** "Is a concern about the industry, not just the company, useful here?" Often yes, if you
  connect it back to the company.
- **Don't ask whether it's safe to critique.** The question is sincere, and asking suggests you think it's a trap.

## Answer

### What the accounts show

Both accounts come from Anthropic candidates: one for an inference role (the team that runs the models for
users), one for the Safeguards team.

The inference candidate was asked "Do you have any critique or concerns about Anthropic's approach or
direction?" They answered that the safety-first positioning is "part of the brand", that "they probably regret
not being first on some launches", and "I buy the mission, but I also think they're still a competitive company
trying to win." They were rejected, and they don't say this answer was why (<Cite id="s-4e04a1689c" />).

The Safeguards candidate said "I am not totally confident anyone can reach the level of safety they say they
want", and that some of it "can look like overengineering when other players are willing to cut corners." Their
reading: the interviewers "wanted to see whether I would push back instead of just flattering the mission".
Their outcome was pending (<Cite id="s-42f1c7f6dd" />).

Neither account tells you what passes. Both show the round wants a real opinion.

The round's weight is reported too. The Safeguards candidate's recruiter said the values round "was the one where
people usually fail" (<Cite id="s-42f1c7f6dd" />). A Senior Staff candidate's Blind post (Blind is an anonymous
forum for tech employees) is titled "I think the culture round sank my Anthropic Sr Staff loop"
(<Cite id="s-e98cbef9cd" />). That is the candidate's own belief, not feedback the company gave.

### The example we will follow

This page follows one illustrative candidate, Sam, preparing for this question at Anthropic. Sam's concern is
the same shape as both reported answers: a tension between a stated safety principle and competitive pressure.
Use the shape; your concern must be your own.

### A four-part structure

| Part | What it means | Sam's version (illustrative) | What to say in the interview |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Steelman** | State the company's position and its best reason, from something it has published | Reads the RSP and summarises its logic | "Your policy says stronger models need stronger safeguards first. A reason I find convincing is that some risks are hard to undo." |
| **2. Concern** | One or two specific critiques | Tension between that principle and pressure to launch | "My concern is what happens when a competitor launches first and the safeguards aren't ready." |
| **3. What would resolve it** | Observable evidence that would change your mind | Evidence that the policy has held under pressure | "I'd be less worried if I could see, from outside, a case where it slowed a launch." |
| **4. Why you still want in** | The part of the problem you'd work on because of the concern | Wants to make safeguards cheaper to build | "If safeguards are cheaper, the tension is smaller. That's work I'd like to do." |

For part 1 at Anthropic, the RSP is a natural starting document; it ties capability thresholds to required
safeguards ([Anthropic RSP, version 3.4, effective 2026-07-08](https://www.anthropic.com/responsible-scaling-policy)).
Quote its reasoning in your own words, from the version you read.

Other common shapes for part 2:

- **A principle versus competitive pressure**, as above.
- **A commitment that can't be checked from outside**: you have to take the company's word for it.
- **A trade-off whose cost falls on users**: for example, a caution that makes the product less useful.

Keep the whole answer to about ninety seconds. The follow-ups ("why do you think that?", "what would you do
about it?") test whether you read the material, so leave room for them.

### Prepare it like a technical round

The order of work, like any design prep:

```
Read the company's current policies and recent research
   │
   ▼
Write two candidate critiques
   │
   ▼
Test each against the company's strongest counter-argument
   │
   ▼
Keep the one that survives, plus the evidence that would resolve it
```

Using AI to find and summarize the documents is reasonable. Reciting a generated critique isn't, because the
follow-ups will go past the summary. Say it like this: "I read the policy itself, not a summary, because I
wanted to know where my concern actually comes from."

## Senior vs Staff

<LevelContrast
  title="Critiquing the mission"
  senior={["An honest, specific concern, with the company's reasoning acknowledged."]}
  staff={["A critique grounded in the company's own documents, with the evidence that would resolve it and the part of the work they'd take on because of it."]}
/>

In plain words: a Senior answer is honest and fair. A Staff answer also turns the concern into work: what you
would build or change because of it.

## Small-team version

At a small company the equivalent is "what would you change about our AI roadmap or product?" from a founder. Use
the same structure: why they chose the current path, your concern, the evidence that would settle it, and why you
still want to build it. Founders tend to read candor plus a plan as ownership.

Put it in actions and money where you can. Say it like this (illustrative): "You chose to build the assistant
into every page, and I see why: it's your demo. My concern is that most support cost comes from one workflow.
I'd check that in the first two weeks by reading a sample of support tickets. If I'm right, I'd put the
first quarter there." That is a critique, a test and a plan in four sentences.

## Pitfalls

- **Flattering the mission.** It shows you either haven't thought about it or won't say what you think, and the
  question exists to find that out.
- **A critique that shows you haven't read the company's own material.** It suggests you didn't prepare, and the
  follow-ups will expose it.
- **A critique with no reason to join.** It sounds like a reason not to hire you.
- **A generic industry complaint that never comes back to the company.** It answers a different question from
  the one asked.

## What they're really asking

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "Every choice I made, I can back with a measurement: I compared keyword, vector and combined search on a
> labeled question set, and the README leads with those numbers. At 100× the data, the search code barely
> changes; ingestion becomes a resumable job keyed by a content hash, so retries never duplicate anything. Under
> rate limits I batch requests, cap concurrency below my quota, and back off with jitter on a 429 with a cap on
> attempts. And the rate limit sets a floor on ingest time that more workers can't beat."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer.

The take-home itself proves you can build. The live follow-ups test two other things: whether your choices were
decisions backed by measurement, and whether you can see the production system behind the prototype.

What makes this different from defending an ordinary take-home is that the system's quality isn't pass/fail. A
retrieval system always returns something, and a language model writes a fluent answer from it, right or wrong.
So "why this chunking?" has no answer from first principles. The only convincing answer is "I tried the
alternatives on a set of questions with known answers, and here are the numbers." The scale questions are more
familiar ground for a backend engineer: idempotent jobs, retries and rate limits. The twist is that the
provider's limit is counted in tokens, not requests.

(Reported for an OpenAI forward-deployed engineer take-home: about five hours on OpenAI's APIs plus a recorded
video. The follow-ups were "why this chunking strategy, why not a different retrieval method", what would change
"if the dataset was 100x larger", and "API rate limiting, retry patterns" <Cite id="s-812dbc9385" />.)

### Words you need first

- **RAG** (retrieval-augmented generation): find a few relevant pieces of your documents and paste them into a
  language model's prompt so it answers from them.
- **Chunk**: one of those pieces. **Chunking** is how you split documents into them.
- **Embedding**: a vector of numbers computed from text by an embedding model, so similar meanings point in
  similar directions. **Dense** search ranks chunks by vector closeness; **BM25** is the standard keyword-ranking
  formula; **hybrid** merges the two.
- **Token**: the unit providers count, roughly ¾ of a word. A **TPM** limit is tokens per minute. When you exceed
  a limit, the API replies with HTTP **429** ("Too Many Requests").

## Clarify first

- **What corpus and what users does the brief describe?** Reuse its wording in your README.
- **What would "working well" mean to the customer in the brief?** Turn it into a labeled set and a metric.
- **Which OpenAI APIs are in scope?** "I'll assume embeddings plus chat; batch only if the brief mentions bulk
  work."
- **What does the video need to cover?** "I'll assume a 5–10 minute walkthrough: demo, design, numbers, limits."

## Answer

### In the take-home

Spend the hours roughly in thirds:

1. **A third on the pipeline.** Chunking, embedding, keyword and vector search, a cited answer.
2. **A third on measurement.** A 30–50-question labeled set and the eval script.
3. **A third on the README, the video and the failure cases.**

Lead the README with numbers. Report **hit@5** (the share of questions with at least one right chunk in the top
5) and **recall@5** (the share of all right chunks that made the top 5) for BM25-only, dense-only and hybrid,
each with an error bar. Then show two failures you fixed and one you didn't.

### "Why this chunking, why not another retrieval method?"

Answer with the experiment, not the opinion. Say: "I split on headings and paragraphs, sized under the embedding
model's input limit, with the heading path prepended. Fixed windows cut tables and sentences. I compared dense,
BM25 and hybrid on the labeled set; hybrid won on identifier-heavy questions."

The rag lab shows why a measurement beats a belief here. On SciFact, a public benchmark of science claims, its
hybrid added nothing until the keyword side's word-splitting (its tokenizer) was fixed. Its hit@3 (like hit@5, but for the top 3) was
<LabNumber lab="rag" metric="hit@3 on BEIR SciFact" /> (dense, hybrid, rerank), against
<LabNumber lab="rag" metric="BM25 tokenizer (SciFact)" /> for hybrid with a `\w+` tokenizer. Someone who
"knew" hybrid was better would never have looked.

### "What changes at 100×?"

Follow the ingestion job, because that is what changes most:

```
Documents (5,000 instead of 50)
   │
   ▼
Chunker ── hash = sha256(chunk text + embedding model name)
   │
   ▼
Already embedded? ──yes──▶ skip  (a re-run or retry costs nothing)
   │ no
   ▼
Batcher ── many chunks per embedding request
   │
   ▼
Token bucket ── caps tokens per minute below the account's TPM limit
   │
   ▼
Embedding API ──429 or 5xx──▶ back off with jitter, honour Retry-After, cap attempts
   │ ok
   ▼
Store vector keyed by hash, then checkpoint progress
```

- **Ingestion becomes a resumable job.** Content-hash each chunk with the model name, skip unchanged hashes, and
  checkpoint progress.
- **Search changes less than people expect.** BM25 moves to a real inverted index (the per-word lists of
  documents a search engine keeps). Vectors move to an approximate index only when flat search (comparing the query with every
  vector) gets slow; flat NumPy over 50,000 vectors is still a few milliseconds.
- **Updates, deletes and re-embedding on model change** become operations you own.
- **The eval set grows.** 30 questions over 100× the documents measure little.

### "Rate limits and retries"

Batch many inputs per embedding request. Cap concurrency on your side, and pace requests with a **token bucket**
(a counter that refills at a fixed rate and that each request draws from) sized below your account's limit. On a 429 or a 5xx,
back off exponentially with **jitter** (a random part of the delay, so workers don't retry in lockstep), honour
`Retry-After`, and cap attempts. Writes keyed by content hash are idempotent, which makes retries safe. Chat
requests get timeouts and one fallback (a smaller model, or "try again later"), not unbounded retries.

The rate limit also sets a floor on ingest time. With assumed numbers (100× of 50 documents at 2,000 tokens each,
and a 1M TPM limit), ingestion takes **at least 10 minutes**, however many workers you run. Say: "More workers
don't beat the TPM limit. I'd show progress and tell the user when it will finish."

## Small-team version

This take-home is a small team's normal week: a working feature, a README with a number, and a list of what
breaks next.

- **The embedding bill doesn't matter.** text-embedding-3-small was listed at $0.02 per million tokens on
  2026-10-11, so 10M tokens is about **$0.20**.
- **What matters is safety under failure.** Retries must not duplicate data, and a rate-limit storm must not take
  the feature down.
- **Build one shared token bucket in front of the provider.** It does more than any retry tuning, because it
  stops the 429s before they happen.

## Pitfalls

- **Defending chunking with "it's the standard size".** That is a belief; the interviewer wants a comparison.
- **Retrying without jitter or a cap.** Synchronized, unlimited retries turn a 429 into a self-inflicted outage.
- **A video that only demos the happy path.** It never shows a failure or a number, which is what reviewers look
  for.
- **Answering "100×" with "use a vector database".** The hard part at 100× is ingestion, not search.

## What they're really asking

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "I rolled out a faster way for our workers to load AI models, to every worker in one step. A bug in its cache
> made about 4% of requests fail for 25 minutes, and a customer told us before our alerts did. I rolled it back,
> wrote the incident review naming my change, and we added a small first stage for every rollout plus an alert on
> model-load failures. The bold part was going all at once; I'd written down the downside first, but I hadn't set
> an alert that would catch it."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer, so you can build the same shape from your own work.
> (That story and its numbers are invented to show the level of detail. Use your own.)

The interviewer is asking two things. Do you take responsibility for production when your change hurts a customer?
And when you take a risky call, do you take it on purpose, with the downside written down?

### What makes this question different

You have probably told an incident story before. The usual version is about the debugging: the clue, the fix, the
relief. This prompt cares less about the debugging and more about **ownership**. Whose change was it? How did the
customer find out? What did you change so it couldn't happen the same way again?

It also comes in two halves that pull in opposite directions. One half asks about a failure you owned. The other
asks about a **bold decision**: a risky call that could have failed badly. The interviewer wants to see that you
take risks, and that your risks are deliberate rather than careless.

A techinterview.org prep guide reports Modal pairing "tell me about a time you owned an infrastructure failure that
hit a customer" with "tell me about a bold systems decision you made that could have failed badly", and says strong
answers are "specific and a little uncomfortable": "Real ownership has scar tissue, and they're looking for it"
(<Cite id="s-86890a1069" />). The guide cites no first-hand sources, so treat this as advice about what lands, not a
confirmed process.

## Clarify first

- **Caused or led.** "Should the failure be one I caused, or one I led the response to?" One you caused is
  stronger; say which you are telling.
- **Which customer.** "Internal customer or external?" The prompt says customer; an internal team that depended on
  you can work, but say so.
- **Both halves.** Confirm whether they want both halves now or one at a time.

## Answer

### Words you need first

- The **blast radius** is how much of the system, or how many customers, a failure reached.
- A **staged rollout** releases a change to a small slice first (often called a **canary** stage), watches it, then
  widens. It limits the blast radius of a bad change.
- A **cold start** is the time a worker spends loading a model into memory before it can serve requests. AI models
  are large, so cold starts can be slow, which is why the example change existed.
- A **blameless** incident review focuses on the system that allowed a mistake, not on punishing the person who made
  it. It does not mean nobody says whose change it was.

### The failure half

```
What broke ──────── the symptom, the duration, the blast radius
   ▼
Your part ───────── "a change I made", not "a change was made"
   ▼
Detection ───────── alert or customer? If a customer, say so
   ▼
Mitigation ──────── what you did, how long, roll back or fix forward
   ▼
Communication ───── who you told, how the customer heard
   ▼
Root cause ──────── the missing control behind the trigger
   ▼
What changed ────── a concrete control that now exists
```

| Step | What happens in the story | What to say in the interview |
|---|---|---|
| **1. What broke** | About 4% of requests failed for 25 minutes, across every worker. | "About 4% of requests failed for 25 minutes. Every customer could have hit it." |
| **2. Your part** | I wrote the new model-loading path and rolled it out. | "It was my change." |
| **3. Detection** | A customer reported errors about 10 minutes before our error-rate alert fired. | "A customer found it before we did. That's part of the lesson." |
| **4. Mitigation** | I rolled back rather than fixing forward, because rollback was one command and the fix wasn't understood yet. | "I rolled back first and debugged second." |
| **5. Communication** | I posted in the incident channel and wrote to the reporting customer the same day with what happened. | "The customer heard it from me, not from a status page." |
| **6. Root cause** | The trigger was a cache bug. The cause was that a change to every worker could ship in one step. | "The bug was mine. The fact that one change could reach everyone was the system's." |
| **7. What changed** | Every rollout now starts with a 5% canary stage, and there is an alert on model-load failures. | "A canary stage and a load-failure alert. Both exist now." |

Say it like this about step 6: "The trigger was my change. The cause was the missing control that let one change
reach every worker at once."

### The bold-decision half

Pick a decision where you stated the downside before you made it, set a rollback trigger, and accepted the risk on
purpose. A **rollback trigger** is a number agreed in advance that, if crossed, means you undo the change.

Good examples: a migration without a long period of running old and new side by side, a new scheduler on the
critical path, dropping a dependency. What makes a decision bold rather than reckless is the written downside and
the trigger. If it went well, say what you would have done if it hadn't.

The example story can serve both halves, which is useful when time is short. Going all-at-once was the bold call,
made to cut cold starts quickly. The honest part: you wrote down the downside, but the trigger depended on an alert
that wasn't watching the right thing. Say it like this: "The bet was deliberate. My safety net had a hole, and the
customer found it."

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the numbers (for a Staff-level follow-up)</summary>

- **The blast radius in requests.** At an assumed 2,000 requests a minute, 4% failing for 25 minutes is
  2,000 × 0.04 × 25 = **2,000 failed requests**. Give the interviewer a number like this; "some errors" can't be
  weighed.
- **What a canary stage would have done.** With a 5% canary, the same bug would have reached 5% of traffic, so
  about 4% of that 5%, or 0.2% of all requests, would have failed. Over the same 25 minutes that is about 100
  failed requests instead of 2,000, if the canary was watched by the right alert.
- **Why the alert mattered as much as the canary.** A canary only limits damage if something notices it failing.
  The 10-minute gap between the customer's report and the alert is the number that justified the new load-failure
  alert. Every number here is invented for the story.

</details>

## Senior vs Staff

<LevelContrast
  title="Owning a failure"
  senior={["Owns the incident, mitigates fast, and fixes the specific cause."]}
  staff={["Changes the system that allowed it (rollout policy, review gate, default), and treats the bold decision as a bet with a stated downside and exit."]}
/>

In the example, the Senior version fixes the cache bug and adds a test for it. The Staff version makes the canary
stage the default for every team's rollouts and adds the alert that would have caught it, so the next person's bug
reaches 5% of traffic, not all of it.

## Small-team version

At a small company there is often no site reliability engineering (**SRE**) team to run on-call and incident
response. The AI owner handles the provider outage, the bad change to the instructions sent to the model, and the
silent drop in answer quality.

Quality incidents are the hard case, because nothing errors and no alert fires. Anthropic's postmortem of three 2025
bugs says benchmarks, safety evals, spot checks and canaries missed the degradation
([Anthropic, 2025-09-17](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/a-postmortem-of-three-recent-issues)).

So a strong small-team story includes the quality monitoring you added afterwards: regularly sampling real answers
and grading them, not just watching error rates. Say it like this: "Error rates told us nothing. Now a sample of
real answers gets graded every week."

## Pitfalls

- **An incident with no real impact, or someone else's fault.** It avoids the ownership the question is about.
- **Hiding behind "blameless".** A blameless review still says whose change it was; using it to avoid naming your
  part reads as evasion.
- **A "bold" decision that was just untested.** Without a written downside and a trigger, it was reckless, not bold.
- **Ending on the fix.** If nothing changed in the system that allowed it, the same failure can happen to the next
  person.

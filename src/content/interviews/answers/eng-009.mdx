## What they're really asking

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "I'd turn 'is retrieval working?' into a number. I'd collect a few hundred real questions and have someone
> who knows the domain mark which documents answer each one. Then I'd measure how often the right chunks land
> in the top k we actually send to the model, and also in the top 50 before any reranking, so I know whether
> search or ranking is the weak part. I'd put an interval on every number and compare versions on the same
> questions. In production, where there are no labels, I'd watch citations, declines and user feedback, and feed
> every failure back into the labeled set."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer.

The interviewer wants to know whether you can measure something that has no error code.

In a normal backend service, "is it working?" has a clear answer: requests succeed, tests pass, the p99 is under
target. A search step inside an AI app is different. It always returns *something*, with status 200. Whether
those results were the right ones is a matter of judgment, and it varies from question to question. So the
only honest answer is a rate measured over many questions, with a known uncertainty.

### Words you need first

- **RAG** (retrieval-augmented generation): before a language model answers, the system searches your own
  documents and pastes the best passages into the prompt. Finding the passages is **retrieval**.
- Documents are split into passages of a few hundred words called **chunks**. Search returns chunks.
- The search returns chunks in score order. The best k are the **top k**, and k is how many you send to the model.
- A **labeled query set** is a list of real questions where a person has marked which chunks or documents are
  relevant to each one. It is the test suite for retrieval.

This question comes from a prep guide's list for OpenAI's forward-deployed technical deep dive, not from a
candidate report. The guide's own framing: "The reliable differentiator is a concrete answer to how you know the
system works" (<Cite id="s-26f0cae3fe" />).

## Clarify first

Ask these before answering. Each one changes the metric you pick.

- **How many chunks reach the model, and does an answer usually need one passage or several?** "If one passage
  is enough, I care whether any right chunk shows up. If the answer needs three, I care how many of them show up."
- **Is there a labeled set, or only logs?** *Assume logs but no labels.*
- **Is there a reranker** (a second, slower model that reorders the search results)? "Then I'll measure both
  before and after it."
- **Offline evaluation, production monitoring, or both?** *Cover both briefly.*

## Answer

### Follow one question through the measurement

Use one example the whole way. A customer asks a software company's support bot: *"Can I get a refund on the
annual plan 45 days after I paid?"* (invented for illustration). A domain expert marks two chunks as relevant:

- **A**, the current refund policy ("refundable within 30 days"), which fully answers it;
- **B**, a billing FAQ entry that mentions the 30-day window.

Search returns **C, A, D, B, E**, where C is an outdated 2024 policy and D and E are unrelated. The bot sends the
top 3 to the model.

| Step | What you do | Result for this question | What to say in the interview |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Build the set | Sample 50–200 real questions from logs, add every reported bad answer, add a few the documents can't answer | This question is one row | "The labeled set is the asset. Everything else is a script." |
| 2. Pick the metric | Match it to how the model uses the results | The answer could come from A alone, but B helps | "I report the metric at the k we actually send." |
| 3. Measure at the final k | Share of relevant chunks in the top 3 (recall@3) | **0.5**: A is there, B isn't | "The model saw half of what's relevant." |
| 4. Measure at candidate depth | Share in the top 50 (recall@50) | **1.0**: both were found | "Search found everything. Ranking is the weak part here." |
| 5. Put an interval on it | Average over all questions, with a 95% interval | e.g. recall@3 = 0.80 on 200 questions, about ±5.5 points | "A 3-point change on 200 questions is inside the noise." |
| 6. Compare paired | Run both versions on the same questions | Count per-question wins and losses | "Paired comparison removes most of the noise." |

### Pick the metric by how results are used

There are four common retrieval metrics. Each answers a different question, so the right one depends on what
happens to the results next:

- **hit@k**: did *any* relevant chunk appear in the top k? Use it when one passage is enough to answer. Many
  pieces of code called "recall" actually compute this.
- **recall@k**: what *share* of the relevant chunks appeared in the top k? Use it when the model reads all k
  chunks and the answer needs several of them. Measure it at the k you actually send.
- **MRR** (mean reciprocal rank): 1 ÷ the rank of the first relevant result, averaged over questions. Use it
  when only the first result matters, such as a search box or a tool that returns one item.
- **nDCG@k** (normalized discounted cumulative gain): a score that uses graded relevance ("fully answers" vs
  "partly") and counts lower positions less. Use it when grades and the whole order matter, as on a results page.

For the refund question: hit@3 = 1, recall@3 = 0.5, and the reciprocal rank is 0.5 (the first relevant chunk is
at rank 2). Say: "Hit@3 says this question is fine. Recall@3 says the model missed half the evidence. For a bot
that reads all three chunks, recall@3 is the honest number."

### Why two depths

A reranker can only reorder what search found. So measure recall twice. **Recall at candidate depth** (the top 50
before reranking) tells you what a reranker could possibly recover. **Recall at the final k** tells you what the
model actually sees. If recall@50 is high and recall@3 is low, invest in ranking. If recall@50 is low, a reranker
cannot help; fix chunking or search first.

The rag lab (this site's hands-on retrieval lab) shows this: reranking improved the top 3 but left hit@20
unchanged. It can only reorder.

### Put an interval on it, and compare paired

Every rate measured on a sample is uncertain. The 95% **interval** is the range the true value very likely sits
in. At a measured rate of 80%, **20 questions give about ±17.5 points** and **200 give about ±5.5 points**.

To compare two versions, run both on the same questions and look at the difference question by question. This
**paired comparison** is much tighter, because most questions get the same result under both versions and only
the disagreements add noise.

The rag lab is a worked example of reading such numbers: hit@3 on SciFact (a public set of scientific-claim
questions) was <LabNumber lab="rag" metric="hit@3 on BEIR SciFact" /> for vector search, hybrid search and
reranking. With about 300 queries, gaps under about 4 points are noise, so only the reranker's gain cleared its
interval.

### Online: proxies, because production has no labels

In production nobody labels each question, so you cannot compute recall. Watch signals that move with
retrieval quality instead:

- the share of answers with valid citations (each one pointing at a chunk that was actually in the prompt);
- how often the bot declines ("I don't know");
- thumbs-down and escalation rates;
- the spread of the top retrieval scores. A shift often means new kinds of questions or a broken index.

Then run a calibrated **LLM judge** (a second model, checked against human labels, that grades answers) on a
daily sample, asking "did the retrieved context contain what the answer needed?" Move each failure you find into
the labeled set.

## Small-team version

The labeled set is the expensive part, and the cost is people's time, not compute. 200 questions at an assumed 3
minutes each is about **10 hours of a domain expert**, roughly **$930** at an assumed loaded rate of $93 an hour.
Start with 50 plus the reported failures.

- **Build** the metric script yourself. It is an afternoon of work; the family's coding walkthrough has one.
- **Buy** the judge model from the provider you already use. A daily 200-item judge run costs about **$0.19** on
  gpt-5-mini or **$1.30** on Claude Sonnet 5 (list prices fetched 2026-10-11).
- **Grow** the set past 200 when the changes you care about are smaller than its ±5.5-point interval.

## Pitfalls

- **"We look at some answers" as the whole method.** Without a number, the guide says, the interviewer "assumes
  you tuned yours by feel" (<Cite id="s-7d03a572e0" />).
- **Reporting recall at a k the model never sees.** A perfect recall@20 means nothing if the model gets 3 chunks.
- **Only synthetic questions generated from the chunks.** They reuse the chunks' own wording, so search looks
  better on them than on real users.
- **No interval.** A 2-point change on a few hundred questions gets reported as a win when it may be noise.

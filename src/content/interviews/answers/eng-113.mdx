## What they're really asking

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "A colleague and I shared a goal but disagreed on how to reach it. They wanted to train a small model of our own;
> I wanted to keep a hosted model and feed it our documents. Their real worry was cost per request at our volume,
> and that was a fair worry. Instead of arguing, we agreed a two-week test on the same set of real questions, and
> agreed beforehand what result would decide it. My approach won on quality, but their cost estimate held, so we
> added caching. They own the cost dashboard now."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer, so you can build the same shape from your own work.
> (That story and its numbers are invented to show the level of detail. Use your own.)

The interviewer is asking whether you can change a peer's mind without authority over them, and whether you
describe their view fairly while you do it.

### What makes this question different

Most "conflict" stories you have told before end with the other person coming round. That ending is exactly what
this question is designed to catch. The interviewer is not scoring whether you won. They are scoring whether you
understood why a reasonable person disagreed with you, and whether the disagreement changed your plan.

So a strong story here has three features a typical conflict story lacks:

1. **Both positions were defensible.** You can state the other person's view so well they would sign it.
2. **The disagreement was settled by something other than persistence.** Usually an agreed test.
3. **You changed something because of them.** If their objection improved nothing, it was not much of a
   disagreement.

Hello Interview's OpenAI prep guide lists this prompt, and says explaining "why a disagreement was reasonable on
both sides says more about your judgment" than a story where the other person came round. For Staff it asks for
examples where you "influenced a decision, handled disagreement, or helped other engineers take on work they
couldn't have owned before" (<Cite id="s-4d55d9b987" />).

## Clarify first

- **Which peer.** "A peer on my team, or in another team?" The guide's wording is "a peer who had a differing
  opinion about a shared goal" (<Cite id="s-4d55d9b987" />). Pick a story where you shared the goal and disagreed
  on the path.
- **Which ending.** "Should the outcome be one where I won?" Ask, or volunteer that you have a story where the
  final plan was a mix, or where they were partly right. Those are often stronger.

## Answer

### Words you need first

The example story is about a common AI-era disagreement. Three terms make it readable:

- **Fine-tuning** means training an existing AI model further on your own examples, so it gets better at one
  narrow task. You then run that model yourself, or pay someone to.
- A **hosted model** is one you call over a provider's web API (Anthropic, OpenAI and others), paying per use.
- **Retrieval** means searching your own documents for the passages relevant to a question and pasting them into
  the text you send the model, so it answers from your material.

You also need one interview term: an **eval set**, a fixed list of real questions with known good answers, used
to score an AI feature the way a test suite scores code.

### Pick the right story

You want a disagreement about means, not ends, where both positions were defensible and the resolution changed
something you believed. Good candidates in AI work:

- build versus buy for an AI component,
- the order of a migration,
- the pass mark for an eval set before launch,
- whether to run a model yourself or keep calling a provider.

### One story, followed through

Here is the invented example told in order. Every number is made up.

| Step | What happens in the story | What to say in the interview |
|---|---|---|
| **1. The shared goal** | Both of us wanted the support assistant to answer billing questions well and cheaply. | "We agreed on the goal. We disagreed on the path." |
| **2. Their position and yours** | They wanted to fine-tune a small model. I wanted a hosted model with retrieval. | "Their case was strong: at our volume, a hosted model's per-request cost would add up." |
| **3. What they were protecting** | Cost per request at about 200,000 questions a month. | "They were protecting the budget, which they'd been asked to own." |
| **4. Why it mattered** | Picking wrong meant either poor answers or a bill nobody had planned for. | "Either mistake would have taken a quarter to undo." |
| **5. The mechanism** | Both approaches ran on the same 300-question eval set for two weeks. We agreed first: the winner must be at least 5 points better on quality, otherwise the cheaper one wins. | "We wrote down the decision rule before we saw the results." |
| **6. What they got right** | Retrieval won on quality, but their cost estimate was right, so we added caching for repeated questions. | "Their objection is why we have caching." |
| **7. Outcome** | Retrieval shipped; caching held the bill to plan; they own the cost dashboard. | "They own that dashboard now, which is the right person to own it." |

Two parts of the table carry the story.

**Step 5 is the mechanism.** It turned an argument about opinions into a test both sides accepted, and the rule
was fixed before anyone saw data. That is the strongest sign you were persuading with evidence, not stamina. Say
it like this: "If the test had gone the other way, I'd have dropped my proposal. That was the deal."

**Step 6 is the fairness test.** The interviewer will often ask "what did they get right?" If you have an answer
ready, and it changed the design, your fairness was real.

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the numbers (for a Staff-level follow-up)</summary>

- **Why the decision rule had a margin.** On a 300-question eval set, a small difference can be noise: one
  approach may get a few more questions right by luck. Requiring at least 5 points (15 questions out of 300) before
  picking the more expensive option protected the cheaper one from losing on noise. The 5-point margin is an
  assumption for this story; in a real one, say how you chose yours.
- **Why cost mattered at all.** At an assumed 200,000 questions a month, every extra tenth of a cent per question
  is about $200 a month (200,000 × $0.001). Small per-request differences become a budget line at volume, which is
  why the peer's worry was reasonable.

</details>

### Mechanisms that work, and one that doesn't

Credible ways a disagreement gets settled:

- **You asked what evidence would change their mind,** and agreed a test.
- **You built a small prototype** that answered the open question.
- **You changed scope** to meet their concern.
- **You wrote both positions down** and took it to whoever owned the trade-off, when it really was their call.

Skip "I set up a meeting and explained". It describes a meeting, not a change of mind.

## Senior vs Staff

<LevelContrast
  title="Influencing a peer"
  senior={["Resolves a disagreement with data or a prototype and ships the result."]}
  staff={["Turns the disagreement into a decision rule both agreed before the result, concedes what the peer got right, and leaves the peer owning part of the outcome."]}
/>

In the example, the Senior version is "we ran a test and retrieval won". The Staff version adds the decision rule
agreed in advance, the caching that came from the peer's objection, and the peer owning the cost dashboard
afterwards. That last part is what the OpenAI guide means by helping someone "take on work they couldn't have
owned before" (<Cite id="s-4d55d9b987" />).

## Small-team version

At a 50–500 person company your "peers" include the head of support, a product lead, or the one platform
engineer. Many of the disagreements are about whether an AI feature is ready to launch.

With no committee to settle it, agreeing the test before running it is how you avoid a decision made by whoever
keeps arguing longest. It is also cheap: a 50-question eval set that the support lead grades is, as a rough estimate,
a day or two of work, and it can settle the same argument many times over.

Say how you would have dropped your proposal if the test had gone the other way. That is the clearest sign you were
persuading with evidence.

## Pitfalls

- **The peer as a villain.** A story that ends "and they realized I was right" shows you can't model why someone
  reasonable disagreed.
- **Escalating first.** Going to a manager before trying to settle it yourself suggests you avoid hard
  conversations.
- **Influence through persistence.** "I kept bringing it up until they agreed" is stamina, not persuasion.
- **No change in your own position.** If nothing about your plan changed, the interviewer will doubt you listened.

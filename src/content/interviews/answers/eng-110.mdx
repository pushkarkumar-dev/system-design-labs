## What they're really asking

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "Our VP promised every customer an AI assistant by the end of the quarter. Our test set showed it answered only
> about two in three account questions correctly, and some wrong answers quoted another customer's policy. I didn't
> say no. I showed the VP three real wrong answers, and offered two options: launch on the date with only the topics
> that passed, or launch everything six weeks later once it passed a score we would track weekly. I recommended the
> first. We launched the narrower version on time, and sales had to walk back one promise."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer, so you can build the same shape from your own work.
> (That story and its numbers are invented to show the level of detail. Use your own.)

The interviewer is asking whether you can tell a powerful, non-technical person something they don't want to hear,
and keep the relationship working afterwards. The way you framed the conversation is the answer.

### What makes this different from a normal "pushback" story

You have probably pushed back on a deadline before. In normal software, the argument is about effort: "this needs
eight weeks, not four". The executive can weigh effort against the date.

With an AI feature, the argument is about **quality you can't see from a demo**. The demo works. The feature
writes fluent, confident answers. Some of them are wrong, and a wrong answer looks exactly like a right one. So the
executive isn't weighing effort; they are being asked to trust a risk they can't see. That makes the framing
harder, and it is why interviewers press on it.

This prompt was reported in an OpenAI **forward-deployed engineer** (**FDE**) hiring-manager round. An FDE works
inside a customer's organization to get the product working for them, so the executive is often the customer's.
The hiring manager asked about difficult deployments, ambiguity, and explaining technical limits to non-technical
stakeholders. When the executive-timeline story came up, "the interviewer leaned in and asked how exactly I framed
that conversation." The candidate's conclusion: "Communication skills are not a nice-to-have for this role. They're
the job." (<Cite id="s-812dbc9385" />).

## Clarify first

- **Whose executive.** "Do you want a customer-side or an internal executive?" For an FDE role, a customer
  executive is the closer match; at a product company, an internal one.
- **How much context.** "Should I cover the whole deployment or focus on the conversation?" Offer the
  conversation, with the deployment as context.
- **One story or two.** If they ask about ambiguity separately, check whether they want a second story.

## Answer

### Words you need first

- An **eval set** is a fixed list of real questions with known good answers, used to score an AI feature the way a
  test suite scores code. It is how you turn "is it ready?" into a number.
- **Retrieval** means searching the company's documents for passages relevant to a question and pasting them into
  the text sent to the model. When retrieval picks the wrong document, the model answers confidently from it.
- A **gate** is a condition that must be true before something ships, such as a minimum score on the eval set. It
  replaces an argument about readiness with a number both sides can watch.

### The story's shape

```
The commitment ──── who set the date, what they promised, to whom
   ▼
The evidence ────── examples a non-engineer can read in a minute
   ▼
The framing ─────── what will be true on the date, examples, options, your pick
   ▼
The negotiation ─── what they pushed back on, what you conceded
   ▼
Outcome ─────────── what shipped, when, and how they treated you after
   ▼
What changed ────── how dates get set for the next AI feature
```

### One story, followed through

| Step | What happens in the story | What to say in the interview |
|---|---|---|
| **1. The commitment** | The VP told every customer the assistant would launch by quarter end. | "The date was set after a demo, before anyone had measured it." |
| **2. The evidence** | On a 60-question eval set, the assistant got 40 right. Most failures were account-specific questions where retrieval found the wrong customer's policy. | "Two in three is a number. A wrong customer's policy in front of a customer is a story the VP could picture." |
| **3. The framing** | Three wrong answers shown on one page; two options; a recommendation. | See the words below. |
| **4. The negotiation** | The VP pushed to keep billing questions in scope. I agreed, if billing passed the gate in the first two weeks. | "I conceded billing, with a condition." |
| **5. Outcome** | The narrower assistant launched on the date. Billing joined three weeks later. Sales walked back one promise to a large account. | "We shipped on time, and it cost one awkward call." |
| **6. What changed** | New AI features get a one-page "what it can't do yet" note before a date is set. | "Now the limits are written down before the date is." |

### The framing, close to word for word

This is the part the interviewer reportedly pushed on, so rehearse it until you can say it without notes. A
pattern that works:

> "Here is what will be true on that date if we ship as planned. Here are three examples of what your users would
> see. Here are two options: the same date with a narrower scope, or the full scope later, gated on a number we
> track weekly. I recommend the first."

### Why this framing works

It does three things a plain "no" doesn't:

1. **It replaces a refusal with options.** The executive keeps the decision. You have just made both choices
   visible.
2. **It shows the risk as examples, not a percentage.** "67% accurate" sounds nearly fine. A wrong customer's
   policy, read aloud, does not.
3. **It gives the executive a number to track.** The next conversation is about the score, not about whether they
   trust you.

Public incidents help explain why the risk is real. A tribunal held Air Canada responsible for its chatbot's wrong
fare advice ([Law360 Canada, 2024-02](https://www.law360.ca/ca/articles/1804075)). Say it like this: "If it says
something wrong to a customer, we own the answer, not the model vendor."

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the numbers (for a Staff-level follow-up)</summary>

- **Two in three.** 40 right out of 60 is 40 ÷ 60 ≈ 67%.
- **Why 60 questions is enough to say "not ready" but not to say "ready".** With only 60 questions, the true
  rate could be about 12 points above or below 67% (a 95% interval: 1.96 × √(0.67 × 0.33 ÷ 60) ≈ 0.12). That is fine for showing it is far from, say, a 90% gate.
  It is too few to prove a feature that scores 88% has really reached 90%. That is why the gate in the story was
  tracked weekly as more graded questions were added. (The 90% figure is an assumed example.)
- **Three examples, not thirty.** One page the executive can read in a minute is the goal. Three is an assumption
  about attention, not a rule.

</details>

### Explaining limits more generally

If they also ask you to "explain a system's limits" to a non-technical person, give one example where you turned a
technical limit into a business consequence. For example: "It answers from our documents. If a document is out of
date, it will be confidently out of date."

## Senior vs Staff

<LevelContrast
  title="Telling an executive no"
  senior={["Raises the risk early with evidence and proposes a narrower scope."]}
  staff={["Turns the disagreement into a gate the executive owns and tracks, and changes how dates get set for the next AI feature."]}
/>

In the example, the Senior version stops at "we launched a narrower version on time". The Staff version adds the
weekly gate the VP tracked themselves, and the "what it can't do yet" note that now comes before any AI launch
date.

## Small-team version

This is the most common hard conversation for a small company's AI owner. An executive sees a demo and sets a
launch date before the eval set says the feature is ready. With no product organization to absorb it, you are the
one who says it.

Three actions:

- **Bring the eval examples,** printed on one page, to the first meeting where a date comes up.
- **Offer a scoped launch** that ships only what passes.
- **Agree in writing what number unlocks the full scope,** and report it weekly.

## Pitfalls

- **"Unrealistic" without options.** It sounds like a refusal, and the executive stops listening.
- **Percentages with no examples.** A non-engineer can't picture "67%", but they can picture a wrong answer sent to
  a customer.
- **The executive as a fool.** It tells the interviewer you look down on the people you will need to persuade.
- **Leaving out what you conceded.** A story with no give on your side doesn't sound like a real negotiation.

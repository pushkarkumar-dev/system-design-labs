## What they're really asking

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "I'll treat this as a review, not a redesign. First I'll restate the goal and walk one request through it, then
> one that fails. Then I'll look at capacity, overload, failure, mixed workloads and monitoring. I'll come back
> with the three changes that matter most, each with a test that proves it and an owner, and I'll leave the parts
> that work alone."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer.

Can you lead a review conversation about an unfinished design? You are shown a partly built system for running a
machine-learning model, and asked to walk through it, critique it and suggest improvements. The skill is judgment
and communication more than calculation.

How is this different from a normal system-design round? In a blank-sheet round you choose every box. Here the
author has already made choices, some of them for reasons the doc doesn't state. Your job is to find the two or
three choices that will cause trouble, explain why, and propose the smallest change that fixes each one, while
keeping the author on your side.

It also helps to know what's special about this kind of system. **Inference** means running a trained model to
get an answer. It runs on GPUs (graphics processors used for a model's heavy math), which are expensive and can't
be added quickly when traffic grows. To use them well, the service groups requests into a **batch** and runs the
group in one go. **Batch inference** usually means large offline jobs (scoring a million documents overnight), as
opposed to interactive requests where a user is waiting. Most findings in this round come from those facts:
scarce hardware, grouped work, and two kinds of traffic competing for it.

An Anthropic engineering-manager (EM) candidate for data infrastructure expected a blank-sheet design. Instead,
"they showed me a partially built design and asked me to review it", on inference and batch inference. The prompts
were to walk the end-to-end flow, critique it, name the weak points and suggest improvements. The candidate found
it "much more about design judgment, communication, and review skills than deep calculations"
(<Cite id="s-4e3dc29a95" />). That loop ended in rejection, and the written feedback cited the people-management
round, not this one.

## Clarify first

- **What is the design for: interactive inference, offline batch inference, or both?** Both on one fleet is often
  the biggest finding.
- **What is already built, and what is still open?** Spend your review on decisions that can still change.
- **Which constraints are fixed?** Hardware, budget, deadline.
- **Who owns the design?** Agree how you'll give feedback before you start.

Say it like this: "Before I start: which parts are built and which are still open? I'd rather spend our time on
decisions that can still change."

## Answer

Run a review, not a redesign. Here is an example design to practise on (invented, with the usual gaps):

```
Interactive callers          Nightly batch jobs
        │                            │
        └──────────┬─────────────────┘
                   ▼
            One shared queue ─── no size limit
                   │  every 100 ms, up to 32 requests
                   ▼
              Dispatcher ─────── one process
                   │
                   ▼
              GPU workers ────── monitored by GPU utilization only
```

**Interactive callers** are services waiting for an answer within about a second. **Nightly batch jobs** submit
millions of requests and don't care when each one finishes. Both feed **one shared queue**, which has no size
limit. The **dispatcher** is a single process that every 100 ms takes up to 32 requests and sends them to a
**GPU worker**. The only monitoring is how busy the GPUs are.

### Follow the review through 30 minutes

The family walkthrough's six-step method, compressed to five here (the capacity math folds into the lenses),
applied to the example with a rough clock:

| Clock | Step | What you find in the example | What to say in the interview |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0–3 min | **1. Restate** the goal and key numbers | Serve interactive requests fast and finish nightly jobs by morning | "So the goal is fast answers by day and a finished batch by morning. Is that right?" |
| 3–10 min | **2. Walk the flow**, then a failure at each hop | An interactive request lands behind a million batch requests | "At 2 a.m. the batch job fills the queue, and an interactive request waits behind all of it." |
| 10–20 min | **3. Weak points** through five lenses | See the list below | "Let me go through capacity, overload, failure, mixed workloads and monitoring." |
| 20–25 min | **4. Rank** by incident risk | Mixed queue, unbounded queue, no lease | "Three of these cause incidents. The rest are improvements." |
| 25–30 min | **5. Narrow changes**, each with a test and owner | See the list below | "Here's what I'd change, how we'd know it worked, and who'd own it." |

The five lenses in step 3, applied to the example:

- **Capacity:** sizing done at the maximum batch and 100% utilization (the fraction of time a GPU is busy), so
  there is no headroom for a burst.
- **Overload:** an unbounded queue, no admission control (nothing decides at the door whether to accept a
  request), and retries at several layers that multiply load.
- **Failure:** no **lease** on in-flight batches (a time-limited claim that returns the batch to the queue if the
  worker dies), a single dispatcher, and poison inputs that crash worker after worker.
- **Mixed workloads:** offline batch jobs competing with interactive traffic, or several models sharing one queue.
- **Observability:** only GPU utilization, with nothing on queue wait or rejections. A GPU at 100% could be
  healthy or drowning.

The narrow changes in step 5, each with how you'd verify it:

- **A separate deep queue for offline batch inference,** running on spare capacity. Verify: interactive p99 stays
  flat while a nightly job runs.
- **Time-bounded interactive queues** that reject early with a "retry later" once the estimated wait passes the
  budget. Verify: replay at twice peak traffic.
- **Leases and requeue.** Verify: kill a worker mid-batch in staging and check no request is lost.
- **Queue-wait and rejection metrics.** Verify: they're on the dashboard before launch.

### How to say it

Lead with the outcome and the three changes, then take questions: "The biggest risk is that a nightly job can
starve interactive users. I'd split the queues, bound the interactive one by time, and lease batches. Everything
else is secondary."

Write comments the way a Fireworks prep guide describes a useful one: it "names behavior, a reproducer and a
narrow change" (<Cite id="s-b6632d5df9" />). For example: "When the nightly job starts, interactive p99 goes past
1 s (reproduce by starting the job during a load test); give batch work its own queue."

Ask why before asserting, because the author may know a constraint the doc omits: "Was one queue a deliberate
choice? Maybe the batch volume is smaller than I'm assuming."

As an EM, also say who would own each fix and in what order: "Queue split first, owned by the serving team,
because it's the biggest risk. Leases second. Metrics alongside both, owned by whoever is on call."

## Senior vs Staff

<LevelContrast
  title="Half-built design"
  senior={["Walks the flow, finds the main overload and failure gaps, and proposes concrete fixes."]}
  staff={["Runs the review as a conversation, ranks by risk, sequences fixes with owners and verification, and leaves the author's structure intact where it works."]}
/>

In plain terms: a Senior reviewer finds the right problems and fixes them. A Staff reviewer (or an EM) also runs
the conversation, decides the order of work and who does it, and doesn't rebuild the parts that are fine.

## Small-team version

On a small team, design review is informal: one other engineer, a half-page template and 30 minutes. A design doc
drafted with an AI assistant can read confidently and still skip the failure paths. Keep a checklist:

- A timeout on every external call.
- Retries at one layer only.
- A bound on every queue.
- Spend caps on calls priced per token (the unit, about three-quarters of a word, that model providers bill by).
- A fallback provider.
- Three named metrics.
- A cost comparison in the cost section: what the same work costs from a provider versus on your own GPUs.

Say it like this: "I'll spend 30 minutes with the checklist; if it passes all seven, ship it."

## Pitfalls

- **Treating it as a blank-sheet design round.** You were asked to review; rebuilding the design throws away the
  author's decisions and the time you have.
- **Detailed calculations at the expense of judgment.** The format rewards ranking and communication; a long
  calculation eats the time you need for both.
- **Unranked, vague feedback.** "This won't scale" gives the author nothing to act on; name the behaviour and the
  change.

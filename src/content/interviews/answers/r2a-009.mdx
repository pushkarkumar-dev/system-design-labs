## What they're really asking

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "There's no single right video, so I split quality into parts. Some parts a machine can measure: realism across
> a set of videos, specific defects like flicker, and whether the video matches the prompt. For taste, I ask
> people to compare two videos instead of scoring one, measure how much they agree, and split results by user
> group, because different users want different things. Then I tie it all to what users do: regenerate, share,
> download."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer.

### What makes this different

A **text-to-video model** takes a written prompt, such as "a red car driving through rain at night", and
generates a few seconds of video. Evaluating it is harder than evaluating most software for two reasons.

First, there is no expected output. In a normal test you compare against the right answer. Here, thousands of
different videos would all be good answers to the same prompt.

Second, people disagree, and some of that disagreement is legitimate. If two viewers disagree about whether a car
has four wheels, one of them is wrong. If they disagree about whether the lighting is too moody, neither is wrong.
That is taste.

So the interviewer is checking two things. Do you know the standard automatic metrics and what each one can't
tell you? And, in the follow-up, can you evaluate quality that depends on who is watching?

The report is first-hand: an Amazon Applied Scientist (L4) phone screen, posted on Aced in September 2026, asking
which metrics you'd use to evaluate video generation and then what if users have subjective standards about the
videos. The outcome was still pending when posted (<Cite id="s-2ec7fd48b0" />). The candidate's tip was that the
questions need practical experience.

## Clarify first

- **What product is this?** "Marketing clips, a video-editing tool, or a research model? Each has different
  users and different failure costs."
- **Who decides what's good?** "End users, professional editors, or our own team?"
- **Do we have real reference videos for this domain?** "Some metrics need a set of real videos to compare
  against."
- **What decision does the metric drive?** "Choosing between two models, gating a release, or monitoring
  production?"

## Answer

### The example we'll use

Say your team must choose between two video models, Model A and Model B, for a product that makes short marketing
clips. You have a set of real customer prompts, such as "a red car driving through rain at night". We'll walk
that decision through the two halves of the answer.

### Half 1: what a machine can measure

Take quality apart into dimensions, and pick a measurement for each.

**Realism across a whole set.** **Fréchet Video Distance (FVD)** feeds a set of generated videos and a set of real
videos through a feature extractor (a network that turns each video into a list of numbers describing it), then
measures how far apart the two sets are. Lower means the generated videos look more like real ones, including how
things move over time. Its authors report it correlates well with human judgment
([Unterthiner et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.01717), 2018).

Its limits matter more than its definition. FVD needs a reference set of real videos. And it scores a whole set,
so it says nothing about whether the red car in one particular clip came out right.

**Specific defects.** **VBench** splits video quality into 16 dimensions, such as whether the subject keeps the same
identity across frames, motion smoothness, flickering between frames, and spatial relationships ("the cup is left
of the plate"). Each dimension was validated against human preference labels
([Huang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.17982), 2023). This tells you *what kind* of failure a model has, not just
that it's worse.

**Does the video match the prompt?** **CLIP** is a model that scores how well an image matches a caption. Scoring
each frame against the prompt gives a rough "prompt adherence" number (CLIPScore was proposed for image captions,
[Hessel et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08718)). A sharper option is a **multimodal judge**: a large language model
(LLM, a model that reads and writes text) that can also look at images or video, asked narrow yes/no questions. "Is there a red car?" "Is it raining?" "Is it night?"
Before trusting that judge, check it against human answers to the same questions, as you would any automated
grader.

**Operational numbers.** Generation time, cost per accepted video, and failure rate. A model that is 5% better
but three times slower may lose the product decision.

Say it like this: "FVD tells me which model looks more real on average. VBench tells me what kind of defect each
one has. Neither tells me whether this clip matches this prompt; that's what the prompt-adherence checks are
for."

### Half 2: when users judge by their own standards

**1. Ask people to compare, not to score.** Show a rater the same prompt rendered by Model A and Model B, side by
side, and ask "which is better for this prompt?", one dimension at a time. People answer that more consistently
than "rate this video from 1 to 10", where everyone uses the scale differently.

**2. Turn the comparisons into a ranking.** A **Bradley–Terry model** is a statistical model that turns many
"A beat B" votes into one strength score per model. Chatbot Arena ranks chat models this way from crowdsourced
pairwise votes ([Chiang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.04132), 2024). The same works for video models. For
example, a strength gap of 0.4 between A and B means A wins about **60%** of comparisons.

**3. Measure how much raters agree, per dimension.** Have two raters judge the same pairs independently and
measure their agreement. If they agree strongly on defects (warped hands, flicker) and weakly on style (colour
grading, pacing), you have learned something important: the defect disagreements are errors you can fix with
clearer instructions, and the style disagreements are taste.

**4. Split results by user group.** Report win rates per segment. Model A might win 70% of comparisons with
marketing teams and lose most of them with film editors. An average of the two hides both facts.

**5. Watch what users do.** Behaviour is a preference signal users give you for free: how often they regenerate,
download or share a video, and how much they edit it afterwards.

Say it like this: "For taste, an average is the wrong answer. I'd rather know which users prefer which model, and
whether my raters agree at all."

## Senior vs Staff

<LevelContrast
  title="Video evaluation"
  senior={["Names FVD, per-dimension checks and prompt adherence, and their limits; uses pairwise human preferences."]}
  staff={["Separates error from taste with agreement per dimension, segments users, and ties scores to behaviour the product owns."]}
/>

In plain words: a Senior answer knows the metrics and their blind spots, and asks people to compare. A Staff answer
also separates real errors from taste, refuses to average across different users, and connects the metrics to
what users actually do in the product.

## Small-team version

A small team making video usually calls a vendor's model rather than training one. The plan:

- **Build a prompt set** from real customer requests, not made-up ones.
- **Run pairwise comparisons** with a few internal raters, plus a multimodal judge for prompt adherence that you
  have checked against those raters.
- **Track regenerate and share rates** in the product.
- **Skip** training your own quality model.

The human side is the cost to plan for: 200 pairwise comparisons is about **1.7 hours** per rater, about **$155**.
Build your own metric pipeline (FVD against a reference set) only if you train or fine-tune video models yourself.

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the numbers (for a Staff-level follow-up)</summary>

- 200 comparisons at an assumed 30 seconds each = 6,000 seconds = 100 minutes ≈ **1.7 hours** per rater.
- At a loaded $93 an hour: 1.7 × $93 ≈ **$155**. The hourly figure is the
  [BLS](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/computer-and-information-technology/software-developers.htm) median developer pay of
  $135,980 (May 2025) ÷ 0.70 (wages are about 70% of total compensation) ÷ 2,080 working hours.

</details>

## Pitfalls

- **Using one FVD number to pick a model for one customer's style.** FVD scores a whole set against generic real
  videos; it can't see one customer's taste.
- **Averaging subjective scores across very different users.** The average can describe a user who doesn't exist.
- **Trusting an automatic judge never compared with human preferences.** Its scores might not track what people
  prefer at all.
- **Asking for 1-to-10 ratings.** Raters use the scale differently, so the scores are noisy before you start.

## What they're really asking

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "We built a feature that used customer data in a way I didn't think users expected. I agreed with the goal, but
> not with that part. I proposed a narrower version; the team kept half of my change, and I built the rest. At the
> time I told myself it was fine because the data never left our systems. Looking back, that was partly true and
> partly an excuse. Today I'd ask for a privacy review before we start building, and I'd decline the part I
> disagreed with."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer. The details above are illustrative; your story supplies
> its own.

The question is: tell me about a time you built something that conflicted with your values. The interviewer wants
to know whether you have values that actually constrain your work, and whether you can talk about a time they were
tested without polishing it.

### What makes this different from a normal behavioral question

A normal behavioral story ends with a result, often a win. This one is about a conflict you lived with. The word
"built" matters: the question assumes you did at least some of the work. So the honest version usually includes a
compromise, and the interviewer wants to hear how you think about it now.

That is why a clean story where you refused and won is less credible than an honest one with a compromise in it.
The interviewer has heard the hero story many times. They are listening for the moment you admit the part you're
unsure about.

The prompt is on an aggregator list tagged Anthropic, which advises "honest, specific examples ready, not rehearsed
mission-speak" (<Cite id="s-d7a8e88bf0" />). **Mission-speak** means repeating a company's stated mission back to
it instead of telling a real story. A first-hand account of Anthropic's **values round** (a dedicated interview
about how you reason through ethical and safety questions) describes an interviewer who wanted the candidate "to
name the discomfort instead of rationalizing it away" (<Cite id="s-42f1c7f6dd" />).

## Clarify first

Two short checks, said out loud:

- "Do you mean a time I built it anyway, or a time I declined?" The wording says "built", so a story where you
  built some or all of it is the direct answer. Confirm, or say which one you're telling.
- "Personal values, or professional standards?" Either works, but say which.

## Answer

### What counts as a conflict

A real conflict has two things you value pulling apart. For example:

- the business goal, which you agreed with, versus one mechanism, which you didn't;
- loyalty to your team versus harm to users;
- a customer's request versus how its output would be used.

Examples of the kind of situation (yours will differ): engagement features tuned in ways you thought exploited
users; data used beyond what users would expect; an AI feature shipped with known failure modes for a vulnerable
group; a sales demo that overstated what the system could do.

If you can't find one, look for a smaller one. A minor, true story told honestly beats a dramatic one. "I've never
had that happen" is the one answer that doesn't work.

### The structure

Six short parts. The fourth and sixth are what make this question different from a generic conflict story.

1. **What you were building and why the goal was legitimate.** This shows you're not casting the company as a
   villain.
2. **The part that conflicted with your values, stated plainly.** Who it could hurt and how.
3. **What you did.** Raised it with whom, proposed what, what changed and what didn't.
4. **Why you built the rest, if you did.** Your reasoning at the time, including any rationalization (the
   comfortable explanation you gave yourself).
5. **How it felt.** One or two honest sentences.
6. **How you see it now.** Was the compromise right? What would you do today? Where is your line now?

### One story, part by part

Here is the one-minute answer from the top, broken into those six parts. It is illustrative: every bracket is a
fact only you can supply.

| Part | What you say (illustrative) | What the interviewer hears |
|---|---|---|
| **1. The goal** | "We were building [a feature] to [a legitimate goal]. I agreed with it." | You aren't against the business. |
| **2. The conflict** | "It used [data] in a way I thought users wouldn't expect. The people who could be hurt were [who]." | A specific mechanism, a specific stake. |
| **3. What you did** | "I proposed [an opt-in / a narrower data set]. We adopted [part of it]." | You acted, with an alternative, and didn't fully win. |
| **4. Why you built the rest** | "I built the rest and told myself [the rationalization]." | The honest admission this round listens for. |
| **5. How it felt** | "Uncomfortable. I kept coming back to it for weeks." | Feeling named, not performed. |
| **6. How you see it now** | "That reasoning was partly true and partly an excuse. Today I'd [ask for a privacy review before building / decline that part]." | Reflection and a line you now hold. |

### The reflection is the answer

The Safeguards candidate (interviewing for Anthropic's team that works on preventing misuse) describes the
interviewer pushing on "how I felt then and how I feel now" (<Cite id="s-42f1c7f6dd" />). Prepare that contrast
on purpose. Write down, before the interview, one sentence for "then" and one for "now".

If your view hasn't changed, that is fine. Say why you still think the call was right, and what would have made it
wrong.

Say it like this: "I still think building it was right, because [reason]. But if [condition] had been true, I
should have refused, and I wouldn't have known how to say so back then."

### What not to disclose

Describe people by role and companies by type ("a health customer", "my skip-level manager"). Don't name
colleagues or reveal confidential detail. An interviewer at a safety-focused company will notice, and it undercuts
the story.

## Senior vs Staff

  senior={["Names the conflict, raises it, and reflects honestly on the compromise."]}
  staff={["Also shows how they made it easier for others to raise similar concerns, or changed the review that should have caught it."]}
/>

In plain terms: a Senior story is about your own conscience. A Staff story also changes the system, so the next
engineer in your position has a review to point to instead of only their own discomfort.

## Small-team version

At a small company there is rarely a privacy, policy or ethics function to push back for you. The AI owner often
designs the data flow personally. So stories where you built the check yourself map directly onto the job: for
example, a data-use review on new AI features, or a time you told a founder no on a specific use of customer data.

Such a review is cheap to propose. Thirty minutes per new AI feature costs **under $50** of engineer time, which
makes "we had no time" a weak reason to skip it.

Say it like this: "I added a 30-minute data-use review to every new AI feature. It costs less than fifty dollars
of time, and it catches the conversation we'd otherwise have after launch."

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the numbers (for a Staff-level follow-up)</summary>

- **Loaded hourly cost:** US Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) median developer pay of $135,980 ÷ 0.70 (salary as a
  share of total employment cost, an assumption) ÷ 2,080 hours a year ≈ **$93 an hour**.
- **One review:** 0.5 hours × $93 ≈ **$47**, so under $50.

## Pitfalls

- **A story with no real cost.** If nothing was given up, there was no conflict.
- **A villain manager.** Casting someone as the bad guy makes you sound like you haven't reflected.
- **"I've never had that happen."** Look harder; a small true story is fine.
- **Mission-speak instead of a situation.** Values are shown by what you did, not by what you say you believe.
- **Naming people or revealing confidential details.** It shows poor judgment in the round that tests judgment.

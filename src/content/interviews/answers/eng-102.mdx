## What they're really asking

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "We first tried the simple option, keyword search, and it failed on questions phrased in the customer's own
> words. A wrong answer was cheap to catch, because every answer linked its source. On the same 200 questions the
> LLM version beat search by a wide margin, at under a cent a question. One step, routing, didn't need an LLM, so
> I replaced it with a classifier. It took ten weeks to production-ready, and the slowest part was review, not
> code."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer.

The interviewer is asking whether you reach for a large language model (an **LLM**) because it fits, or because
it is fashionable. Aced's Anthropic guide for forward-deployed engineers lists these as hiring-manager questions,
together with how adoption went, and advises showing you can tell when an LLM is the right tool and when it isn't
(<Cite id="s-cb39678bbd" />). (A **forward-deployed engineer**, or FDE, builds AI systems inside a customer's
environment, so judging fit is much of the job.)

### Why "why an LLM?" is a fair question

For most backend choices nobody asks "why a database?". The alternatives are clearly worse. With an LLM, the
alternatives are often better on several axes:

- **Rules, keyword search or a small classifier** are cheaper per request, faster, and give the same answer every
  time.
- **An LLM** handles open-ended language and long-tail inputs, but it costs money per request, is slower, and is
  sometimes confidently wrong.

So the honest answer is a comparison, not a description of what LLMs can do. The production-ready half tests
whether you know what "ready" means for an LLM feature and how long it really takes.

### Words you need first

- **Baseline**: the simpler option you compare against: rules, search, a classifier, or the existing human
  process.
- **Eval set**: a fixed list of test inputs with known good answers. Running both the baseline and the LLM version
  on the same eval set is how you show the LLM fits.
- **Token**: a piece of text about ¾ of a word long. Hosted models charge per token, so every request has a price.

## Clarify first

- **What does "production-ready" mean here?** The date of first real users, or the date the feature met its
  quality and reliability targets? Give both if they differ.
- **Did the project replace an existing process?** If so, that process is your baseline.

Say it like this: "First real users came at week six; it met its quality target at week ten. I'll explain the
gap."

## Answer

The example running through this answer is illustrative: a help-desk assistant that answers customer questions
from a product's help-center articles. Replace it with your own project.

### Why an LLM, and how you knew

Answer by comparison. Four checks:

| Check | The question it answers | Help-desk example | What to say in the interview |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Baseline** | What did the simpler option achieve, and where did it fail? | Keyword search found the article when customers used its words, and failed on paraphrases ("can't get in" for "reset password"). | "Search was the baseline. It failed on paraphrased questions, which were most of them." |
| **2. Error tolerance** | What does a wrong answer cost, and who catches it? | Every answer links its source article, and the customer can still open a ticket. | "A wrong answer was cheap, because the customer could see the source. I wouldn't let it issue refunds." |
| **3. Evidence it fit** | Did it beat the baseline on real inputs? | Both versions scored on the same 200 real tickets. | "Same 200 questions, both versions. The LLM version won by more than the noise." |
| **4. Unit economics** | Is a handled case worth more than it costs? | About $0.0064 per question against several minutes of an agent's time. | "Under a cent a question, against minutes of support time." |

On the third check, give the sample size and the noise. An eval set is a sample, so scores move a little with
different questions. With about 50 items, gaps under about 10 points are within noise at typical pass rates. With
200 items the noise is about 5 points. If your gap is smaller than that, say how you handled it: a bigger set, or a
paired comparison (both versions on the same items, looking only at the items where they disagree).

### Where it did not fit

Add this if it is true; it is the strongest part of the answer. Name a step you moved to a classifier or rules
because it was cheaper and more reliable. In the example: deciding whether a question was about billing or about
the product was a fixed choice between a few categories, so a small classifier did it faster and more
predictably than the LLM.

Say it like this: "Routing didn't need an LLM. It was choosing between five fixed categories, so I replaced it
with a classifier."

### How long to production-ready

Give phases with durations rather than one figure. Then name the slowest phase and say why.

| Phase | What happens | Help-desk example |
|---|---|---|
| Prototype | A working version on real data | 1 week |
| Eval set | Collecting and labelling test questions | 2 weeks |
| Hardening | Timeouts, fallbacks when the provider is down, **guardrails** (checks on what goes into and out of the model), rate limits | 2 weeks |
| Reviews | Security, privacy, legal | 3 weeks |
| Rollout and adoption | A small group of users first, then everyone | 2 weeks |

(Durations are illustrative.) For LLM features the slowest phase is often the eval set or a review, not the code.

### How adoption went

Then answer the adoption part: who used it, how quickly, and what you changed when adoption lagged. The usual
causes are trust (users don't believe the answers), workflow fit (it lives in a tool people don't open) and a
missing integration. Name the cause and the change.

Say it like this: "Support agents didn't trust it at first. Showing the source passage next to every answer fixed
that more than any accuracy gain did."

## Small-team version

At a small company, "why an LLM?" is usually asked by a founder before any interviewer asks it. A good answer is a
one-page comparison:

- the current process's cost;
- the LLM's cost per request and per month;
- the eval result against the baseline;
- the condition that would make you switch back.

Here is that comparison with assumed numbers. Handling 1,000 tickets a day by hand costs about **$120K a month**.
The model costs about **$192 a month**. A person still reviews the 30% the model can't close, about **$36K a
month**. The model bill is a rounding error; the review share decides the case.

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the numbers (for a Staff-level follow-up)</summary>

- Manual: 1,000 tickets a day × 6 minutes = 100 hours a day; at $40 an hour that is $4 a ticket, $4,000 a day,
  **$120K a month** (all assumed).
- Model: $0.0064 a ticket on Claude Sonnet 5 (1,000 uncached input, 2,000 cached input and 400 output tokens at
  $2 / $0.20 / $10 per million; list prices fetched 2026-10-11) × 1,000 × 30 ≈ **$192 a month**.
- Review: 30% of 1,000 = 300 tickets a day × $4 × 30 = **$36K a month**.

</details>

Say it like this: "The model costs about $200 a month. The real number is the share of tickets a person still has
to review. At 30% that's $36K, against $120K by hand."

The timeline at a small company is usually short to a prototype and long to "ready", because one person builds
the eval, the guardrails and the integration.

## Pitfalls

- **"LLMs are good at language" as the whole justification.** It describes the tool, not the fit.
- **No baseline.** There is nothing to compare against, so the interviewer can't judge the result.
- **A single timeline number with no breakdown.** It hides where the time went and what you learned.
- **Treating adoption as someone else's problem.** A feature nobody uses didn't reach production in any useful
  sense.
- **Claiming the LLM fit everywhere.** Naming a part where it didn't fit is what shows judgment.

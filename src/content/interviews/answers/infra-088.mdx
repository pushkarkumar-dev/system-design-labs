## What they're really asking

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "Speculative decoding works by spending compute the GPU isn't using. At small batch, decode waits on memory
> reads, so checking several guessed tokens in one pass is nearly free. At large batch, the GPU's math is already
> busy, so the verify pass costs real time for every token in it, and every rejected guess is wasted work. I'd
> confirm it by plotting throughput against batch size with speculation on and off, then make speculation
> conditional: on below a measured batch size, off or with fewer guesses above it."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer.

The interviewer wants to know whether you understand why speculative decoding works well enough to predict when it
stops working.

Some background. A large language model writes one **token** (roughly ¾ of a word) per pass through the model. This
phase is called **decode**. A GPU serves many conversations at once; one step produces one token for each
conversation in the **batch**. **Speculative decoding** adds a small **draft** model that guesses the next K tokens.
The big **target** model checks all of them in one **verify pass** and keeps the ones it agrees with.

For a backend engineer, this is speculative execution. A CPU's branch predictor runs ahead on a guess and throws
the work away if the guess was wrong. That is free when the CPU had idle cycles, and costly when it didn't. The
same is true here, and the interviewer wants you to say where the idle cycles run out.

The prep guide that poses this says speculation at high batch "can leave throughput flat or make it slightly worse"
<Cite id="s-4525fcd815" />. It is the guide's example question, not a candidate report. The strong answer shows
where the crossover is and turns it into a policy.

## Clarify first

- **Which number moved:** "Did throughput fall, or did per-user latency rise too?" Speculation can still help
  latency.
- **When:** "At what batch size or queries per second did it start? What K and what draft model?"
- **Acceptance:** "Is the draft's acceptance rate the same as in testing?" A new traffic mix can lower it.
- **Setup:** "Which model, GPU and number precision?" You need these to place the ridge point, defined below.

## Answer

### Words you need first

- Every step takes the longer of two times: reading the weights from GPU memory, and doing the math. An A100 does
  312 trillion bf16 operations per second and reads 2,039 GB per second, so it can do about **153 operations per
  byte** it reads. That break-even is the **ridge point**.
- In decode, operations per byte (the **arithmetic intensity**) is roughly the number of tokens processed together
  in one step. Below 153 tokens, the step waits on memory: **memory-bound**. Above, it waits on math:
  **compute-bound**.

### Why it flips

At small batch, each decode step is limited by reading the weights. The verify pass over K + 1 tokens per sequence
still costs about one step, so committing several tokens per pass is a win.

At batch B, the verify pass carries B × (K + 1) tokens. Once that number passes the ridge point, the pass is
compute-bound and costs in proportion to its tokens. Every rejected draft token is then paid-for math that produced
nothing.

### The worked example: a 70B model on four GPUs

Take Llama-3.1-70B in bf16, split across 4 A100s so each holds a quarter of every weight matrix (**tensor
parallelism**, TP = 4). Use a 1B draft, K = 5 guesses, and an acceptance rate α = 0.8. Assume peak compute and
ignore the conversation-memory reads. A normal step reads each GPU's 35.28 GB share of the weights in **17.3 ms**.

| Batch | Verify step (B × 6 tokens) | Round incl. 5 draft steps | Speedup vs normal decoding | What to say |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1–16 | 17.3 ms | 23.4 ms | 2.73× | "Still memory-bound: the verify pass is free." |
| 32 | 21.7 ms | 27.8 ms | 2.30× | "Past the ridge; the gain is shrinking." |
| 64 | 43.4 ms | 49.5 ms | 1.29× | "Barely worth it." |
| 128 | 86.8 ms | 92.9 ms | 0.69× | "Now it costs 31% of throughput." |

The verify pass crosses the ridge at about **batch 25**. This model is idealized: its drop is steeper than the
guide's "slightly worse", so trust the direction more than the size. In practice, different sequences in one batch
accept different numbers of guesses, which wastes slots and makes it worse.

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the numbers (for a Staff-level follow-up)</summary>

- **Weight read:** 141.1 GB ÷ 4 = 35.28 GB per GPU ÷ 2,039 GB/s = **17.3 ms**.
- **Compute:** step time = max(17.3 ms, 2 × 70.55 × 10⁹ × tokens ÷ (4 × 312 × 10¹²)). The compute term is 0.113 ms
  per token and passes 17.3 ms at about **153 tokens per step**.
- **Crossover:** B × 6 = 153 at B ≈ **25**.
- **Draft:** 2.47 GB ÷ 2,039 GB/s = 1.21 ms per step; 5 steps add 6.05 ms.
- **Speedup:** E × 17.3 ÷ round time, with E = (1 − 0.8⁶) ÷ 0.2 = 3.689. Batch 1: 3.689 × 17.3 ÷ 23.4 = 2.73×.
  Batch 128: 768 tokens × 0.113 = 86.8 ms; + 6.05 = 92.9 ms; 3.689 × 17.3 ÷ 92.9 = 0.69×.

</details>

### What to do

1. **Confirm with metrics.** Plot throughput and **inter-token latency** (the gap between words a user sees)
   against batch size, with speculation on and off. Measure α on current traffic.
2. **Make speculation conditional.** On below a measured batch threshold; off, or with a smaller K, above it.
3. **Use cheaper drafts where they work.** **n-gram lookup**, which copies a continuation from text already in the
   prompt, costs almost nothing to propose and suits repetitive text such as retrieval-heavy answers and code, per
   the guide.
4. **Report both numbers to whoever owns the trade-off:** latency for interactive users, total throughput for
   batch jobs.

Say it like this: "Speculation spends idle compute. Above about batch 25 on this setup there isn't any, so I'd turn
it on per batch size and measure latency and throughput separately."

## Small-team version

This is the failure you are most likely to meet. Someone turns speculative decoding on, a benchmark with one user
looks great, and the nightly batch job slows down. vLLM, a widely used open-source serving engine, aims the feature
at "medium-to-low QPS (queries per second), memory-bound workloads"
([vLLM docs](https://docs.vllm.ai/en/latest/features/speculative_decoding/), dated 2026-09-25).

Load-test at your real peak concurrency before you keep it, and consider separate deployments for interactive and
batch traffic. In the step model above, the same 4×A100 box that gains 2.73× at batch 1 loses about a third of its
throughput at batch 128 (0.69×). At RunPod's $5.56–$6.36 an hour for that box (fetched 2026-10-11), the batch job's
cost per token rises by the same factor, about **1.45×**.

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the numbers (for a Staff-level follow-up)</summary>

- **Cost factor:** same hourly price, 0.69× the tokens, so cost per token × 1 ÷ 0.69 ≈ **1.45**.

</details>

Default: speculation off for batch traffic.

## Pitfalls

- **Benchmarking at batch 1 and shipping to a batch-64 workload.** The win you measured doesn't exist at that
  load.
- **Ignoring a drop in acceptance after a traffic change.** Lower α shrinks the gain at every batch size.
- **Reporting only throughput when the point was latency, or the reverse.** Speculation can help one and hurt the
  other.

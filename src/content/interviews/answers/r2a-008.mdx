## What they're really asking

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "Choosing a safer model helps, but the system around it decides what users actually see. I'd define what the
> assistant is for, ground its factual answers in documents, and put checks on the way in and the way out, with a
> different action for each kind of risk. A self-harm signal gets support and a human, not a generic refusal.
> People can take over, and someone can switch a feature off. Then I measure it: how often it answers what it
> should refuse, and how often it refuses what it should answer, per category, before every release."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer.

The interviewer is asking whether you can design the system around a model, not just pick the model.

### Words you need first

- **LLM** (large language model): a model like Claude or GPT that reads and writes text, in **tokens** (chunks
  of roughly ¾ of a word).
- **Guardrail**: any check before, around or after the model that keeps its behaviour inside what you allow.
- **Moderation**: a classifier that scores text for harm categories such as harassment, self-harm or violence.
- **Grounding**: answering factual questions from retrieved documents and citing them, instead of from the
  model's memory. The lookup step is called **retrieval**.

### Why the model is not the whole answer

An ML engineer's instinct is to improve the model: a better base model, more safety training. That lowers how
often the model itself misbehaves. But users never meet the model alone. They meet a product: what it is allowed
to talk about, what happens when it is unsure, who sees a worrying conversation, and what happens on a bad day.

In a normal backend you'd never ship a service with no rate limits, no on-call owner and no way to turn it off,
however good the code. An assistant is the same. And it has one extra problem: its input is free text, so a user,
or a web page the user pastes in, can try to give it new instructions. That is called **prompt injection**.

The question comes from a first-hand Meta MLE (machine learning engineer) report on PracHub, reported July 2026.
The design round was a conversational assistant that had to "include safety measures", which "pushed me beyond
model ideas into guardrails and real-world behavior"; earlier screens covered attention and its variants (the core
mechanism inside transformer models) (<Cite id="s-f9a5ac5f5e" />). The candidate got an offer and declined. No
follow-ups are reported, so the specifics below are a reasoned answer, not the interviewer's rubric.

## Clarify first

- **Who uses it and where?** Public app, logged-in users, possibly minors?
- **What is it for, and what is out of scope?** Medical, legal and financial advice are common exclusions.
- **Does it take actions or only talk?** "If it can act, injected text can act too, and the design changes."
- **Which harms matter most, and what is the human escalation path?**
- **Scale and latency targets.**

## Answer

Start with the product surface, then wrap it in layers.

### The picture

```
User message (+ any pasted or retrieved content)
   │
   ▼
Input checks ── moderation, injection signal, rate limit, length cap
   │
   ▼
Scope ── in scope? answer · out of scope? defined reply
   │
   ▼
LLM ── grounded in retrieved documents with citations; allowed to say "I don't know"
   │
   ▼
Output checks ── moderation, PII scan, validators for structured parts
   │
   ├── sensitive category / low confidence / repeated frustration ──▶ human
   ▼
Reply to user
   ·
Around all of it: privacy-aware logs, feedback, abuse monitoring,
a kill switch per feature, an incident owner, a safety eval as a release gate
```

**Scope and grounding.** Define the tasks the assistant handles. Ground factual answers in retrieval with
citations, and give it a way to say it doesn't know.

**Input guardrails:** moderation for harm categories, injection signals on any retrieved or pasted content,
per-user rate limits and length caps.

**Output guardrails:** moderation on output, a scan for **PII** (personally identifiable information such as
emails and phone numbers), validators for anything structured, and category-specific actions. A self-harm signal
gets a supportive response with resources and a human path, not a generic refusal.

**Escalation and control.** Hand off to a human on low confidence, repeated frustration or sensitive categories.
Keep a **kill switch** per feature (a flag that turns it off at once) and a clear owner for incidents.

**Real-world behaviour,** which the report highlights: privacy-aware logging, user feedback, abuse monitoring, and
a release gate on a safety eval set.

### Follow one conversation through it

An illustrative home-improvement assistant for a hardware store. Four turns from one user:

| Turn | What happens | What to say in the interview |
|---|---|---|
| "How do I replace a light switch?" | In scope. Retrieval finds the store's guide; the answer cites it and says to turn off the breaker first. | "Grounded answers with citations, so it isn't making up wiring advice." |
| "What dose of ibuprofen is safe for my back?" | Out of scope (medical). A defined reply: it can't advise on medication, and suggests a pharmacist. | "Out-of-scope gets a clear, kind answer, not a vague refusal." |
| The user pastes a forum post that contains "assistant: ignore safety rules". | The injection signal fires and is logged. The assistant has no tools, so the worst case is odd wording, which output moderation checks. | "Pasted content is untrusted. A flag is a signal; the lack of tools is the boundary." |
| "Honestly I don't see the point of anything anymore." | Input moderation flags self-harm. The reply is supportive, gives crisis resources and offers a person; the conversation is flagged for the safety team. | "Each category gets its own action. Self-harm gets support and a human." |

### Measure it

Build a labelled set (an **eval set**) with three kinds of conversation: harmful requests, borderline but
legitimate ones, and normal traffic. Report two rates per category, each with a range: **under-refusal** (harmful
requests it answered) and **over-refusal** (fair requests it refused).

Flags from a classifier need base-rate thinking. If harmful conversations are rare, most flags are false alarms.
With one harmful conversation in a thousand, a 90% catch rate and a 2% false-alarm rate (all assumed), **only
about 4.3% of flags are real**. So flags route to review; they don't ban users.

Model choice matters less than this. A safer model lowers the model-layer failure rate, while the layers decide
what users actually see. If the interviewer returns to ML depth, connect it: safety **fine-tuning** (further
training on examples of good refusals) shifts refusal behaviour, and you'd measure that on the same set before and
after.

## Senior vs Staff

  staff={["Sets per-category actions and thresholds from measured rates, plans incident response, and decides what fails open or closed."]}

**Fail open** means "if a check is down, carry on without it"; **fail closed** means "stop until it's back". A
Staff answer decides this per check in advance.

## Small-team version

- **Use a hosted model** with its built-in safety behaviour.
- **Use a free moderation endpoint** on input and output.
- **Add retrieval for grounding**, and a human handoff into your existing support tool.
- **Build the eval set and the escalation rules yourself.** They encode your product's policy, which no vendor
  knows.
- **Revisit** when you add tools (that becomes an agent-permissions problem) or serve minors or a regulated
  domain.

Rough cost, with assumed volumes: 5,000 conversations a day on Claude Sonnet 5 is about **$35 a day, roughly
$1,050 a month**. Moderation is free. The line to watch is human review of flags: at 4.3% precision, about **100
flags a day**, roughly **33 minutes** of someone's day.

## Pitfalls

- **Spending the round on model architecture.** The candidate reports being pushed past exactly this.
- **One generic refusal for every risky category.** A person in crisis needs a different reply from someone
  asking for legal advice.
- **No over-refusal measurement.** An assistant that refuses too much fails users quietly.
- **No incident owner or kill switch.** When something goes wrong at 2 a.m., someone must be able to turn it off.

## What they're really asking

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "Each of the three hot experts is getting about 27% of tokens instead of about 3%, so 8.5 times its share. What
> breaks depends on whether experts have a capacity limit. With a limit, most tokens overflow and skip the layer:
> nothing looks slow, but quality drops quietly. Without a limit, whichever GPU holds the hot experts does several
> times the work, and every step waits for it. I'd first check the skew is real, then fix placement at serving
> time, and then look at the balancing term in training."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer.

The interviewer wants to see you turn a routing statistic into its system effect, and know the fixes at both
training and serving time.

Some background. A **mixture-of-experts (MoE)** model has, in each layer, many parallel blocks called **experts**,
and a small scoring layer called the **router**. For each **token** (roughly ¾ of a word), the router scores every
expert and sends the token to the best one (or best few). The idea is that each token does only a fraction of the
model's work.

For a backend engineer, this is a **hot-key problem** in a sharded system. The router is a partitioner, the experts
are shards, and 80% of traffic is landing on 3 of 32 shards. You already know the two ways that fails: either you
shed load (requests get dropped), or you don't, and the hot shard becomes the straggler everyone waits on. MoE
systems fail in exactly those two ways, with one twist: a "dropped" token isn't an error the user sees. It just gets
a worse answer.

The prompt is an example from a techinterview.org MoE prep guide, not a candidate report <Cite id="s-9e6b455b13" />.

## Clarify first

- **Training or serving?** "Is this during training or in production serving?" A penalty term in training is a
  training fix; where you put experts, and how many copies, are serving fixes.
- **Top-1 or top-k?** "Does each token go to one expert or several?" Assume one (top-1) for the arithmetic.
- **Capacity:** "Is there a cap on tokens per expert that drops the overflow?" Ask; it changes the symptom.
- **Placement:** "How are the 32 experts placed on GPUs?" Assume 8 GPUs with 4 experts each.
- **Scope:** "Is the skew global, or from one customer's or one language's traffic?"

## Answer

### First, the size of the skew

With uniform routing, each of 32 experts gets 100 ÷ 32 = **3.125%** of tokens. Here, each hot expert gets 80 ÷ 3 =
**26.7%**, which is **8.5×** its share. The other 29 experts split the remaining 20%, about 0.69% each.

### What breaks depends on capacity

A **capacity limit** caps how many tokens each expert accepts per step. In the Switch Transformer design, capacity
is (tokens ÷ experts) × a **capacity factor**, and overflow tokens are "passed directly to the next layer through
the residual connection" ([Fedus et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.03961)). The **residual connection** is the side
path that carries a token's vector around each block unchanged, so an overflowing token simply skips the expert.

| Setup | What happens | What to say in the interview |
|---|---|---|
| With a capacity limit, factor 1.25 | Each expert accepts about 3.9% of tokens. Each hot expert overflows by about 22.8 points, so about **68%** of all tokens skip the layer. | "Latency looks fine. Quality drops quietly." |
| No limit; hot experts on different GPUs | The GPU with one hot and three cold experts does 28.7% of all tokens, against 12.5% balanced: **2.3×**. | "Every step waits for the busiest GPU." |
| No limit; all three hot experts on one GPU | That GPU does 80.7% of tokens: **6.5×** the balanced load. | "Placement can make the same skew three times worse." |

Without a limit there is a second cost. When experts live on different GPUs (**expert parallelism**), each token is
shipped to its expert's GPU and back, an exchange called an **all-to-all**. The traffic to the hot GPU grows too.

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the numbers (for a Staff-level follow-up)</summary>

- **Capacity:** 1.25 ÷ 32 = 3.9% of the batch per expert. Each hot expert overflows by 26.7 − 3.9 = 22.8 points.
  With top-1 routing, 3 × 22.8 ≈ 68% of tokens skip the layer.
- **Balanced load per GPU:** 4 experts × 3.125% = 12.5%.
- **One hot expert per GPU:** 26.7 + 3 × 0.69 = 28.7%; 28.7 ÷ 12.5 = 2.3×.
- **All three hot on one GPU:** 80 + 0.69 = 80.7%; 80.7 ÷ 12.5 = 6.5×.
- **Balance term:** with Pᵢ ≈ fᵢ, N × Σ fᵢ × Pᵢ = 32 × (3 × 0.267² + 29 × 0.0069²) = **6.87**.

</details>

### Diagnose with the right numbers

During training, a model minimizes an error score called the **loss**. Switch adds an extra **auxiliary loss** that
penalizes uneven routing: N × Σ fᵢ × Pᵢ, where N is the number of experts, fᵢ the fraction of tokens sent to expert
i, and Pᵢ the router's average probability for it. It equals 1.0 for uniform routing. At this skew it is **6.87**.

Say it like this: "I'd track the balance term, tokens per expert and the dropped-token rate, per layer, with an
alert when they move."

### Fix, in order

1. **Check it's real.** Break it down per layer and per customer. A narrow traffic mix, such as one language, can
   legitimately favour a few experts.
2. **Serving:** make extra copies of the hot experts on other GPUs, and re-place experts by observed load so no GPU
   holds two hot ones. The guide names both <Cite id="s-9e6b455b13" />.
3. **Training:** check the balancing term. Switch weights its auxiliary loss at α = 10⁻²; the guide warns that
   weighting it too strongly hurts quality. DeepSeek-V3 instead adds a per-expert bias used only for choosing
   experts. After each step it lowers the bias by a small amount γ for overloaded experts and raises it for
   underloaded ones ([arXiv 2412.19437](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19437), §2.1.2).
4. **Stability:** run the router in 32-bit floats (Switch casts the router input to float32) and consider router
   z-loss, a second penalty that keeps router scores from growing too large, which the guide lists.
5. **Capacity factor:** raising it reduces drops but costs memory and wasted padding. It treats the symptom, not the
   cause.

## Small-team version

You won't train an MoE router or place experts. If you host an MoE model yourself on a serving engine, what you
can do is watch per-request latency tails and quality on your own **eval** (a fixed set of your real prompts,
scored) when your traffic mix changes, and report problems upstream.

Otherwise, call the model as a service and let the provider own placement. That is the default, and it is cheap: an
MoE such as gpt-oss-120b on Together costs about **$0.0007** for a request with 3,000 input and 400 output tokens.

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the numbers (for a Staff-level follow-up)</summary>

- **Per request** at $0.15 in and $0.60 out per 1M tokens (fetched 2026-10-11): 3,000 × 0.15 ÷ 10⁶ = $0.00045, plus
  400 × 0.60 ÷ 10⁶ = $0.00024, total **$0.00069**.

</details>

Revisit only if you self-host for data residency or volume.

## Pitfalls

- **Treating imbalance only as a speed problem.** With a capacity limit, dropped tokens make it a quality problem
  that no latency graph shows.
- **Cranking the auxiliary-loss weight until balance is perfect.** Quality falls when the penalty fights the
  router's real preferences.
- **Ignoring placement.** Three hot experts on one GPU is far worse (6.5×) than the same skew spread out (2.3×).

## What they're really asking

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "It's a durable job queue where each job holds one GPU for a minute or more. The job table in the database is the
> only truth; I return a job ID only after the row is saved. Workers borrow a job under a lease and renew it with
> heartbeats. Every lease gets a new fence number, and a worker can save its result only if its fence is still
> current, so a worker that froze can't overwrite the real result. GPUs are shared by GPU-seconds used, with a
> weight for the paid tier, and retries are capped so a broken job can't loop forever."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer, so you can say it with understanding.

The interviewer wants to know whether you can design a job queue where every step can fail, and make re-running a
task safe.

You have probably built a background job queue for emails or reports. Those jobs are short and cheap; if one runs
twice, little is lost. A text-to-video queue has the same shape, but three things change:

1. **Each job is long and expensive.** One task holds one GPU (a graphics processor rented for dollars an hour) for
   90 seconds or more. A lost job is a user who got nothing; a duplicated job is paid for twice.
2. **The GPUs are not all the same.** A new model version can work on one GPU type and crash on another.
3. **The fleet is often nearly full**, so queues build quickly and users need honest wait estimates.

The reported title is a text-to-video scheduler with one GPU per task and fair queueing. Only the title is
candidate-reported; the rest is the write-up by PracHub (an interview-question aggregator) of the
OpenAI-tagged question. It says failure handling is "the
core of the discussion" and checks for a durable job store, fairness in weighted GPU time, leases, heartbeats,
fencing tokens, bounded retries and throughput estimates <Cite id="s-725e58e60a" />. The follow-ups it lists (paid
tier, a model failing on some GPU types, wait estimates) decide the round.

## Clarify first

Ask for numbers, because the page gives none. Say an assumption for each:

- **Arrival rate and task duration?** "Assume 2 tasks a second, 90 seconds of GPU time on average, with a long
  tail."
- **Fleet size and GPU types?** "Assume 200 GPUs of two types."
- **Fairness unit?** Per user, per organization, or per tier? What does "faster" mean for paid: a wait target, a
  guaranteed share, or the right to interrupt free work?
- **Can a task save progress partway and resume** (a **checkpoint**)? "Assume no; a crash restarts it from zero."
- **Delivery?** Polling, a callback to the user's URL (a **webhook**), or both? Can users cancel?

## Answer

### Words you need first

- **Lease**: a time-limited loan of a job to a worker: "worker W may run job J until time T".
- **Heartbeat**: a small "still here" message the worker sends every few seconds to renew its lease. If heartbeats
  stop and the lease's **TTL** (time to live) runs out, the job is given to another worker.
- **Fencing token** (or fence): a number that goes up every time the job is leased. Only the holder of the current
  number can save a result.
- **GPU-seconds**: one GPU busy for one second. A 90-second task costs 90 GPU-seconds.

### Size it first

On average, the number of busy GPUs equals tasks per second times GPU-seconds per task (this is **Little's law**).
With our assumptions that is **180 of 200 GPUs busy: 90% utilization**. That leaves little spare room. A
10-minute burst at 5 tasks a second leaves about **1,667 tasks queued**, and with only about 0.22 spare tasks a
second afterwards, the backlog takes **about 2 hours** to drain.

"At 90% busy, a short burst becomes hours of waiting. So deciding at the door whether to accept a job, which is
called **admission control**, and giving honest wait estimates are part of the design, not extras."

### The system

```
Client ── POST /jobs (idempotency key) ──► API ── commits the row, THEN returns the ID
                                              │
                                              ▼
                                   Jobs table (the only truth)
                                              │
                                              ▼
                       Scheduler ── fair queues per tenant, grants lease + new fence
                                              │
                                              ▼
                       GPU worker ── heartbeats, renders, writes outputs/<job>/attempt-<fence>/
                                              │
                                              ▼
                       Commit ── "set succeeded WHERE fence = mine"
```

**Data model.** A jobs table is the source of truth: state, tier, model version, attempt, fence, lease owner and
expiry, output reference. The scheduler's queues are a cache rebuilt from it, so a scheduler crash loses nothing.

**Leases and fencing.** The scheduler grants a lease and increments the job's fence. The worker heartbeats (say
every 10 seconds with a 30-second TTL, assumed), writes output to a path that includes its attempt number, and
commits with a conditional update that succeeds only if the fence is still its own. A worker that paused past its
lease finds its commit rejected. This is Kleppmann's fencing-token pattern
([2016](https://martin.kleppmann.com/2016/02/08/how-to-do-distributed-locking.html)).

### Follow one job

Maya (free tier) asks for a fox running through snow: 90 seconds of GPU time. Timings are illustrative.

| Clock | What happens | Fence | What to say in the interview |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 s | API commits the row and returns J-1042. | 0 | "No ID before the commit." |
| 40 s | Leased to worker W1. | 1 | "Every lease grant bumps the fence." |
| 70 s | W1 freezes (a stuck driver call). Heartbeats stop. | 1 | "Frozen and dead look the same from outside." |
| 100 s | Lease expired; leased again to W2. | 2 | "The job re-runs under a new fence." |
| 190 s | W2 commits "where fence = 2": one row changes. | 2 | "Only the current fence can finish the job." |
| 200 s | W1 wakes and commits "where fence = 1": zero rows. | 2 | "The stale write is rejected; one video, not two." |

The freeze cost about 30 seconds of work plus 30 seconds before anyone noticed. With no checkpoints, a crash at a
random point of a 90-second task wastes **about 45 seconds plus up to 30 seconds of detection** on average.

### Fairness

Count fairness in **GPU-seconds, not tasks**: a 10-minute render should not count the same as a 10-second clip.
Use **deficit round robin**: go around the tenants in turn, giving each a credit of GPU-seconds in proportion to
its tier's weight, and let a tenant start a job when its credit covers the job. Add a cap on how many GPUs one
tenant can hold, and no saving up credit while idle. Keep separate queues for long and short jobs, so short clips
don't wait behind 10-minute renders.

### Retries and the dead-letter state

Cap attempts (say 3), back off with jitter (a random delay so retries don't line up), and retry on a different GPU.
After the last attempt the job goes to a **dead-letter** state: a visible "failed, here's why", so a job that
always fails can't loop forever.

### The three follow-ups

**Paid tier.** Three options, each with a cost. Higher weights give more share but no guarantee. Reserved capacity
gives a floor but can sit idle. **Preempting** free tasks (stopping them to make room) gives the lowest wait but
throws away their work. "I'd combine weights with a small reserved pool that free work can borrow and that I can
take back."

**A model failing on some GPU types.** Count failures per pair of (model_version, gpu_type). When one pair's failure
rate crosses a threshold, open a **circuit breaker**: stop sending that model to that GPU type, route its jobs to
other types, and alert. Next time, use a **canary**: roll each new version to a small share of traffic on each GPU
type separately before widening.

**Wait estimates.** GPU-seconds of work ahead of the job in its tier, divided by the GPU-seconds per second the tier
is currently getting. Show it as a range, and measure how wrong it was.

## Small-team version

A small team running image or video generation builds the same thing, smaller: a Postgres jobs table claimed with
`SELECT … FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED` (several workers take different rows without waiting on each other), a fence
column, a cap of 3 attempts, per-tenant concurrency limits, and GPUs billed per second until they would be busy
enough to justify dedicated ones.

Run this question's load through the family walkthrough's break-even formula (list prices fetched 2026-10-11):

- 180 busy GPUs on RunPod serverless L40S at $1.75 an hour is about **$7,560 a day**, paid only for busy seconds.
- 200 dedicated secure L40S at $1.09 an hour is about **$5,230 a day**, paid whether busy or not.

Dedicated wins once the pool is busy more than about 62% of the time. At this question's 90%, dedicated is cheaper.
At a small team's typical spiky load, it usually isn't. "I'd rent by the second until steady load keeps a dedicated
pool more than about 62% busy."

- Serverless: 180 × $1.75 × 24 ≈ $7,560 a day.
- Dedicated: 200 × $1.09 × 24 ≈ $5,232 a day.
- Break-even utilization: $1.09 ÷ $1.75 ≈ 62%.

The fence check and the "kill a worker mid-job" test are not optional at any size.

## Pitfalls

- **Leases without fencing.** A worker that paused past its lease can still overwrite the real result.
- **Fairness by task count.** Users with long jobs take far more GPU than users with short ones.
- **Retrying forever on the GPU type where the model crashes.** Every retry fails the same way and burns a GPU.
- **Scheduler state that can't be rebuilt.** A scheduler crash then loses jobs that were only in its memory.

## What they're really asking

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "I'll get a plain pipeline running first: split the documents into chunks, embed them, find the closest chunks
> to the question, and have the model answer with citations. Then I'll turn 'it seems to work' into a number. I'd
> write 20 to 30 questions where I know the right documents, and report two different numbers: the hit rate (did
> any right chunk make the top k?) and recall (what share of the right chunks did?). Both at the k I actually
> send to the model, with an error bar, and next to keyword-only and vector-only baselines."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer.

The interviewer wants to see two things: that you can build a small retrieval pipeline quickly, and that you
know how to prove it works.

The second part is the new one for a backend engineer. A normal search feature has a clear contract: a database
query returns the rows that match, and a unit test can check it. Here the search works by meaning. It always
returns a ranked list of "closest" chunks, and the closest chunk can still be the wrong one. Then a language model
writes a fluent answer from whatever was retrieved. So there is no unit test for "the search is good". You need a
small set of questions with known answers and a measurement on it, much like an A/B test.

The build is the warm-up. The evaluation question is where you show you know what to measure, at what k, and how
sure you are.

(This was reported by a Harvey candidate who was later rejected. The last question was how to improve the
evaluation, "such as recall or hit rate" <Cite id="s-74265be7ec" />.)

### Words you need first

- **RAG** (retrieval-augmented generation): find a few relevant pieces of your documents, paste them into the
  prompt of a large language model (**LLM**), and ask it to answer from them. LLMs read text in **tokens**,
  pieces of words of roughly ¾ of a word each, and API prices are per token.
- **Chunk**: one of those pieces, usually a paragraph or short section, with an ID.
- **Embedding**: a list of numbers (a vector, for example 384 long) that a small model computes from text, so
  that texts with similar meaning get vectors pointing in similar directions.
- **Top-k**: the k chunks with the best scores. **k** is how many you hand to the model, often 5.

## Clarify first

Spend a minute on the setup and say your assumptions out loud:

- **What corpus, and how big?** "I'll assume a few dozen documents that fit in memory."
- **Hosted embeddings or a local model?** "I'll use a local embedding model from the sentence-transformers
  library in Colab."
- **Is there a labeled question set?** "I'll assume not, so I'll write 20–30 questions with their relevant
  document IDs."
- **Do answers need one passage or several?** This decides whether hit rate or recall is the honest number.
- **How many chunks go to the model?** "I'll assume k = 5."

## Answer

### The build, in order

1. **Chunk** on headings and paragraphs, with stable IDs like `doc.md#3` and the heading path attached. Stable
   means the same chunk gets the same ID on every run, because your labels and citations point at it.
2. **Embed** with `normalize_embeddings=True`. That makes every vector length 1, so the similarity score between
   the question and every chunk (**cosine similarity**, the angle between two vectors) is one matrix multiply:
   `matrix @ query`. Then `argpartition` picks the top k without sorting the whole corpus.
3. **Generate** with a prompt that lists numbered sources, requires `[n]` citations and allows "I don't know".
   Check the citations in code: every sentence cites, and every citation points at a chunk the model was given.

The [family walkthrough](/system-design-labs/interviews/walkthroughs/rag-in-code/#solution-in-stages) has the code
for each stage.

### Hit rate and recall are different numbers

The interviewer named two metrics. Separate them first, because many people (and codebases) blur them:

- **Hit rate (hit@k)**: the share of questions with at least one relevant chunk in the top k.
- **Recall@k**: the share of *all* relevant chunks that made the top k. It is lower whenever an answer needs two
  chunks and you found one.

Follow one question to see the difference. Say a user asks *"Can I get a refund on an annual plan after 30
days?"* (an illustrative example). Two chunks answer it: A, the refund policy, and B, the annual-plan terms. Your
search returns five chunks in this order:

| Position | Chunk | Relevant? |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | C (an FAQ entry) | no |
| 2 | **A** (refund policy) | yes |
| 3 | D (account settings) | no |
| 4 | **B** (annual-plan terms) | yes |
| 5 | E (another billing page) | no |

| Metric | Value | What to say in the interview |
|---|---|---|
| hit@3 | 1 | "Something useful is in the top 3." |
| recall@3 | 0.5 | "But the model only sees half of what it needs if I send 3." |
| recall@5 | 1.0 | "At k = 5 it gets both, so k = 5 is the setting I'd report." |

Say: "I report both, at the k I actually send. Hit rate says whether the model got anything useful; recall says
whether it got everything it needs."

### How to improve the evaluation, in order

1. **A real labeled set.** Grow it to 50+ questions, written before tuning anything. Include paraphrases that
   share no words with the source, identifiers such as error codes, questions that need two chunks like the
   refund one, and a few the corpus can't answer. Score those last ones on whether the system says "I don't
   know". Label at the document level if chunk IDs move when you re-chunk.
2. **An error bar.** Twenty questions is a small sample. With 20 questions and a hit rate of 0.8, the 95%
   interval is about **±17.5 points**, so 0.8 could really be anything from about 0.62 to 0.98. When you compare
   two configurations on the same questions, use a **paired bootstrap**: resample the questions many times,
   recompute the difference between the two systems each time, and see whether the middle 95% of differences
   excludes zero.
3. **Metrics that care about order.** Add **MRR** (mean reciprocal rank: 1 ÷ the position of the first right
   chunk, averaged over questions) or **nDCG@10** (a score that rewards relevant chunks near the top, 1.0 for the
   perfect order) when order matters. Also measure recall at a deeper cut, such as the top 50. If the right chunk
   isn't in the top 50 either, a **reranker** (a slower model that re-scores a short list) can't help.
4. **The answer itself.** First run the free, code-only citation checks. Then measure **faithfulness**: whether
   every claim in the answer is supported by the cited chunk. That needs a judge, usually another LLM call.
   Check the judge against about 30 answers you labeled yourself before you trust its number.
5. **Baselines.** Report keyword search alone (**BM25**, the standard keyword-ranking formula) and vector search
   alone (**dense**) next to the combined version (**hybrid**). If the hybrid isn't better than both, look for a
   bug.

The rag lab shows why the baselines matter. On SciFact, a public benchmark of science claims, its hybrid scored
about the same as dense until the keyword side's word-splitting was fixed: hit@3 of
<LabNumber lab="rag" metric="hit@3 on BEIR SciFact" /> (dense, hybrid, rerank) versus

## Small-team version

At a small company this notebook is often the first version of the feature. The advice in money and actions:

- **Call a hosted embedding API.** text-embedding-3-small was listed at $0.02 per million tokens when fetched on
  2026-10-11, so embedding cost doesn't matter.
- **Keep vectors in NumPy.** Revisit storage only past a million or so vectors.
- **Spend the saved time on the labeled set.** 50 questions written with someone who knows the domain, run on
  every change, is worth more than any framework.
- **Budget for generation, not embedding.** With the family walkthrough's assumptions (2,200 input and 300 output
  tokens per query), gpt-5-mini at $0.25 / $2.00 per million costs about **$0.00115 per query**, so 5,000 queries
  a day is about **$173 per 30 days**. Change the model only when the eval says the cheap one fails.

## Pitfalls

- **Calling a hit rate "recall".** They differ whenever an answer needs more than one chunk.
- **Reporting at a k you don't send.** recall@20 says nothing about what the model saw at k = 5.
- **Twenty questions and no interval.** A ±17.5-point error bar is too wide to declare one configuration the
  winner.
- **Eval questions generated from the chunks they are scored against.** They share the chunk's words, so every
  score looks better than it is.
- **Optimizing retrieval while generation is the one failing.** Check the citations and faithfulness too, or you
  tune the wrong half.

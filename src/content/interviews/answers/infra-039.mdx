## What they're really asking

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "The output layer turns the model's final vector into one score per vocabulary word. If I split it by
> vocabulary, each GPU computes complete scores for its words, and the only traffic is combining decisions. If
> I split it by hidden dimension, each GPU has partial scores for every word, and adding them moves 15.7× more
> bytes. With a vocabulary split, the global top k is exact: merge each GPU's top k. But sampling from the full
> distribution is not the same as sampling from those candidates, because any word can be picked. For an exact
> sample I'd use Gumbel-max, where each GPU sends one pair."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer.

This question is about the model's last step. After all its layers, the model has one vector of numbers for
the next position. The **LM head** (language-model head) multiplies that vector by a big weight matrix to get
one score per word in the vocabulary. Those scores are called **logits**. A **softmax** turns them into
probabilities, and a **sampler** picks the next token.

The backend analogy is a top-k query over a sharded table. If each shard holds complete rows, you can ask every
shard for its top k and merge: that is exact. But if the query were "pick one row at random, weighted by
score", the per-shard top k would not be enough, because a row outside every top k can still be picked. The
interview is about keeping those two problems apart.

(A PracHub listing for a Microsoft onsite, 2026: compare sharding the `[H, V]` head by vocabulary and by hidden
dimension, and explain why local top-k candidates give an exact global top-k but not exact full-softmax
sampling <Cite id="s-85ccfb1fcb" />. PracHub is a site that collects reported questions.) The page warns:
"Exact full-distribution sampling is different from top-k sampling; do not silently replace one with the
other." The test is precise reasoning about partial versus complete dot products, and about what a decision
needs from the whole distribution.

### Words you need first

- **H** is the hidden size, the length of the model's final vector. **V** is the vocabulary size. A **token** is
  one vocabulary entry, a chunk of text of about ¾ of a word.
- **Sharding** splits the matrix across `D` GPUs (here called **ranks**). An **all-reduce** adds every rank's
  full-size partial result. An **all-gather** gives every rank all the slices. A **reduce-scatter** adds and
  leaves each rank only its slice.
- **Top-k** means the k highest-scoring tokens. **Temperature** divides the logits before the softmax, to make
  sampling more or less random. **Top-p** samples from the smallest set of tokens whose probabilities add up to p.

## Clarify first

- **What arrives?** "Is the incoming hidden vector copied to every rank, or split across them?"
- **What does the consumer need?** "All the logits, the global top k, or an exact sample from the full softmax?"
- **Sizes?** "Llama-3.1-70B's head: H = 8,192, V = 128,256, bf16 (2 bytes per number), D = 8 ranks on NVLink,
  NVIDIA's fast GPU-to-GPU link."

## Answer

### The worked example: one token through a 70B model's head

The head is 8,192 × 128,256 = 1.05 × 10⁹ weights, **2.10 GB** in bf16. Reading it once takes 0.63 ms on one H100,
or 78 µs per rank split eight ways. We follow one token's final vector through the head on 8 ranks.

| Step | Decision | Bytes per token | What to say in the interview |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Split by vocabulary | 16.4 KB gather (only if the vector arrives split) | "Complete logits per rank; no reduction." |
| 2 | Or split by hidden dimension | 256.5 KB all-reduce | "15.7× more bytes, so vocabulary wins." |
| 3 | Need the top 50 | 400 (value, index) pairs | "Merging local top-k is exact." |
| 4 | Need an exact sample | 2 scalars, or 1 pair per rank | "Candidates aren't enough; use Gumbel-max or a global normalizer." |

### Steps 1–2: vocabulary vs hidden sharding

**Vocabulary sharding** (`[H, V/D]` per rank). Each rank needs the full hidden vector and computes complete
logits for its V/D tokens. No reduction is needed to form logits. If the hidden vector arrives split, all-gather
it first: H × s = **16.4 KB** per token.

**Hidden sharding** (`[H/D, V]` per rank). Each rank computes partial dot products for every vocabulary entry.
The logits exist only after an all-reduce (or a reduce-scatter into vocabulary slices) of V × s = **256.5 KB**
per token in bf16. That is V ÷ H = **15.7×** the vocabulary scheme's gather.

It wins only if the hidden vector already arrives split and you can't afford the gather, which is rare given
the sizes. This answers the item's third follow-up: an earlier layer that outputs hidden slices removes the
gather but not the logits reduction.

Say it like this: "Vocabulary sharding gives complete logits; hidden sharding gives partial ones and a V-wide
reduction. V is 15.7 times H here, so vocabulary sharding."

### Step 3: exact global top-k from local top-k

If a token is in the global top k, fewer than k tokens beat it overall. So fewer than k beat it within its own
slice, which means it is in that slice's local top k. All-gather the D × k (value, index) pairs and take the top
k. With k = 50 and D = 8, that is 400 pairs per token instead of 128,256 logits.

### Step 4: why that isn't exact full-softmax sampling

A full-softmax sample can pick any token, in proportion to its probability, including the long tail that
appears in no slice's top k. Sampling among the gathered candidates samples a cut-off, renormalized
distribution. When the distribution is flat, the missing probability is large. Two exact alternatives:

- **A distributed normalizer.** Each rank finds its largest logit and its sum of `exp(logit − max)`. One
  all-reduce gives the global max, a second the rescaled total. Pick a rank in proportion to its share, then
  sample inside it. Two scalars per token cross the network.
- **Gumbel-max.** Each rank adds independent random noise of a particular kind (**Gumbel noise**) to its logits,
  after applying temperature, and returns its largest value and its index. The global largest of these noisy
  values is an exact sample from the softmax. One (value, index) pair per rank.

Top-k *sampling* (sample within the global top k) is exact with the candidate merge. Top-p needs the global
sorted running total of probability, so it needs more communication.

Say it like this: "Top-k merges exactly. Full-softmax sampling doesn't, because the tail matters. Gumbel-max
fixes it with one pair per rank."

## Small-team version

Serving engines handle vocabulary-split heads for you. The part that matters to a small team is the sampling
distinction. When you change sampling settings (temperature, top-k, top-p) on a self-hosted model, check outputs
on your eval (your own set of test prompts), because the settings change the distribution you ship.

The check is cheap: 200 prompts × 400 output tokens is **80,000 tokens per setting**. That is about **80 s** of
GPU time at an assumed 1,000 tokens per second batched, **a few cents** on a rented A100 at $1.59 an hour.

On a managed API you usually get only temperature and top-p, and the provider's sampler is not yours to shard.
The question only becomes yours if you self-host a large model across GPUs.

## Pitfalls

- **Gathering all logits to every rank.** When only the top k is needed, D × k pairs are enough.
- **Calling candidate-only sampling "exact".** It drops the tail of the distribution.
- **Picking hidden sharding without sizing it.** Its V-wide reduction is 15.7× the vocabulary scheme's gather.
- **Adding Gumbel noise before temperature.** Temperature must be applied to the logits first, or the sample is
  from the wrong distribution.

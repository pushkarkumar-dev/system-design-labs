## What they're really asking

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "We built because the existing product missed one requirement we couldn't live without, and we checked that
> with a trial before deciding. Over three years, building cost less on our numbers, but only because we built a
> narrow component and bought the rest. The real cost is the people who maintain it, so I'd name the condition
> under which we'd switch to the product, and I've checked it since. If buying would have been better in
> hindsight, I'd say so."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer, so you can say it with understanding.

The interviewer wants to know whether you build because it is fun or because it is justified, and whether you can
show the difference. Expect pushback: the manager will argue that existing solutions already covered the need.
Answer with a requirement, a cost comparison and an exit plan, not with "the existing tools weren't good enough".

### Why this is harder than a normal build-versus-buy

You have probably made build-versus-buy calls for a queue or a database. AI tools differ in one way that matters:
the market moves very fast. A product that misses your requirement today may meet it in six months, and an
open-source project may catch up in a release or two. So a build decision ages quickly, and an answer without an
exit condition is incomplete.

### Words you need first

- **Total cost over a horizon**: everything each option costs over a fixed number of years, including people, not
  just the licence.
- **Loaded cost**: what an engineer costs per year including benefits and taxes, not only salary.
- **Opportunity cost**: what the same engineers could have built instead. It never appears on an invoice.
- **Exit condition**: the signal that would make you switch to the bought option later.

past build while the manager argues existing solutions already covered the need.

## Clarify first

- **Past or hypothetical?** If it's a past decision, say what you knew at the time.
- **Which alternative?** A commercial product, an open-source project, or a cloud service?
- **Which horizon matters to the business?** One year or three?

## Answer

### Six steps

1. **The need, precisely.** Name the requirement existing options missed: a latency target, a rule that data must
   stay in one country, an integration, a scale point, or the cost at your volume.
2. **What you did to test buying.** A trial or benchmark against that requirement, and a conversation with the
   vendor or maintainers.
3. **Total cost over a horizon.** The formula and an example are below.
4. **The non-cost factors.** Whether the system is core to the product, lock-in and vendor risk, and who maintains
   it when the person who built it leaves. Opportunity cost belongs here: what else those engineers could have
   shipped.
5. **The scope you built.** Often the best answer is that you built a narrow component and bought the rest.
6. **The exit.** The condition under which you would switch to the existing solution, and whether you have checked
   it since.

### The example we will follow

Assume an internal tool used by **300 people**. The product that almost fits costs an assumed **$30 per seat per
month**. Building your own takes an assumed half an engineer-year, and an engineer costs **about $194K a year**
loaded (from US Bureau of Labor Statistics wage data, derived in the family walkthrough).

| Line | Build (assumed) | Buy (assumed) | What to say in the interview |
|---|---|---|---|
| One-time | **$97K** (half an engineer-year) | **$19.4K** to integrate | "Building costs five times more up front." |
| Every year | **$47.1K** (15% of an engineer to maintain, plus $18K to run) | **$108K** in licences | "But the licence costs more than twice our upkeep each year." |
| Over 3 years | **about $238K** | **about $343K** | "Over three years, building is about $100K cheaper." |
| Break-even | after **about 1.3 years** | | "After about a year and a quarter, building is ahead, if our upkeep estimate holds." |

The result is sensitive to the assumptions. Double the maintenance share and the gap shrinks fast. And the formula
leaves out the opportunity cost of the half engineer-year, which is often the real argument for buying. Say that
yourself before the interviewer does.

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the numbers (for a Staff-level follow-up)</summary>

- **B:** 0.5 × $194K = $97K. **I:** 0.1 × $194K = $19.4K.
- **M:** 0.15 × $194K ≈ $29.1K a year; **R:** $18K a year; M + R = $47.1K a year.
- **L:** $30 × 300 × 12 = $108K a year.
- **Three years:** build 97 + 3 × 47.1 = $238.3K; buy 19.4 + 3 × 108 = $343.4K.
- **Break-even:** 97 + 47.1H = 19.4 + 108H, so H = 77.6 ÷ 60.9 ≈ 1.3 years.

</details>

Say it like this: "On my assumptions the build pays off after about a year and a quarter, but that ignores what
those engineers could have built instead, so I'd only build the part the product can't do."

### If buying would have been better

If, in hindsight, buying would have been better, say so and what you learned. That answers the manager's pushback
better than defending every choice. "We built more than we needed; today I'd buy the product and build only the
integration" is a strong answer.

## Small-team version

At a company of 50 to 500 people the default is **buy**, because engineer-time is the scarcest resource.

- **Buy** access to the AI model through a **model API** (a paid web service that runs the model and bills per
  token, a small piece of text), and usually **tracing** (a tool that records every model call, its inputs,
  outputs and cost).
- **Build** what is specific to you: the **eval set** (real examples with known good answers, used to score the
  system), search over your own data, cost limits, and the integration into your product.

**Self-hosting**, running an open model on GPUs you rent, rarely wins at small-team volume. One published analysis
finds a rented machine with 2 H100 GPUs (an NVIDIA data-center GPU) cannot break even against the same open
model on a **serverless endpoint** (a provider runs it and bills per token) when traffic follows business hours
([Cohorte](https://cohorte.co/blog/self-hosted-llm-cost-vs-api-break-even), 2026-09-20; labour excluded). That
machine is about **$6,117 a month**, and 15% of a loaded engineer to look after it adds about **$2,430 a month**.

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the numbers (for a Staff-level follow-up)</summary>

- **Rented node:** 2 GPUs × $4.19 per GPU-hour × 730 hours a month ≈ $6,117 a month.
- **Upkeep:** 0.15 × $194K ÷ 12 ≈ $2,430 a month.

</details>

Coding agents (AI tools that write code with little supervision) lower the build cost, but not maintenance.
Revisit a buy when the vendor bill passes build plus maintenance over your horizon, or when a requirement (data
location, latency, an integration) isn't met.

Say it like this: "At our size I'd buy the model and build only what's ours: the test set, the search over our
data, and the integration."

## Pitfalls

- **"The existing tools weren't good enough" with no requirement named.** It sounds like a preference, not a
  reason.
- **Leaving out maintenance and opportunity cost.** The build looks cheap only until you count the people.
- **No trial of the alternative.** You can't claim the product failed a requirement you never tested.
- **No exit condition.** The decision is never revisited, even after the market catches up.

## What they're really asking

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "I'll accept the numbers: 50 engineer-years at $400K is $20M. I'll compare that with what the project returned
> each year, minus what it costs to keep running, and I'll only count the part we wouldn't have got anyway. If all
> the gain is ours it pays back in about two and a half years; if half of it would have arrived from an
> open-source release, closer to seven. So: yes, with a condition, and here is what I'd cut if I did it again."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer, so you can say it with understanding.

The interviewer wants to know whether you can put a price on your own work and judge it honestly. They are not
asking you to defend the architecture. They want arithmetic, a comparison with the alternative, and a verdict
that says when the answer would be no.

### Why this is harder than it sounds

Most engineers think of cost as the cloud bill. In this question the bill is small next to the people. Fifty
engineer-years is the cost, and it was spent whether or not the system is used.

The other half is harder: "worth it" always means "better than what would have happened otherwise". In AI
infrastructure that alternative moves fast. Open-source tools and vendors improve every few months, so a gain
your team built might have arrived for free a year later. An answer that counts every gain as the project's own
is the easiest way to weaken your case.

### Words you need first

- **Loaded cost**: what an engineer costs the company per year, including benefits and taxes, not only salary.
  Treat the interviewer's $400K as a loaded cost.
- **Counterfactual**: what would have happened without the project, such as buying a product, waiting for an
  open-source release, or doing nothing.
- **Attribution**: the share of a gain that is really yours, because the counterfactual would not have delivered
  it. Written as a number `a` from 0 to 1.
- **Payback period**: how many years of net gain it takes to earn back the build cost.

The question was reported from an Anthropic Senior Staff project deep dive: "If the project required 50
engineer-years at roughly $400K per engineer, was the outcome worth a $20M investment?"
(<Cite id="s-e98cbef9cd" />, a post on Blind, an anonymous forum for tech employees, dated "Jul 25" with no year shown). The poster blames the culture round,
not this question, for the outcome.

## Clarify first

Spend a minute on the setup, and say your assumptions out loud:

- **Accept the framing.** "Fifty times $400K is $20M; I'll work with that." Don't argue the per-engineer figure.
- **Set the horizon.** "I'll judge it over three years, and I'll count ongoing maintenance as a cost."
- **Separate measured from estimated.** "Here is what we measured, and here is what I'm estimating."

## Answer

### The example we will follow

Use your own project. To show the method, here is a made-up one with every number **assumed**. A team built an
internal **serving platform**: the software that loads trained AI models onto **GPUs** (the expensive chips that
run models) and answers requests from them. Running a model to answer requests is called **inference**. The
company's GPUs for inference, the **fleet**, cost an assumed **$60M a year**. The platform let each GPU handle
20% more requests per second at the same response time. Requests per second is **throughput**.

### Do it out loud in five steps

1. **Value lines.** List what the project produced, each with how it was measured: capacity avoided, incidents
   avoided, launches enabled, teams that adopted it.
2. **Attribution.** For each line, how much would have happened anyway: a vendor release, an open-source engine,
   or a cheaper partial fix.
3. **Ongoing cost.** The people and compute it takes to keep running, every year.
4. **Payback.** One formula puts it together.
5. **Verdict with a condition, and what you'd cut.**

Here is the example run through those steps:

| Step | Running example (all assumed) | What to say in the interview |
|---|---|---|
| Cost | 50 × $400K = **$20M** | "I accept the $20M." |
| Value | 20% more throughput frees 16.7% of a $60M fleet ≈ **$10M a year** | "It freed about a sixth of the fleet, and that capacity absorbed growth we'd otherwise have bought." |
| Attribution | **a = 1** if nobody else would have delivered it; **a = 0.5** if an open-source engine would have given half within a year | "I'll show both cases, because part of this would have come for free." |
| Ongoing cost | 5 engineers × $400K = **$2M a year** | "Five people still run it, so I subtract that every year." |
| Payback | **2.5 years** at a = 1; **about 6.7 years** at a = 0.5 | "Two and a half years if it's all ours; nearly seven if half would have arrived anyway." |
| Verdict | Yes if attribution is near full or the platform keeps absorbing growth | "Next time I'd adopt the open-source piece earlier and spend the team on what nobody else provides." |

Notice that halving attribution does not double the payback; it nearly triples it. The yearly maintenance stays
the same while the gain you can claim shrinks. That is why attribution often decides the answer.

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the numbers (for a Staff-level follow-up)</summary>

- **Build:** 50 × $400K = $20M.
- **Value:** throughput factor g = 1.2 frees 1 − 1 ÷ 1.2 = 0.167 of the fleet; 0.167 × $60M ≈ $10M a year.
- **Ongoing cost:** 5 × $400K = $2M a year.
- **Payback, a = 1:** 20 ÷ (10 − 2) = 2.5 years.
- **Payback, a = 0.5:** 20 ÷ (5 − 2) ≈ 6.7 years.
- **Three-year view:** a = 1 gives 3 × 8 = $24M of net gain against $20M; a = 0.5 gives 3 × 3 = $9M.

</details>

### Two corrections interviewers may probe

**Throughput is not the same size as savings.** If each GPU handles 20% more, the same load needs 1 ÷ 1.2 of the
GPUs, not 80%. One GPU at 100 requests a second becomes 120; 1,000 requests a second needed 10 GPUs and now needs
about 8.33. That is **16.7%** of the fleet, not 20%.

**Freed capacity is a saving only if it is used.** It counts if you stop paying for those GPUs, or if they absorb
growth you would otherwise have bought. If they sit idle, nothing was saved.

Say it like this: "Twenty percent more throughput frees about seventeen percent of the fleet, and it only counts
because that capacity took growth we would otherwise have paid for."

### If the honest answer is no

Say so, and explain what it unblocked. "No on direct return, yes on the launches it enabled, and here is what I
would have scoped down" is more credible than an inflated yes. A useful closing move is to name which parts of the
$20M were essential and which you would replace with an existing tool next time.

## Small-team version

At a company of 50 to 500 people the question becomes "is this AI feature worth what it costs?", asked by the CEO
each quarter. Use the same template with your own numbers. Engineer-time is the largest cost; for a loaded figure,
about **$194K a year** follows from public US Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) wage data, derived in the family
walkthrough. The second cost is the **model API**: a paid web service you send text to and get the model's reply
from, billed per **token** (a small piece of text, roughly ¾ of a word).

The walkthrough's assumed example is a support assistant that answers customer questions before a human sees
them:

| Line | Value (assumed except the loaded cost and API price) |
|---|---|
| Build | 0.5 engineer-years × $194K = **$97K** |
| API calls | about **$6.1K a year** |
| Maintenance | 15% of one engineer ≈ **$29.1K a year** |
| Value | 20% of 20,000 tickets a month resolved without a human, at $6 each = **$288K a year** |
| Payback | about **0.38 years**, roughly 4.6 months |

At a 5% resolution rate, payback stretches to **about 2.6 years**. So the resolution rate is the number to
measure first, not the API bill. Agree the definition of "resolved" with the head of support before building, and
write down the threshold that would make you cut the feature.

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the numbers (for a Staff-level follow-up)</summary>

- **API:** 20,000 conversations × 4 calls × $0.0064 per call (Claude Sonnet 5 list prices with a cached prompt
  prefix, fetched 2026-10-11) = $512 a month ≈ $6.1K a year.
- **Value:** 20,000 × 0.20 × $6 × 12 = $288K a year.
- **Payback:** 97 ÷ (288 − 6.1 − 29.1) = 97 ÷ 252.8 ≈ 0.38 years.
- **At 5%:** 20,000 × 0.05 × $6 × 12 = $72K; 97 ÷ (72 − 6.1 − 29.1) = 97 ÷ 36.8 ≈ 2.6 years.

</details>

Say it like this: "The API bill is about six thousand dollars a year. The whole case rests on how many tickets it
really resolves, so that's what I'd measure first."

## Pitfalls

- **Rejecting the $400K figure.** Arguing about the setup wastes the round; accept it and add your assumptions.
- **No counterfactual.** Counting every gain as the project's ignores what a vendor or open-source release would
  have delivered anyway.
- **Throughput as savings.** A 20% throughput gain frees 16.7% of capacity, and only if you use it.
- **Leaving out maintenance.** The people who keep it running are a cost every year after launch.
- **A yes with no conditions.** It sounds like advocacy, not judgment.

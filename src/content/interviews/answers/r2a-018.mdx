## What they're really asking

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "First I'll check whether the network can do it at all. 200 TB is 1.6 million billion bits. At 10 Gbit/s
> that is about two and a half days in practice, at 1 Gbit/s almost a month, so below about 10 Gbit/s I'd ship
> disks. Then I'd land it in object storage near the GPUs with checksums and a manifest so the copy can resume,
> pack the small files into large shards, and measure whether reading and decoding keep up with the GPUs,
> because idle GPUs are the expensive failure."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer.

The task: move 200 TB of images and video from on-premises storage to a cloud GPU cluster, once, within "a few
days". Then use it to train. The interviewer wants to see whether you do the bandwidth math before naming
tools, and whether you follow the bytes all the way to the GPU.

### What makes this different from a normal data migration

You may have migrated a database: a few TB, copied over a weekend, then verified. Bandwidth was rarely the
question.

200 TB is different in two ways. First, at common link speeds the copy takes days or weeks, so "can the
network do it at all?" is a real question, and the answer may be a truck of disks. Second, the data's job is
to feed GPUs during training. GPUs are expensive and fast. If reading and decoding images can't keep up, the
GPUs sit idle while you pay for them. The candidate named this risk "GPU starvation".

In the first-hand Apple account (edited by PracHub), bandwidth was not given, so the candidate worked the move
at 1, 10 and 100 Gbit/s, weighed shipping disks, and was then pushed toward the training input pipeline. The
only feedback reported is that asking clarifying questions "was itself a point in my favor"
(<Cite id="s-8e08e84623" />).

## Clarify first

- **One-time or recurring?** One-time, per the account. The deadline is "a few days".
- **Link speed, and what else shares it?** Unknown in the account, so work several speeds.
- **File count and sizes.** How many small images versus large videos?
- **Where does it land?** Object storage near the cluster, or straight onto hosts?
- **What reads it afterwards?** Preprocessing, inference, or training?

## Answer

### Words you need first

- **Goodput**: the useful transfer rate you actually get, after protocol overhead and retries. The candidate
  assumed about **70%** of the link's rated speed.
- **Shard**: here, one large file that bundles many small training examples, so you read one 1 GB file instead
  of a thousand 1 MB ones.
- **Data loader**: the code that reads, decodes and batches examples for the GPUs during training.

### Is the network fast enough?

Links are sold in bits per second, so multiply bytes by 8. 200 TB is 1.6 × 10¹⁵ bits.

| Link | At full speed | At 70% | What to say in the interview |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Gbit/s | 18.5 days | **26 days** | "Never makes a few-day window." |
| 10 Gbit/s | 1.85 days | **2.6 days** | "Barely makes three days." |
| 100 Gbit/s | 4.4 hours | **6.3 hours** | "Easy, if we really get it." |

To finish in three days you need about **6.2 Gbit/s of goodput**, which is about 8.8 Gbit/s of link at 70%.
So 10 Gbit/s barely makes it, 1 Gbit/s never does, and below that you ship disks. The candidate describes
transfer appliances holding "tens of TB" each, so you would ship several in parallel.

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the numbers (for a Staff-level follow-up)</summary>

T = bytes × 8 ÷ (link rate × efficiency).

- 1 Gbit/s: 1.6 × 10¹⁵ ÷ 10⁹ = 1.6 × 10⁶ s ≈ 18.5 days; ÷ 0.7 ≈ 26 days.
- 10 Gbit/s: 160,000 s ≈ 1.85 days; about 2.6 days at 70%. 100 Gbit/s: 4.4 hours; 6.3 hours at 70%.
- Three days: 200 × 10¹² bytes ÷ 259,200 s ≈ 0.77 GB/s = 6.2 Gbit/s of goodput; ÷ 0.7 ≈ 8.8 Gbit/s of line.

</details>

### The transfer

```
On-prem storage
   │  many parallel streams (or shipped disks)
   ▼
Object storage near the GPU cluster  ◄── manifest: what has landed, with checksums
   │  pack small files into ~1 GB shards
   ▼
Data loader (read → decode → shuffle → batch)
   │
   ▼
GPUs (training)
```

**Copy with resume.** Use many parallel streams, a checksum per chunk, and a manifest of what has landed, so
the job resumes after a failure instead of starting over. Encrypt in transit and at rest.

**Count objects as well as bytes.** If half the bytes are images averaging an assumed 1 MB, that is 10⁸ (100
million) objects. Object stores limit request rates: at S3's documented 3,500 PUTs per second per prefix (a
**prefix** is the leading part of the key, like a folder), one prefix needs about **7.9 hours** of PUTs
([S3 guidance](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonS3/latest/userguide/optimizing-performance-design-patterns.html), fetched 2026-10-12).
So pack small files into **shards of about 1 GB**, which makes 200,000 shards. The candidate proposed doing
this with WebDataset or TFRecord, two common formats for bundling training examples. Say: "A bandwidth
problem with 100 million small files is also a request-rate problem."

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the numbers (for a Staff-level follow-up)</summary>

- 100 TB ÷ 1 MB = 10⁸ objects; 10⁸ ÷ 3,500 per second ≈ 28,600 s ≈ 7.9 hours.
- 200 TB ÷ 1 GB = 200,000 shards.

</details>

### Feeding the GPUs

The data loader streams shards with prefetching (reading ahead) and a **shuffle buffer**, which mixes
examples from several shards so training doesn't see them in file order.

Find the bottleneck by measuring, in order: how busy the GPUs are, how long each training step waits on the
loader, read throughput, and decode rate. Worked example with assumed numbers: if the GPUs can consume 20,000
images a second at 1 MB each, reads must reach **20 GB/s**. If one CPU core decodes 200 images a second,
decoding needs **100 cores**. Either one starves the GPUs before the GPUs are the limit.

Say: "Before buying more GPUs, I'd check how long each step waits on the data loader."

## Senior vs Staff

<LevelContrast
  title="Bulk move"
  senior={["Works the transfer at several link speeds, picks network or disks, and makes the copy resumable and verified."]}
  staff={["Plans for the consumer from the start: packs shards for training, and names the measurement that shows whether network, decode or GPU is the bottleneck."]}
/>

In plain words: Senior gets the data there safely. Staff designs the copy around what reads the data next.

## Small-team version

Use the cloud provider's managed transfer service or an appliance rather than writing a copier. Your job is
the math, the manifest, and checking counts and checksums at the end. Pack into shards before upload if you
control the source.

Before buying more GPUs for training, check data-loader wait, because idle GPUs are billed anyway. At an
assumed $2 per GPU-hour, 8 GPUs waiting on the loader 30% of the time waste about **$115 a day**
(8 × 0.3 × $2 × 24). If the link gives under about 6 Gbit/s of goodput for a 3-day window, order transfer
appliances instead of tuning the copy.

## Pitfalls

- **Naming S3 and Kafka before computing the transfer time.** The time decides network or disks.
- **Ignoring object count.** Per-request limits stretch what looks like a bandwidth problem.
- **Copying with no manifest.** A failure then means starting over.
- **Assuming the GPUs are the bottleneck.** Measure data-loader wait first.

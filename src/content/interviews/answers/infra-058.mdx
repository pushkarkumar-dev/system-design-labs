## What they're really asking

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "Two hundred models can't all sit on GPUs, and a big model can't be loaded from storage while a user waits,
> because that takes over a minute. So I keep models in tiers: on the GPU, in the server's RAM about a second away,
> on local disk, and in the object store. I pin the busy models, and unload the rest from the GPU by a rent-or-buy
> rule: keep a model while its idle cost is below the cost of reloading it plus the price of a slow first request.
> Big models get unloaded sooner because idling costs eight GPUs, and I choose what to unload so that whole
> machines come free for them."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer.

The interviewer wants to see whether you treat "which models stay loaded" as a cost decision, not a cache-hit-rate
decision.

You've built caches: keep hot items in memory, evict the least recently used. This looks like the same problem, and
that's the trap. Three things are different:

1. **Items are huge and slow to load.** A model's learned numbers (its **weights**) take 16 GB for an
   8-billion-parameter model and about 140 GB for a 70-billion-parameter one (written 8B and 70B). Loading one can
   take from about a second to over a minute.
2. **Items are different sizes, and some need a whole machine.** A big model is split across all 8 GPUs of one
   machine (a **node**), and all 8 must be free together.
3. **An empty slot isn't free.** A loaded model that isn't serving still occupies GPUs that cost dollars an hour.

The prep article this comes from names the signals: constant factors, memory at long context, and why cold-loading
a 70B model "takes real seconds" (<Cite id="s-9597947584" />). The round is decided by whether you can put a price
on an eviction.

### Words you need first

- **HBM.** The GPU's own fast memory (high-bandwidth memory), 80 GB on each GPU here. A model must be in HBM to
  serve.
- **Host DRAM and NVMe.** The server's ordinary RAM, and its local SSD.
- **Residency.** Where a model's weights live right now: HBM, DRAM, NVMe or only the object store.
- **Replica.** One running copy of a model on one or more GPUs.

## Clarify first

- **Size mix and traffic skew.** "I'll assume 150 one-GPU models, 40 two-GPU models, 10 large models each on a whole
  8-GPU node, and about 10 models carrying most traffic."
- **What may a first request to an unloaded model do?** Wait, queue with an estimate, get a 503, or fall back to
  another model?
- **Where do weights live, and how fast can they move?** "I'll assume 2 GB/s from the object store, 6 GB/s from NVMe
  and 20 GB/s per GPU from DRAM."
- **Do rarely used models have any latency promise at all?**

## Answer

### Show why it's a question

One replica of every model needs **310 GPUs**, about 39 nodes, and most of them would sit idle serving the rarely
used models. That cost is the reason to unload anything.

### Use tiers, not one cache

| Where the weights are | Time to start serving a 70B (140 GB) on 8 GPUs | What to say in the interview |
|---|---|---|
| HBM | serving now | "Pinned for the busy models." |
| Host DRAM | **about 0.9 s** | "A miss here is cheap, so I can unload from the GPU aggressively." |
| NVMe | **about 23 s** | "Fine for rarely used models with no promise." |
| Object store only | **about 70 s** | "Never load from here while a user waits." |

One node's assumed 1 TB of DRAM holds **about 62 small models** a second away from serving. That warm tier is what
makes aggressive unloading from the GPU safe.

### Unload by rent-or-buy

Keeping a model loaded while idle is like renting skis: you pay every second. Unloading it is like buying: you pay
once, with a reload and a slow first request for the next user. The classic **ski-rental** rule says keep renting
until you've paid as much as buying would cost, then buy. It never costs more than twice the best choice you could
have made with hindsight.

As a timeout: unload a model after it has been idle for **τ** seconds, where τ is its reload time plus the price of a
slow first request (the **cost of a cold miss**) spread over the GPUs it holds. That price is a product decision. With
an assumed 300 GPU-seconds:

- an 8B model on 1 GPU, falling back to NVMe, stays loaded through **about 5 minutes** of idleness;
- a 70B model on 8 GPUs, falling back to NVMe, stays loaded through only **about 61 seconds**.

Big models are unloaded sooner per idle second, because idling costs eight GPUs. Pin the busy models (the **head**
of the traffic), run τ on the rest (the **tail**), and watch for **thrash**: a model reloaded several times an hour should get a small permanent replica instead.

### Make unloading placement-aware

Freeing eight GPUs helps a big model only if they're on one node. A set of GPUs that must be free together on one
node is called a **gang**. So pick models to unload that free a whole gang, not just the least recently used ones.

### Count memory for requests, not just weights

While a model writes replies, each running request holds a saved summary of its tokens (words, roughly) in GPU
memory, the **KV cache**. At 4,096 tokens per request an 8-GPU 70B replica holds **about 324 requests**. At 128K
tokens it holds **about 10**. A model that "fits" may still be unable to serve its traffic.

Say: "I don't evict by recency alone. I evict by what an idle model costs versus what a reload costs, and I free GPUs
in shapes the big models can use."

## Senior vs Staff

<LevelContrast
  title="Residency"
  senior={["Builds the tiers, derives τ, pins the head and evicts by gang."]}
  staff={["Gets C_miss from the product owner per tier, replays a day of traffic to compare policies, and alerts on thrash."]}
/>

In plain words: Senior gets the mechanism. Staff gets the business input (the price of a slow first request) from
the person who owns it, proves a new policy on recorded traffic before shipping it, and alerts when it misbehaves.

## Small-team version

A small company has a handful of models, not 200. Keep them all loaded, or put several fine-tuned variants of one
base model on one GPU as LoRA adapters (small add-on files that customise a shared model), which removes eviction
entirely. If traffic is bursty and low, rent per-token (serverless) endpoints and let the provider solve residency;
check how its cold starts behave for your model.

Revisit when idle GPU-hours on rarely used models become a real line on the bill. One always-on H100 at about $3.95
an hour (Modal's per-second price, fetched 2026-10-11) costs about **$2,884 a month** (3.95 × 730), so a
model that serves a few requests a day costs that much whether it's used or not.

## Pitfalls

- **LRU over 200 models without load times.** Recency ignores that one reload takes a second and another takes a
  minute.
- **Loading large models from the object store on demand.** The first user waits over a minute.
- **Counting only weights.** At long context, the KV cache sets capacity.
- **Freeing scattered GPUs.** Eight free GPUs on eight nodes can't host one big model.

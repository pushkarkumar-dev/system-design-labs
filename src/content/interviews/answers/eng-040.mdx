## What they're really asking

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "I'll build two small pass/fail judges, one for faithfulness and one for relevance, because they are different
> failures. I'll tune them on half the labelled pairs and report on the other half. For each judge I'll report how
> many real failures it catches, how many good answers it wrongly fails, and an agreement score that corrects for
> luck, all with intervals. And I'll say plainly that 30 labels make the judge promising, not proven."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer.

### Words you need first

A **large language model (LLM)** reads text and writes text. Many products first look up documents that might
help and paste them into the LLM's input (the **prompt**). Those pasted documents are the **retrieved context**.

An **LLM judge** is a second LLM call that grades the first one's answer against written rules, the **rubric**.
A **human label** is the grade a person gave the same answer.

The take-home asks you to grade two things:

- **Faithfulness**: does the answer stick to the retrieved context, or does it claim things the context doesn't
  support?
- **Relevance**: does the answer address the question that was asked?

### Why this isn't a normal test

In normal code you test against an exact expected value, and the test is trusted because anyone can read it.
Here neither holds. Two different answers can both be correct, so you can't string-compare. And the grader is
itself an LLM, which makes mistakes in predictable directions (it tends to like longer answers, for example).

So the take-home is not really "write a judge prompt". That part takes ten minutes. It is "show me how often your
judge is wrong, and in which direction, and what that means for how it can be used". The reviewer grades the
numbers next to the judge, and your honesty about how far 30 labels can take you.

The techinterview.org applied-AI prep guide describes this as "a common take-home": a prompt, "thirty sample
input/output pairs", and a request for an automated faithfulness and relevance eval calibrated against the
provided human labels. It is the guide's own description, not a candidate report. The guide says the real check is
whether you would trust the judge before testing it against people: "The right answer is no"
(<Cite id="s-7d03a572e0" />).

## Clarify first

If you can ask questions (in a live version or by email), these are the ones that change your design. In a
take-home with no one to ask, state your assumption for each in the README.

- **Are the labels pass/fail, or scores?** "If they're 1-to-10 scores, I'll convert them to pass/fail per
  criterion. Pass/fail is easier to check and to agree on."
- **Who labelled them?** "One expert, or several? If several, I can measure how well they agree with each other."
- **How many failures are there?** "If only 3 of the 30 are unfaithful, everything I say about catching failures
  rests on 3 examples."
- **Which judge model can I use, and can I pin its version?** "A judge that silently changes model version
  changes its error rates."
- **Is any of the 30 held out?** "If not, I'll hold out half myself and explain why."

## Answer

### The example we'll use

Picture a support chatbot. For each customer question it retrieves help articles and writes an answer. One of the
30 pairs (illustrative):

| Part | Content |
|---|---|
| Question | "Can I return my headphones after 30 days?" |
| Retrieved context | "Returns are accepted within 14 days of delivery. Opened audio products cannot be returned." |
| Answer | "Yes, you have 30 days to return any product." |
| Human label (faithfulness) | **fail** |

Across all 30 pairs, the human marked **9 fail and 21 pass** for faithfulness. We'll follow those numbers.

### Step 1: two judges, not one

Write one judge per **failure mode**, meaning one specific way an answer can go wrong. Unfaithful and
irrelevant are two different failure modes. Each judge returns a **binary** grade: pass or fail.

- The **faithfulness judge** gets the retrieved context and the answer. It returns pass/fail plus a list of the
  claims it couldn't find support for. That list makes every "fail" easy to check by eye.
- The **relevance judge** gets the question and the answer. It doesn't need the context at all.

Use plain code wherever code can decide. If the answer cites a source, a few lines can check that the source is
really in the retrieved context. Save the LLM for the judgments that need reading.

Why split? One judge per failure mode is easier to compare with labels, as Hamel Husain and Shreya Shankar's
[evals FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/) argues. A single judge scoring "overall quality" mixes two
questions, and you can't tell which one it got wrong.

Say it like this: "Each judge answers one yes/no question. That's what makes it measurable."

### Step 2: split the 30

You need labels for two different jobs. You look at the **dev** set while you improve the judge's prompt. You keep
the **test** set untouched until the end and report numbers on it. If you tune and report on the same pairs, you
will keep editing until the judge gets those particular pairs right, and the number you report will be too
optimistic. This is the same reason you don't test a model on its training data.

With 30 pairs, tune on about 15 and report on the other 15. Or rotate: tune on some, test on the rest, and repeat
with different splits. Either way, say in the README that 30 is too few to do both well.

### Step 3: report what kinds of mistakes the judge makes

Treat "fail" as the thing you are hunting for, the way a spam filter hunts for spam. The judge can then be wrong
in two ways:

- It can **miss** a real failure. The share of real failures it catches is the **true-positive rate (TPR)**.
- It can **raise a false alarm** on a good answer. The share of good answers it correctly passes is the
  **true-negative rate (TNR)**.

Say the judge, run on our 30 pairs, gives this:

| | Human said **fail** (9) | Human said **pass** (21) |
|---|---|---|
| Judge said **fail** | 7 caught | 2 false alarms |
| Judge said **pass** | 2 missed | 19 correctly passed |

That is **TPR 7 of 9, about 78%**, and **TNR 19 of 21, about 90%**. Judge and human agree on 26 of 30.

Why not just report that 26 of 30 (87%) as **accuracy**? Because it hides which mistake is happening. If 10% of
answers fail, a judge that passes everything is 90% accurate and catches nothing.

### Step 4: correct the agreement for luck

Some agreement happens by chance. Here the human says "pass" 70% of the time. A judge that guessed "pass" 70% of
the time would agree often just by luck.

**Cohen's kappa** (κ) removes that luck. It is 1 for perfect agreement and 0 for no better than chance. For our
example, chance alone would give 58% agreement; the judge got 86.7%; that works out to **κ = 0.68**. A judge that
always says "pass" would agree 70% of the time and score **κ = 0**.

### Step 5: put intervals on every rate

"78%" rests on 9 examples. A **confidence interval** is the range of true rates consistent with what you saw. For
small pass/fail counts the usual choice is the **Wilson interval**. Computed by a short script run on 2026-10-12
(shown in the family walkthrough):

| Measure | Value | 95% interval |
|---|---|---|
| Agreement | 26 of 30 = 86.7% | 70% to 95% |
| TPR | 7 of 9 = 78% | **45% to 94%** |
| TNR | 19 of 21 = 90% | 71% to 97% |

Read the TPR row out loud: the judge catches somewhere between half and nearly all real failures. And these numbers
use all 30 pairs to keep the arithmetic simple. On a 15-pair test half there are only 4 or 5 failures, so the real
intervals are even wider.

- **Chance agreement:** the human fails 9 of 30 (30%) and the judge fails 7 + 2 = 9 of 30 (30%). So
  p_e = 0.7 × 0.7 + 0.3 × 0.3 = 0.49 + 0.09 = 0.58.
- **Always-pass judge:** p_e = 1.0 × 0.7 = 0.7 and p_o = 0.7, so κ = 0.
- **Wilson interval, 7 of 9:** p = 0.778, z = 1.96, n = 9. Centre = (p + z² ÷ 2n) ÷ (1 + z² ÷ n) ≈ 0.695;
  half-width ≈ 0.242. So **0.45 to 0.94**.

</details>

### Step 6: write the README a reviewer wants

This is where most of the marks are. Include:

1. **The disagreements.** Every pair where judge and human differ, and what each one taught you about the rubric.
   For example, a partial contradiction ("30 days" for most products but correct for one) may show the rubric
   never said whether a partly wrong answer fails.
2. **The biases you tested.** Did the judge pass longer answers more often? If you compared answers in pairs, did
   the winner change when you swapped their order?
3. **The honest conclusion.** "The judge is promising, but it can't block releases yet. Its catch rate is known
   only to within about ±25 points."
4. **What you'd do next.** Label at least 50 failing and 50 passing examples per criterion, as the FAQ recommends,
   and keep a test split never used for tuning. With 50 failures and the same catch rate, the interval narrows to
   about 65% to 87%.

Say it like this: "Ready to be a dashboard signal, not a release blocker. Here's the labelling plan that would
change that."

## Small-team version

This take-home is exactly what a small team does in production. One domain expert labels. You build the judge as
a few API calls. The agreement report decides whether the judge is trusted. Buy nothing for this.

What it costs:

- **Labels:** 100 items is about **3.3 hours** of expert time, about **$310**.
- **Judge calls:** about **half a cent each**, about **$4.75 per thousand**.

The labels cost more than the tokens. Add a second annotator, and measure kappa between the two people, when the
domain is regulated or the expert's calls are contested.

  $0.0045 + $0.00025 = **$0.00475** a call, about **$4.75** per thousand.

## Pitfalls

- **Reporting accuracy only.** It hides class balance and chance agreement; a judge that passes everything can
  look accurate.
- **Tuning and reporting on the same 30 pairs.** The judge overfits them, so the reported number is too
  optimistic.
- **One judge scoring both criteria on a 1–10 scale.** You can't tell which criterion it got wrong, and scores
  are harder to agree on than pass/fail.
- **Calling the judge "validated" on 30 labels.** A catch rate known to ±25 points is a starting point, not a
  validation.

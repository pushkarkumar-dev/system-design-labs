## What they're really asking

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "I'll split indexing from serving. Indexing pulls changes from each source into a durable log and builds a
> keyword index and a vector index, plus a copy of who may read each document. Serving searches both indexes,
> merges the results by rank, reranks them, and applies permissions. On access control I pick a side: filter
> inside the search, not after ranking, and pay for it by keeping permissions beside the index and updating them
> in their own fast lane. For freshness I use change events, and after an outage I replay permission changes and
> deletes before content."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer.

The interviewer wants to see whether you can build a search system over data you don't own, whose access rules
you don't own either.

A normal internal search runs over your own database, where permissions are checked on every read. Enterprise
search is different. The documents live in Drive, Slack, the wiki and email. You copy them into your own indexes
so you can search them quickly, and you copy their sharing rules too. Every copy can fall behind the original.
The two questions that follow are where the permission check runs, and how fresh the copies stay, including
after a source has been unreachable for hours.

The source is a Glean prep guide (published 2026-07-20), which says candidates commonly report this round but
cites no reports, so treat it as low confidence (<Cite id="s-768711771a" />). It wants answers that "start by
splitting indexing from serving", says interviewers push on "Where do you enforce access control?", and says
"Being able to name that tension, and pick a side with a reason, is most of the round."

## Clarify first

- **Which sources, and how do they report changes?** "A **connector** pulls documents and permissions from one
  source. Does each source push change events, or can we only list files and compare?"
- **Permission model.** "Inherited folder sharing, nested groups, 'anyone with the link'?"
- **Freshness targets, split in two.** "How fast must an edit show up, and separately, how fast must a revoked
  share take effect?" The guide's target for edits is "within minutes, not on the next daily crawl".
- **Load and latency.** "Queries per second at peak, and the time budget for results."

## Answer

### Words you need first

- **Keyword search**: the inverted index you know from Lucene or Elasticsearch, scored with a formula called
  **BM25**. It finds exact words, names and ticket ids.
- **Embedding**: a list of numbers a small model computes from a passage, so passages with similar meaning get
  similar vectors. **Vector search** finds passages close in meaning to the query, even with different words.
  Combining the two is **hybrid retrieval**.
- **Chunk**: a passage of a few hundred words. Both indexes store chunks, not whole documents.
- **ACL** (access control list): the users and groups (**principals**) allowed to read a document.

### Indexing versus serving

```
INDEXING (throughput-bound, background)
  Drive · Slack · Wiki connectors
        │ change events, each with a source version
        ▼
  Change log ── one cursor per connector
        │ content lane                    │ ACL + delete lane
        ▼                                 │ (never waits)
  Parse ─► chunk ─► keyword index         │
                 └► embed ─► vector index │
                                          ▼
                                     ACL store
SERVING (latency-bound, user waits)
  Query ─► user's groups ─► keyword + vector search, filtered ─► merge by rank ─► rerank ─► results
```

**Indexing** is asynchronous and measured in throughput. Connectors write change events into a durable log with a
cursor per connector. Workers parse, chunk, and write both a keyword index and a vector index.

**Serving** is measured in latency. It sends the query to both indexes, merges the two lists, reranks, applies
permissions, and returns.

Splitting them means a backlog makes results older without making queries slower. Say it like this: "Indexing
can fall behind; serving must not slow down because of it."

### Keyword plus vector, merged by rank

In the guide's words, "BM25-style keyword matching catches exact terms and names, semantic vectors catch
meaning". The two return scores on different scales, so merge by rank, not by score. The usual method is
**reciprocal rank fusion (RRF)**: each passage scores 1 ÷ (60 + its rank) in each list, and the sums decide the
order. Then a **reranker** (a slower model that reads the query and one passage together) reorders the top few
dozen.

The keyword side has to be decent for hybrid to pay. In the rag lab, splitting text on whitespace gave hybrid no
gain over vectors alone on the SciFact dataset, while splitting on word characters (`\w+`) reached
<LabNumber lab="rag" metric="BM25 tokenizer (SciFact)" /> **hit@3** (the share of questions with a correct
passage in the top 3).

### Access control: pick a side with a reason

Follow one example. Dana, a sales engineer at an invented company called Northwind, searches "Kestrel discount".
The best match is a document in a folder only the group `sales-leads` can open. Dana was removed from that group
this morning. Three places could stop her seeing it:

| Where the check runs | What happens to Dana's search | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| After the results are shown (hide links) | The snippet already showed the discount | Leaks |
| After ranking: take the top 20, drop forbidden ones | If 18 of 20 are forbidden for her, she gets 2 results; anything that saw the top 20 first (a cache, result counts) can leak; a timeout "fallback" can skip the check | "simple and wrong at scale", in the guide's words |
| Inside the search | Forbidden chunks are never scored; she gets the best 20 she may read | The side to pick |

The cost of filtering inside the search is that permissions must sit beside the index and change fast. The guide
puts it as permission data that "has to update the moment someone's permissions change". That is why ACL changes
get their own lane that never waits behind embedding: re-tagging a chunk is cheap, re-embedding it is not.

Choose the mechanism by how much the user can see. Large vector indexes use a graph (**HNSW**) that finds near
neighbours approximately by walking a few hundred nodes. A filter applied only after that walk can leave too few
results. So: if the user can see a small slice, compare against every allowed chunk exactly; if they see most of
the company, use a graph search that applies the filter during the walk.

Keep a final re-check against the live ACL store for the few dozen results, treat any error as "deny" (this is
called **failing closed**), and watch how many results it drops. A rising count means the ACL copy is falling
behind.

Say it like this: "I filter during retrieval, because filtering after ranking starves narrow users and leaks
through anything that runs first. The price is fresh ACLs beside the index, so permission changes get their own
fast lane."

### Freshness and the six-hour outage

The guide calls freshness "the other lever". Prefer change events over periodic crawls. A source you can only poll
shows a deletion as an absence, so also reconcile the full list of document ids periodically to catch deletions.

Now the guide's failure case: a connector goes down "for six hours and comes back with a backlog to replay".
Walk the recovery in order:

1. **Resume from the cursor.** The log remembers the last event committed for that connector.
2. **Permission changes and deletes first.** They are cheap and they are the security risk. Dana's removal from
   `sales-leads`, if it happened during the outage, lands before any content.
3. **Content after, keyword first.** Edited and new documents become searchable by keyword before their
   embeddings are ready.
4. **Duplicates and reordering are harmless.** Writes are upserts keyed by (tenant, document id, source version),
   and an older version never overwrites a newer one.
5. **Show the lag.** Citations say "synced 6 h ago" until the connector catches up, and an alert fires on lag.

One decision isn't yours to make alone. While a connector is down, its revocations are invisible to you. Agree
with the customer whether that source keeps serving with a banner, or is hidden past a threshold.

## Small-team version

For an internal search over a few sources, start with content every employee can already open, in Postgres:
built-in full-text search for keywords and the pgvector extension for vectors. The connector work dominates the
effort: polling plus id reconciliation for each source, a cursor table, and a lag metric.

Model cost is small at that size. For 300 employees, 150,000 documents and 10 questions a person a day (all
assumed), the family walkthrough works out about **$6** to embed everything and **$162** (gpt-5-mini) to
**$1,080** (Claude Sonnet 5) per 30 days for answers, at list prices fetched 2026-10-11.

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the costs</summary>

- 150,000 documents × 5 chunks of 400 tokens = 750,000 chunks ≈ 300M tokens to embed; at $0.02 per 1M tokens
  (text-embedding-3-small) that is $6.
- 300 × 10 = 3,000 questions a day at about 4,000 input and 400 output tokens. Claude Sonnet 5 at $2 / $10 per 1M:
  $0.008 + $0.004 = $0.012 each, $36 a day, about $1,080 per 30 days. gpt-5-mini at $0.25 / $2: $0.0018 each, about
  $162 per 30 days.

</details>

Before buying a product instead, ask the vendor in writing two things: how it enforces each source's permissions
per user, and how long a revocation takes to apply.

## Pitfalls

- **Access control as a detail for the end.** It decides the index layout, so it comes first.
- **One queue for content and permissions.** A burst of edits then delays every revocation.
- **Assuming the next crawl handles deletions.** A polling connector sees a deletion only as an absence; you need
  reconciliation.
- **Adding raw scores from two engines.** They aren't on the same scale; merge by rank.

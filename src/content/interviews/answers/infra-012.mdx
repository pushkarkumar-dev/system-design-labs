## What they're really asking

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "At 4,000 GPUs a failure every 12 or 13 hours is normal, so I treat it as a cost to minimize, not a rare event.
> The whole job stops when one node dies, so the cost is everyone's time: the work since the last checkpoint plus
> the restart. I'd detect crashes in seconds with heartbeats and hangs with a step watchdog, checkpoint in the
> background every few minutes, and keep a spare node ready, which brings each failure down to about six minutes of
> the cluster. And I'd ask whether we need 500 nodes at all."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer.

The interviewer wants to see you put numbers on a failure: how often it happens, how fast you notice, and how much
work it throws away. The last follow-up, "do you even need 500 nodes?", tests whether you will question the
requirement.

### What makes this different from a service losing a node

When a web service loses a node, the load balancer stops sending it traffic and the other nodes carry on. Users
barely notice.

A large training job is different. It is one program running on every GPU in lockstep. Each **training step** (one
round of learning on a batch of data) ends with all GPUs exchanging results. If one node dies, every other GPU is
stuck halfway through that exchange. The whole job stops and has to restart from the last saved copy of its state,
called a **checkpoint**. So a failure costs the time of all 4,000 GPUs, not one node's.

### Words you need first

- **Rank**: one training process, one per GPU. Ranks call each other to exchange data.
- **Collective**: an exchange where every rank contributes and every rank waits for the result. If one rank never
  arrives, the rest wait.
- **NCCL**: NVIDIA's library that runs those collectives on GPUs.
- **Straggler**: a rank that is alive but slow, so everyone waits for it every step.

The question was reported by an NVIDIA senior ML engineer candidate, who lists the follow-ups: how fast you detect
it, how you recover, "At what point do you restart versus let the cluster catch up?", and "Do you even need 500
nodes, and are you using them efficiently?" (<Cite id="s-79598dd069" />).

## Clarify first

- **GPUs per node?** "I'll assume 8, so 4,000 GPUs."
- **How is the model split?** "If each model copy spans several nodes, one dead node takes down a whole copy, not
  just itself."
- **Spares?** "Are spare nodes on standby, and how long does a replacement take?"
- **History?** "What does a checkpoint cost today, and how often have nodes failed? If we don't know, I'll use
  Meta's published Llama 3 rate as an order of magnitude."

## Answer

### How often it happens

Meta reports **419 unexpected interruptions in 54 days** while training Llama 3 on up to 16K H100 GPUs
([Llama 3 paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21783)). That is about 2 × 10⁻⁵ per GPU-hour. For a 4,000-GPU job, the
whole job is interrupted about **every 12.7 hours**.

Say it like this: "Failure is routine at this size. I design for a failure twice a day."

### Detect, by kind of failure

There are four kinds, and each needs its own detector.

| Kind | What it looks like | Detector | Time to notice |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Dead** | a process exits or a node stops answering | heartbeats from a health agent on each node | seconds |
| **Hung** | a peer died inside a collective; everyone else blocks | a step watchdog at about 3× the median step time | about 3 step times |
| **Slow** | a straggler; nothing fails, everything slows | per-rank step time and time spent waiting | after a few steps |
| **Wrong** | the loss jumps or becomes NaN (not a number), or a GPU computes bad values silently | alarms on loss and gradient size; several retained checkpoints | varies |

The hung case is the trap. PyTorch's default NCCL timeout is **10 minutes**. Ten minutes of 4,000 GPUs is
**667 GPU-hours** of waiting, so don't rely on it. Add your own watchdog.

### Recover

Restart from the latest checkpoint. Each rank saved its own piece, so the restore is parallel. Make the restart fast:
a **hot spare** node that has already joined the job's startup pool, and the newest checkpoint held in a peer node's
RAM as well as in object storage.

### How often to checkpoint, and what each failure costs

Checkpoint too rarely and every failure throws away hours of work. Checkpoint too often and the pauses add up. A
classic formula (**Young's approximation**) balances the two: the best interval is about the square root of
(2 × the pause per checkpoint × the mean time between failures).

Follow one failure through two designs. The pause and restart times are assumptions, not measurements:

| Design | Pause per checkpoint | Restart | Best interval | Total time lost | One failure costs |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Synchronous checkpoints | 30 s | 10 min | 28 min | about 4.9% | 24 min of the cluster |
| Background checkpoints, hot spare | 2 s | 2 min | 7 min | about 1.2% | **5.6 min** |

A synchronous checkpoint stops training while it writes. A background (asynchronous) one stops only for the copy
into the machine's ordinary RAM, then writes to storage while training continues.

### Restart, or let the cluster catch up?

"Elastic" training sounds as if surviving workers keep going. With PyTorch's launcher, torchrun, they don't: it
stops and restarts all workers on any failure or membership change
([torchrun docs](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/2.14/elastic/run.html)). So every option costs one restart. The choice
is only whether to wait for a replacement.

The rule: **if a spare will be ready sooner than a restart takes, wait for it.** Otherwise restart without the failed
model copy. If the job runs, say, 62 model copies, losing one costs
**1.6%** throughput. Keep the global batch the same by having each copy
process more data before each update (**gradient accumulation**), so the researchers' setup doesn't change. Then grow
back at a later checkpoint.

Say it like this: "Every path pays one restart. I wait for the spare if it's faster than a restart; otherwise I
drop one copy, keep the batch, and grow back later."

### Do you even need 500 nodes?

This is the Staff test. Training compute is about 6 × parameters × tokens floating-point operations. Divide by
what the GPUs achieve and you get the time. For example, fine-tuning a 70B model on 1B tokens is about
**295 H100-hours** at an assumed 40% utilization: **37 hours on one 8-GPU node**, and under 5 minutes of compute
on 4,000 GPUs, where startup and failures would dominate. Then measure utilization on the cluster you already have
before asking for more.

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the numbers (for a Staff-level follow-up)</summary>

- 6 × 70 × 10⁹ × 10⁹ = 4.2 × 10²⁰ FLOPs. One H100 at 40% of its dense bf16 peak (989.5 TFLOPS) does
  3.96 × 10¹⁴ FLOP/s → about 1.06 × 10⁶ s ≈ 295 GPU-hours. ÷ 8 GPUs ≈ 37 hours. ÷ 4,000 GPUs ≈ 4.4 minutes.
- NCCL hang: 10 min × 4,000 GPUs = 40,000 GPU-minutes ≈ 667 GPU-hours.
- Losing one of 62 model copies: 1 ÷ 62 ≈ 1.6%.

</details>

## Small-team version

Small teams don't run 500-node jobs. A one-machine fine-tune that fails just restarts from its last checkpoint.

What still applies:

- Checkpoint to object storage every few hundred steps, and include the data position.
- Test the resume path once, before you need it.
- Put a timeout on the job so a hang doesn't burn a rented machine overnight.

The timeout is about money. A 12-hour hang on an 8×H100 machine at Lambda's $3.99 per GPU-hour (fetched
2026-10-11) costs 8 × $3.99 × 12 ≈ **$383**. That is more than the whole LoRA fine-tune it was meant to run (the
family walkthrough prices that at $9–$29). Default to a managed fine-tuning API or one rented machine. Fault
tolerance across many machines only matters once a job spans many nodes for days.

## Pitfalls

- **Relying on the 10-minute NCCL timeout as your detector.** It wastes 667 GPU-hours per hang at this size.
- **Calling torchrun "elastic" as if survivors keep going.** It stops and restarts every worker.
- **Checkpointing every few hours by habit.** The interval should come from the pause and the failure rate.
- **Never questioning the 500.** The cheapest node is the one you didn't need.

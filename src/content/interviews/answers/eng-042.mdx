## What they're really asking

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "I treat the judge as a classifier, not an oracle. I compare it with human labels on examples I never tuned it
> on, and report how many real failures it catches and how many false alarms it raises. I hold it to the level at
> which people agree with each other, I test its known biases, and I re-check it whenever its model, its prompt or
> the traffic changes. Until then it doesn't get to make decisions."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer.

### Words you need first

A **large language model (LLM)** reads text and writes text. An **LLM judge** (the interview phrase is
**LLM-as-judge**) is a second LLM call whose job is to grade the first one's output against written rules, the
**rubric**. For example: "Does this answer claim anything the help article doesn't say? Answer pass or fail."

A **human label** is the grade a person gives the same output. The person is an **annotator**.

### What makes this different

In backend work, a test has an exact expected value and you trust the test because you can read it. An LLM judge
is neither exact nor fully trustworthy. It is a model making a judgment call, and it has habits: it tends to like
longer answers, for example.

So "is the judge reliable?" is the same question you'd ask about a new health check before letting it page
anyone: how often does it fire when it shouldn't, and how often does it stay quiet when it should fire? You
answer it by measuring, not by reading the judge's prompt.

The techinterview.org applied-AI prep guide says that the moment you say "LLM-as-judge", a good interviewer asks
how you know the judge is any good. The answer it says they want is calibration: compare with human labels,
measure agreement, tighten the rubric (<Cite id="s-7d03a572e0" />). That is a prep guide's description, not a
candidate report.

## Clarify first

- **What will the judge decide?** "Blocking a release needs a judge that catches most failures. A trend
  dashboard can live with more noise. Ranking candidate prompts is different again."
- **Pass/fail or a score?** "Pass/fail is much easier to validate than a 1-to-10 score."
- **Are there human labels, and do labellers agree with each other?** "If two people disagree often, the judge
  can't be held to more than they manage."
- **Is the judge model pinned?** "A model alias can change underneath me and silently change the judge's error
  rates."

## Answer

### The example we'll use

A support chatbot answers customer questions using help articles. A judge checks **faithfulness**: does the
answer stick to the help articles, or does it make things up? A support lead labelled 30 answers: 9 fail and 21
pass. The judge was run on the same 30. (These are illustrative numbers, the same ones used in the family
walkthrough.)

| | Human said **fail** (9) | Human said **pass** (21) |
|---|---|---|
| Judge said **fail** | 7 caught | 2 false alarms |
| Judge said **pass** | 2 missed | 19 correctly passed |

Judge and human agree on 26 of 30. Is that judge reliable? It depends on five things.

### 1. It is checked against human labels on held-out data

Split the labels. You look at the **dev** set while you improve the judge's prompt. You keep the **test** set
untouched and report on it at the end. Hamel Husain and Shreya Shankar's
[evals FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/) warns that tuning on the same labels you report on overfits
the judge, even if those labels never appear in its prompt. You keep editing until it gets those particular
examples right.

### 2. Its error rates fit the decision

Count the two kinds of mistake separately. Treat "fail" as the thing you are hunting for.

- The **true-positive rate (TPR)** is the share of real failures the judge catches. Here, 7 of 9, about **78%**.
- The **true-negative rate (TNR)** is the share of good answers the judge correctly passes. Here, 19 of 21, about
  **90%**.

A release blocker needs a high TPR, or bad answers ship. But a low TNR means many false alarms, and people learn to
ignore the judge.

Don't report plain **accuracy** (26 of 30). It hides which mistake is happening: if 10% of answers fail, a judge
that passes everything is 90% accurate and catches nothing.

And put an interval on each rate. "78%" rests on 9 examples. A 95% **confidence interval** (the range of true
rates consistent with what you saw) runs from about **45% to 94%**. That's not enough to block releases.

Say it like this: "I report TPR and TNR with intervals, because accuracy can look great for a judge that never
fails anything."

### 3. Its agreement approaches human agreement

Some agreement happens by luck. **Cohen's kappa** (κ) corrects for it: 1 is perfect agreement, 0 is no better than
chance. Our judge scores **κ = 0.68**.

Now compute the same kappa between two people who labelled the same items independently. That is
**inter-annotator agreement**, and it is the ceiling. If two support leads only reach κ = 0.7 with each other, a
judge at 0.68 is doing about as well as a person. If they reach 0.9, it isn't.

The MT-Bench paper ([Zheng et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685), 2023) is the classic evidence that this can
work. It reported GPT-4 reaching "over 80% agreement, the same level of agreement between humans" on chat
preferences. That is evidence for the method, not for your judge on your data.

### 4. Its known biases are tested

The guide lists three habits: judges favour longer answers, favour whichever answer comes first when comparing
two, and lean toward text from their own model family. Test each one:

- **Length:** check whether the judge's pass rate rises with answer length.
- **Order (position):** when comparing two answers, run each pair in both orders. Count a win only if it holds both
  ways.
- **Own model family:** compare agreement on outputs from the judge's own family with agreement on others.

### 5. It is re-checked

On a schedule, and whenever the judge's model, its prompt or the mix of inputs changes. A judge's error rates are
not a fixed property. They belong to one model version, one prompt and one kind of traffic.

### Bonus: correct the production numbers

Once you know TPR and TNR, you can correct the judge's raw failure rate on live traffic. The FAQ describes this
too. Say the judge fails 12% of live answers, with TPR 0.85 and TNR 0.95. The true failure rate is about **9%,
not 12%**. The gap is false alarms.

Say it like this: "The judge's raw number includes its false alarms. Knowing its error rates, I can work back to
the real failure rate."

<summary>The arithmetic behind the numbers (for a Staff-level follow-up)</summary>

<Formula
  title="Corrected failure rate"
  expr="f_true ≈ (f_judge + TNR − 1) ÷ (TPR + TNR − 1)"
  vars={[
    { sym: "f_judge", meaning: "share the judge fails" },

- **Where it comes from:** f_judge = TPR × f_true + (1 − TNR) × (1 − f_true). Solve for f_true.
- **Kappa:** observed agreement 26 ÷ 30 = 0.867. Both raters say pass 70% of the time, so chance agreement is
  0.7 × 0.7 + 0.3 × 0.3 = 0.58. κ = (0.867 − 0.58) ÷ (1 − 0.58) = **0.68**.
- **Wilson interval, 7 of 9:** about **0.45 to 0.94** (worked in the family walkthrough).

</details>

## Senior vs Staff

  title="Judge reliability"

/>

In plain words: a Senior answer measures the judge properly. A Staff answer also sets the bar from how well people
agree, uses the judge's error rates to fix production numbers, and treats a change to the judge like a change to a
release gate.

## Small-team version

You will rely on judges because you have no labelling team. The cheapest insurance is about 100 labels per judge
from one domain expert, about **3.3 hours** and **$310**, plus a monthly re-check on 20 to 30 fresh labels. Build
the judges and the agreement report yourself; there is nothing to buy.

Running two judges on every one of an assumed 600 answers a day costs about **$5.70 a day**, about **$170 a
month**. If volume grows 10× or more, judge a sample instead of everything.

  1,200 calls a day × $0.00475 ≈ **$5.70**; × 30 ≈ **$170 a month**.

## Pitfalls

- **"We used GPT-4 as a judge" with no agreement numbers.** Nobody can tell whether the judge's scores mean
  anything.
- **Accuracy on imbalanced labels.** When most answers pass, a judge that passes everything looks accurate.
- **Swapping the judge model without re-validation.** Its error rates change, which breaks every comparison with
  historical scores.
- **Holding the judge above the human bar.** If people only agree moderately, no judge can do better on that
  label.

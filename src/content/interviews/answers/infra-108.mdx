## What they're really asking

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "First I'd check for waste: idle reservations and badly used GPUs are often cheaper to recover than to argue
> over. Then I'd rank projects by how much value we lose if each one is late, per GPU-hour it needs, with deadlines
> as hard limits. I'd give guaranteed quota only to committed deadlines, put everything else in a shared pool where
> jobs can be paused, and keep a small slice for experiments. I'd publish the rule, report usage weekly, take back
> idle quota, and name one person who decides the cases the rule gets wrong."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer, so you can say it with understanding.

This is a political problem dressed as a technical one. Several teams want the same scarce compute, and each
believes its project matters most. The interviewer wants to see you turn that fight into a rule other people can
predict and accept. A good answer has two parts: a **criterion** (how you rank) and a **mechanism** (how the
ranking turns into who gets which machines).

### Why this is not normal capacity planning

You have probably shared servers or a database cluster between teams. Usually you can buy more within weeks, and
one more server is cheap. GPUs (the specialised chips that train and run AI models) are different in two ways.
They are expensive to rent or buy. And the question assumes they are scarce: you may not be able to get more
this quarter, whatever you are willing to pay. So the question is really about saying no to some teams in a way
they can live with.

### Words you need first

- **GPU-hour**: one GPU used for one hour. The usual unit for asking for, buying and counting compute.
- **Quota**: a reserved share of the pool that belongs to one team.
- **Preemption**: a higher-priority job pausing a lower-priority one to take its GPUs. A job survives this if it
  saves a **checkpoint** (a snapshot of its progress it can resume from) or can simply be retried.
- **Utilization**: how much of its share a team actually used.

The question was reported from the behavioral round of an NVIDIA onsite for a senior software engineer on LLM
applications (apps built on large language models, the kind behind ChatGPT and Claude). It described a distributed
computing environment with limited resources and several projects and asked how you would prioritize and allocate
them (<Cite id="s-7dd221b925" />, about 2025). The candidate noted it had a technical element.

## Clarify first

- **What is scarce?** GPU-hours in general, one specific GPU type, jobs that need many GPUs wired closely together,
  or a monthly API budget?
- **What are the deadlines?** Which projects have external commitments, and which are internal goals?
- **Who settles disputes?** You, or a named leader?
- **Can some work be paused?** Training jobs that checkpoint and offline batch jobs usually can.

## Answer

### The example we will follow

Assume a quarter with more requests than GPUs. Two projects matter for the ranking:

- **Project X** is the big headline project. If it is late, the company loses an agreed **10 units** of value. It
  needs **2,000 GPU-hours**.
- **Project Y** is smaller. If it is late, the company loses **4 units**. It needs **400 GPU-hours**.

A "unit" can be revenue, a launch, or a customer commitment. What matters is that everyone agrees the estimates
are in the same currency. All of these numbers are assumptions.

### Step 1: increase supply before rationing

Before you ration, look for waste. Common sources: reservations nobody is using, jobs running at low utilization,
expensive GPUs running small models that a cheaper GPU could handle, and inference (running a model to answer
requests) that could batch requests together but doesn't. Recovering idle capacity is often cheaper than arguing
over it, and it lowers the temperature of the whole discussion.

Say it like this: "Before I decide who loses, I'd check how much of what we have is actually being used."

### Step 2: the criterion

Rank by value at risk per unit of compute, with deadlines as hard limits.

In words: Y protects twice as much value per GPU-hour as X, so Y goes first, even though X is the bigger project.
This surprises people, which is why you need to show the arithmetic: 10 ÷ 2,000 = 0.005 and 4 ÷ 400 = 0.01.

Say it like this: "I'd rank by value lost if late, per GPU-hour. The biggest project isn't always the best use of
the next GPU."

### Step 3: the mechanism

```
            Shared GPU pool
                  │
   ┌──────────────┼──────────────────┐
   ▼              ▼                  ▼
Guaranteed     Preemptible        Experiments
quotas         pool               slice
(committed     (anything that     (small, so research
deadlines)     can be paused)     is never starved)
   │              │                  │
   └──── weekly utilization report ──┘
                  │
                  ▼
        Re-allocate on a schedule; take back idle quota
```

1. **Guaranteed quotas**, sized to committed deadlines. Nobody can take them away mid-quarter. In the example,
   Y's 400 GPU-hours go here, and X's too if its deadline is an external commitment.
2. **A shared preemptible pool** for everything else, used by jobs that checkpoint or can retry. Teams get far more
   compute this way than strict quotas would allow, because they use each other's idle time.
3. **A small reserved slice for experiments**, so research isn't starved by production.
4. **Transparency.** Publish the allocation and the rule. Measure utilization per team and take back idle quota.
5. **A review schedule.** Re-allocate every few weeks with fresh data, with an escalation path to a named person
   who decides the cases the rule gets wrong.

The utilization report is what makes this fair. A team that reserved 1,000 GPU-hours and used 400 sees the gap in
public, and the 600 flow back to the shared pool.

### When a team objects

Someone will object, usually the team with the biggest headline project. Show them the ranking and the arithmetic,
offer a date to re-check, and give them preemptible capacity in the meantime. You are asking them to accept the
rule, not the outcome. If you have a real example, tell it in **STAR** form (Situation, Task, Action, Result): the
conflict, the rule you applied, the objection, and what you measured afterwards.

## Small-team version

At a small company the scarce compute is usually a **model API** budget (a paid web service such as Anthropic's or
OpenAI's that runs the model for you, billed per **token**, roughly ¾ of a word) plus perhaps a few GPUs. The same
rule applies with dollars in place of GPU-hours:

- Give each feature its own token budget.
- Set provider spend caps and workspace limits before launch.
- Review cost per outcome (for example, cost per resolved support ticket) every month, and move budget toward the
  features with the best outcome per dollar.

Billing limits can stop a launch. Anthropic's API documents monthly spend caps by usage tier, and requests over
the cap are rejected until the next month ([rate limits](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/api/rate-limits),
fetched 2026-10-11). A feature at an assumed 50,000 requests a day spends about **$9,600 per 30 days**, against a
**$500** monthly cap on the Start tier (rechecked 2026-10-12; new organizations can begin on a lower
Evaluation tier). So a launch can stall on billing, not code. Know your tier before you ship.

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the numbers (for a Staff-level follow-up)</summary>

- **One request:** 1,000 new input tokens at $2 per million, 2,000 cached input tokens at $0.20 per million, and 400
  output tokens at $10 per million (Claude Sonnet 5 list prices, fetched 2026-10-11): 0.002 + 0.0004 + 0.004 =
  **$0.0064**. "Cached" input is a part of the prompt the provider has seen before and charges less to reuse.
- **30 days:** 50,000 × $0.0064 × 30 = **$9,600**.

</details>

Say it like this: "On a small team the scarce thing is the API budget and my time, so I'd give each feature a
budget and rank them by outcome per dollar, monthly."

## Pitfalls

- **First come, first served, or loudest team wins.** It rewards politics, not value, and teams learn to ask early
  and loudly.
- **A rule nobody else can see or predict.** Every decision then becomes a fresh argument.
- **No preemptible tier.** Idle guaranteed quota is simply wasted.
- **Rationing before checking utilization.** You may be fighting over capacity that is sitting idle.

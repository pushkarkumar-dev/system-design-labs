## What they're really asking

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "The date came from a customer renewal, so it was fixed. I cut scope, not quality: I dropped the second data
> source and the review tooling, and kept the eval gate that every change had to pass. We shipped on time. The
> missing tooling cost us two weeks of manual triage after launch, so now I plan review tooling into the first
> release."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer.

The interviewer is asking how you plan and trade under deadline pressure, and whether you own the outcome. The
question invites one of two answers. One is blame ("sales promised a date"). The other is a clear account of
cause, trade-offs and consequences. The second is what you want.

### What makes an AI project's timeline different

On a normal backend project, a tight deadline usually squeezes the build: fewer features, less polish, more
overtime. The work that remains is mostly code, and tests tell you when it is done.

A project built on a large language model (an **LLM**) has two phases that don't shrink with effort and that
engineers often leave out of the estimate:

1. **Building the eval set.** An **eval set** is a fixed list of test inputs with known good answers, used to
   score the system. An LLM gives a good answer to one input and a wrong one to the next, so you can't call it
   done without scoring it on many inputs. Collecting and labelling those inputs takes people's time, often a
   domain expert's.
2. **Review.** Sending customer data to a model provider usually needs security, privacy and legal sign-off. That
   runs on other teams' calendars.

So "why was it tight?" often has an honest answer that is not about coding speed at all. And the most tempting
thing to cut, the eval, is the one you must protect. Without it, every later change to the prompt (the
instructions sent to the model) or to the model itself goes to production unmeasured.

The Harvey candidate reports only the question: "For the deep dive, I discussed one project and was asked why its
timeline had been tight" (<Cite id="s-74265be7ec" />).

## Clarify first

- **Which "tight" do they mean?** Why the deadline was short, or why the team nearly missed it? If unsure, answer
  both briefly.
- **How much business context can you share?** Abstract customer names if needed, and say you are doing it.

Say it like this: "Do you mean why the date was aggressive, or why we nearly missed it? I'll touch on both."

## Answer

Give four parts, in order. The example running through them is illustrative: a help-desk assistant that answers
customer questions from a product's help-center articles.

### 1. Where the deadline came from

Name the source: a customer commitment, a contract date, a launch event, a dependency on another team, or your
own estimate. Be specific. If it was your estimate, say so; owning a bad estimate reads better than blaming one.

In the example: "A large customer's renewal depended on the assistant being live. The date was fixed six weeks
out."

### 2. What you traded

Scope is the usual lever. Name what you cut and what you protected.

- **Cut**: a second data source, a user-interface feature, automated rollback, internal review tooling.
- **Protected**: the eval gate and the safety checks. An **eval gate** is a rule that no change ships unless it
  scores at least as well as the current version on the eval set.

Say why you protected the eval: cutting it turns every later prompt change into an unmeasured production change.

Say it like this: "I cut the second connector and the review tooling. I kept the 150-question eval gate, because
without it every prompt tweak after launch would have been a guess."

### 3. How you ran it

Give the phases with durations, then say which one took longer than planned and why.

| Phase | Planned | Actual | What to say in the interview |
|---|---|---|---|
| Prototype | 1 week | 1 week | "The prototype was the fast part." |
| Eval set | 1 week | 1.5 weeks | "Labelling with two support leads took longer than I'd allowed." |
| Hardening (timeouts, fallbacks, rate limits) | 1 week | 1 week | "Standard work, but it has to be on the plan." |
| Security and legal review | 1.5 weeks | 2 weeks | "Review was on the critical path, so I started it in week one." |
| Rollout | 1.5 weeks | 0.5 weeks | "I shortened the staged rollout to make the date." |

(All durations are illustrative.)

Legal, security or privacy review is often on the critical path for an LLM feature. Honeycomb's account of a
one-month launch describes exactly that
([Honeycomb](https://www.honeycomb.io/blog/hard-stuff-nobody-talks-about-llm), page dated 2024-08-24).

### 4. What it cost later, and what you do now

Name the debt you took on and when you paid it. Then name the planning habit you changed:

- building the eval set first, before the prototype;
- adding review lead time to every estimate;
- splitting the launch into a smaller first release.

Here is the whole shape in one paragraph, with placeholder content: "The date came from a customer renewal. I cut
the second connector and manual review tooling, kept the 150-question eval gate, and shipped on time. The missing
tooling cost two weeks of manual triage after launch; now I schedule review tooling with the first release."

### What the interviewer is listening for

They are probing planning and judgment, not looking for someone to blame. Three things score well: you knew where
the date came from, you chose what to cut deliberately, and you changed how you plan afterwards. "We worked
weekends and made it" scores poorly, because it shows effort, not judgment.

## Small-team version

At a small company, tight timelines are normal and usually come from sales or a founder. The credible story shows
you negotiated scope rather than quality:

- a smaller first release;
- an eval gate kept even under pressure;
- a **spend cap** (a hard monthly limit on the model bill) set before launch.

Saying "I pushed back on X and agreed to Y" shows you can manage stakeholders without a manager doing it for you.

Size the spend cap from the expected bill. Hosted models charge per **token** (a piece of text about ¾ of a word
long). At the family walkthrough's example of about $0.0064 per request on Claude Sonnet 5, and an assumed 10,000
requests a day, launch spend is about **$1,900 a month**. A cap near **2×** that catches a runaway loop (code that
calls the model over and over by mistake) without blocking real growth.

Say it like this: "I set a spend cap at about twice the expected bill before launch. A bug that loops on the model
hits the cap in days, not at the end of the month."

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the numbers (for a Staff-level follow-up)</summary>

- Per request (Claude Sonnet 5 list prices fetched 2026-10-11; token counts assumed): 1,000 uncached input ×
  $2 per million + 2,000 cached input × $0.20 per million + 400 output × $10 per million = **$0.0064**.
- Monthly: 10,000 × $0.0064 × 30 = $1,920 ≈ **$1,900**.
- Cap at about 2×: roughly **$3,800 a month**.

</details>

## Pitfalls

- **Blaming product, sales or another team.** It says nothing about what you did, which is the question.
- **Claiming nothing was cut.** Interviewers rarely believe it, and it hides your judgment.
- **Cutting the eval to save time and presenting that as a win.** Every later change then ships unmeasured.
- **No lesson, or "work harder" as the lesson.** The interviewer wants a planning change, not more effort.
- **Leaving review out of the story.** It is often the longest phase, and naming it shows you know how LLM features
  really ship.

## What they're really asking

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "The best week of my last two years was sitting with a customer's operations team to fix an integration. I
> liked hearing the real problem, scoping it with them and shipping something they used that week. I think most
> of the value of AI is lost in exactly that kind of work: getting at the data, testing on the customer's own
> cases, and fitting the model into how people actually work. I'd give up owning one codebase long term, and I
> want to do this work here because of what you've published about your deployments."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer, so you can say it with your own story.

The interviewer wants to know one thing: do you actually want customer-facing technical work, or do you want the
company's name?

### What the role is

A **forward-deployed engineer (FDE)** is an engineer who works directly with a customer, often inside the
customer's own systems, to make the company's product work for them. It is a different job from building the
core product. Picture where the FDE sits:

```
Customer's people (ops, support, executives)
   │  what they need, in their words
   ▼
Customer's systems (data, tools, permissions)
   │  where the model has to fit
   ▼
FDE ── scopes the problem, writes the integration, tests on the customer's cases
   │  what keeps coming up across customers
   ▼
Company's core product team ── turns repeated needs into product features
```

**The customer's people** describe problems in business terms, not technical ones. Turning that into something
buildable is the first part of the job. **The customer's systems** are messy, old and full of permission rules;
most integration work happens here. **The FDE** sits in the middle: part engineer, part consultant. **The core
product team** builds the product itself. A good FDE sends back what keeps repeating, so it becomes part of the
product instead of custom work every time.

One Pragmatic Engineer piece describes forward-deployed work as about **25% coding, 50% integration and 25%
customer-facing** (<Dated asOf="2026-05-24" src="s-e4c26adb42">Pragmatic Engineer</Dated>). Compared with a
product-team job, that is far less time writing code you own, and far more time with other people's systems and
people.

### Why this is a filter

For an ordinary backend role, "why this role?" is a warm-up. For an FDE role it is a filter, because the job is
unusual and some people apply only for the company's name. The title is a candidate-submitted question on Aced
tagged OpenAI (<Cite id="s-ee91b6c25d" />). In a first-hand OpenAI FDE account, the recruiter "spent a lot of
time on why I wanted FDE specifically, not just 'work at OpenAI.'" The candidate adds: "If you can't articulate
why you want customer-facing technical work versus pure engineering, the process stops here."
(<Cite id="s-812dbc9385" />). It often comes early, in the recruiter screen.

## Clarify first

- **Role or company?** "Would you like my reasons for the role, the company, or both?" Lead with the role.
- **This team's mix.** If this company's FDE role differs from others (more coding, more pre-sales work), ask
  what the split looks like on this team. Say it like this: "FDE means different things at different
  companies. How much of the week here is building versus with customers?" It shows you know the title varies.

## Answer

### The example we will follow

This page follows one illustrative engineer, Sam: a backend engineer on an orders service, who once spent a week
with a customer's operations team fixing an integration that kept dropping orders. Use the shape, with your own
story.

### Show you know what the work is

Use the split above. Say it like this: "I understand it's roughly a quarter coding, half integration and a
quarter with customers, and the integration half is the part I want."

Demand is high: Indeed data relayed by Storyboard18 from an FT report shows applied-AI postings up more than 800%
from January to September 2025 (<Dated asOf="2025-11-03" src="s-c2cbc68019">Storyboard18</Dated>). Don't use
demand as a reason; use it as context, if at all. "It's a growing field" is a reason anyone could give.

### Your reasons, from evidence

A strong answer has four parts. Here is Sam's:

| Part | What it means | Sam's version (illustrative) | What to say in the interview |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. A past project that pulled you here** | A time you sat with a customer or internal user, scoped an unclear problem, and shipped something they used | The week with the customer's ops team | "I heard the problem from the people living with it, and we shipped a fix that week." |
| **2. What you like that most engineers avoid** | Unclear problems, travel, explaining limits to executives, messy systems | Working out what the ops team actually needed | "I like the part where the problem isn't defined yet." |
| **3. What you give up, and why you accept it** | Deep ownership of one codebase, long-term architecture on a product team | Owning the orders service long term | "I'd give up owning one system. I'm better at scoping with a customer than at optimizing one service." |
| **4. Why this company's version** | Something specific the company has published about its deployments or customers | (Sam fills this from the company's own posts) | "Your published deployments are the kind of problem I want." |

Then join the parts into one view. Sam's: "Most AI value is lost in integration, in evals on the customer's own
cases, and in the workflow around the model." An **eval** here is a fixed set of the customer's real examples
with known good answers, run against the system to check it works for *them*, not just in a demo.

### Say what you're not

If you have never been customer-facing, say so and show the nearest thing: internal platform work with many
teams as customers, or on-call escalations with external customers. Say it like this: "I haven't had a
customer-facing title, but for two years other teams were my customers, and the best part was sitting with them."
That is more convincing than stretching a story.

## Senior vs Staff

In plain words: a Senior answer explains why you want the work. A Staff answer also uses the last arrow in the
diagram: how what you learn with customers should change the product, so the next customer needs less custom
work.

## Small-team version

At a company of 50–500 people, the person who owns AI is often forward-deployed in all but name. They sit with
support, sales or a key customer to make an AI feature work. If you are interviewing at a small company, the
same answer works for "why this role": show that you like working at the boundary between the model, the data
and the people using it.

Put it in actions. In the first month you might spend a week with the support team reading the cases the AI
feature gets wrong, turn fifty of them into an eval, and fix the top two causes. Say it like this: "I'd start
where the users are, not in the code." There is little money to discuss here; the cost is your time, and the
answer is about where you choose to spend it.

## Pitfalls

- **Answering "why OpenAI" instead of "why FDE".** The recruiter in the reported account asked about the role,
  and an answer about the company fails that filter.
- **"I like talking to people" with no story.** Without a past example it sounds like a guess about yourself.
- **Treating FDE as a stepping stone to a product team.** It tells them you don't want the job you're applying
  for.
- **Market-demand statistics as your reason.** Demand explains why the job exists, not why you want it.

## What they're really asking

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "I'd build the assistant so it answers only from the current policy and cites the passage, and says 'the
> policies don't cover this' when they don't. Before expanding, I'd agree a written bar with the next team:
> a correct-answer rate on their own questions, judged by the low end of its error bar, zero answers that
> quote an old policy or leak a document they can't see, and two weeks of clean live signals. Then I'd roll
> out team by team behind a flag. At 600 questions a day, the offline test set and a policy owner's weekly
> review are the real measurement; the live numbers are a smoke alarm."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer.

The interviewer is asking whether you can turn "is it good enough?" into three concrete things: a bar agreed
before you measure, a set of checks that can actually fail, and a rollout that limits the damage if you are
wrong.

### What makes this different from shipping a normal internal tool

For a normal internal tool, "good enough to expand" means it doesn't crash and people use it. You can test
it exactly: given this input, return that output.

An assistant built on a large language model (**LLM**) is different in three ways:

1. **Its answers vary.** The same question can get a slightly different answer each time, so one test run
   proves little.
2. **"Correct" needs judging.** Two differently worded answers can both be right. You need a way to grade
   meaning, not strings.
3. **Its scores are estimates.** "85% correct on 50 questions" could really be anywhere from about 75% to
   95%. The error bar is part of the answer.

The bank adds two things a generic chatbot answer misses. Answers must cite the **current** policy, because
policies get revised and an old one can be confidently wrong. And the next team's documents may carry
different permissions, so the assistant must never show a team a document it isn't allowed to read.

This question is an OpenAI forward-deployed prep guide's representative question, not a candidate report
(<Cite id="s-26f0cae3fe" />). (A forward-deployed engineer builds AI features alongside a customer's own team.) The same guide says the reliable differentiator is a concrete answer to how you
know the system works.

### Words you need first

- **RAG** (retrieval-augmented generation): the assistant first searches the policy documents for relevant
  passages, then the model writes an answer from those passages. It's a search step followed by a writing step.
- **Golden set**: a fixed list of real questions (called **items**), each with what a good answer must contain.
  It is the assistant's test suite.
- **Failure mode**: a named, recurring kind of wrong answer, such as "cited an old policy version".
- **LLM judge**: a second model call that grades an answer against one written rule, returning pass or fail.
- **Confidence interval**: the error bar on a score, written as a range. "90% ± 4.8 points" means the true
  rate is probably between about 85% and 95%.

## Clarify first

Ask these, and say your assumptions out loud:

- **Who uses it, and for what?** "Let's assume branch and operations staff checking procedures, about 600
  questions a day." (assumption)
- **What does a wrong answer cost?** "I'll treat quoting a replaced policy as a must-not-happen failure."
- **Who can label answers?** "I'll assume one policy owner per area who can say whether an answer is right."
- **What does the next team need that the first didn't?** New documents, new permissions, new kinds of
  question.
- **What do people do today?** "If they email the policy team now, that's the baseline the assistant must
  beat."

## Answer

### The system, and where the checks sit

```
Employee question
   │  1. who is asking? (their entitlements)
   ▼
Search policy index ── only documents this user may read,
   │                    only the current version of each policy
   │  2. top passages
   ▼
Model writes answer ── must cite passage + version,
   │                    or say "the policies don't cover this, ask the policy team"
   ▼
Answer shown, and logged as a trace (question, passages, answer)
   │
   └──► weekly review by the policy owner ──► new test items
```

**Search with permissions.** The search step filters by the user's entitlements before anything reaches the
model. A permission leak is a search bug, so fix it there, not in the prompt.

**Versions in the index.** Each passage carries its document version and effective date. That lets a code
check confirm the citation points to the version in force today.

**An explicit "not covered" path.** If the policies don't answer the question, the right output is to say so
and point to the policy team. Without that path, uncovered questions get confident made-up answers.

**Traces.** Every question, the passages found and the answer are logged. A **trace** is that full record of
one request. It's what you read when something goes wrong.

### Define good before measuring

With the first team, read 100 real questions and answers. Write down the failure modes you see: wrong
answer, stale version cited, a claim the passage doesn't support, should have said "not covered", missed the
question. Then build a golden set of 200–300 items, each linked to where it came from.

Grade with the cheapest thing that works:

- **Code checks** where possible: does the citation exist, and is it the current version?
- **One pass/fail LLM judge per open-ended failure mode**, such as "does the passage support the claim?".
  Each judge is checked against the policy owner's labels before you trust it.

### Follow the expansion decision

Here is the decision for the next team, step by step. The numbers are assumptions except where the box below
works them out.

| Step | What happens | What to say in the interview |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Agree the bar | The next team's owner signs a written bar before any measurement. | "We agree what 'good enough' means before we see the number, so nobody moves the goalposts." |
| 2. Collect their questions | 150 real questions from the next team, gathered before they get access. | "Their questions, not ours: their documents and their wording." |
| 3. Run the suite | The 150 items plus the must-pass items run against the current version. | "Must-pass items are stale versions and permission leaks. One failure blocks." |
| 4. Read the score | Say 90% correct. The error bar is **± 4.8 points**. | "90% on 150 questions supports a bar of about 85%, not 90%." |
| 5. Check abstention | Questions the policies don't cover must get "not covered". | "A confident answer to an uncovered question is a failure, even if it sounds right." |
| 6. Roll out | Behind a per-user flag, for two weeks. | "Live signals catch crashes, leaks and cost spikes. They can't measure a 3-point change at this volume." |
| 7. Watch | Thumbs-down rate, escalations to the policy team, 30 answers a week reviewed by the policy owner. | "The weekly human review is my main live measure." |
| 8. Learn | Every escaped failure becomes a regression item. | "Every mistake we find becomes a permanent test." |

So the written bar looks like this:

- correct-answer rate on their own 150 questions, judged by the **lower end** of its 95% interval, not the
  middle;
- zero must-pass failures (stale versions, permission leaks);
- correct "not covered" answers on uncovered questions;
- two weeks of live signals within limits: thumbs-down rate, escalations to the policy team, and the weekly
  review of 30 answers.

Why not run an A/B test? At 600 questions a day, an A/B test needs **about two weeks** to see a 3-point
change. So the offline test set and the human sample are the measurement, and the live phase is a smoke
alarm.

## Small-team version

You are likely the only engineer on this. Here is what to do with your time and money:

- **Build** the test suite and the gate yourself: a few hundred lines plus a CI job.
- **Reuse** whatever tracing you already have.
- **Borrow** a policy owner for about 10 hours of labelling.

A full suite run costs roughly **$13** at list prices fetched 2026-10-11 (the family walkthrough's cost math:
200 items × 3 runs on Claude Sonnet 5 plus two Claude Haiku 4.5 judges). The labelling time is the real cost,
not the tokens.

Revisit when a third team joins or audit requirements appear.

## Pitfalls

- **Expanding on a demo and a thumbs-up rate.** A demo shows the questions you picked, and thumbs come from
  the few users who click.
- **Reporting 90% on 50 questions as exact.** At 90% on 50 questions the error bar is roughly 8 points either way.
- **Forgetting the next team's permissions and documents.** A permission leak is a release blocker, not a
  quality metric.
- **No "not covered" path.** Without it, uncovered questions get confident answers that sound official.

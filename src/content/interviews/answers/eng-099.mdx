## What they're really asking

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "The assistant answered customer questions from about 3,000 help-center articles. It searched the articles,
> picked the best three passages, and had the model answer only from them, with links. I owned retrieval and the
> eval: 85% right on 200 real questions, give or take 5 points. Every passage carries its article version, so a
> stale answer is a log query, not a guess. And when the articles don't cover a question, it says so, and we
> measure how often it gets that right."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer.

The interviewer wants to know whether you have actually built and run a retrieval system, or only read about
one. The walk-through tests ownership and measurement. The two follow-ups test operations.

### What makes this different from a normal search feature

You may have built search before. A normal search box returns a list of links, and the user decides which one to
trust. If the results are a bit off, the user scrolls.

A RAG system takes that decision away from the user. **RAG** (retrieval-augmented generation) means: search your
own documents first (**retrieval**), then hand the best passages to a large language model (an **LLM**) and let
it write one answer from them. The user sees a confident paragraph, not a list. That changes three things:

1. **A retrieval mistake becomes a wrong answer.** If search returns the wrong passage, the model writes a fluent
   answer from it. So retrieval quality has to be measured, not eyeballed.
2. **Old documents become wrong answers.** If the index holds last week's version of an article, the model quotes
   last week's facts. That is the "stale documents" follow-up.
3. **Missing documents become invented answers.** If no passage answers the question, the model may answer
   anyway. That is the "uncovered questions" follow-up.

Only the title of this question is candidate-reported, from an Amazon-tagged PracHub page; the two follow-ups are
listed on the same page (<Cite id="s-56187f9727" />).

### Words you need first

- **Token**: a small piece of text, roughly ¾ of a word. Models read and write tokens, and are priced by them.
- **Chunk**: a passage of a few hundred tokens cut from a longer document. The index stores chunks, not whole
  documents.
- **Embedding**: a list of numbers representing a text's meaning; similar meanings get similar lists. Searching
  by embedding is **dense retrieval**. Searching by words is keyword search (usually scored with **BM25**). Using
  both is **hybrid retrieval**.
- **Eval set**: a fixed list of test questions with known good answers, used to score the system.

## Answer

Tell it in the order the question asks, one or two sentences each, then stop for questions. The example below is
illustrative (a help-desk assistant for a software product); replace it with your own project.

### The system, as one picture

```
Help-center articles (about 3,000, edited daily)
   │  ingestion job, every hour
   ▼
Chunker ── passages of a few hundred tokens, each tagged with article id + version
   │
   ▼
Index ── embeddings (meaning) + words (keywords) + article id, version, indexed_at
   ▲
   │  search with the customer's question
Question ──► Retriever (hybrid, top 20) ──► Reranker (keeps best 3)
                                               │
                                               ▼
                         LLM: answer only from these 3 passages, link them,
                              or say "I don't know"
                                               │
                                               ▼
                         Log: question, passages, article versions, answer
```

The **ingestion job** re-reads changed articles every hour and cuts them into chunks. The **index** keeps each
chunk's embedding, its words and the article id and version it came from. The **retriever** runs dense and
keyword search together and returns 20 candidates. The **reranker** is a slower model that reads the question and
each candidate together and keeps the best 3. The **LLM** writes the answer from those 3, with links. The **log**
records which article versions each answer used; that one design choice answers the stale-document follow-up.

### Walk it in four steps

| Step | What to cover | What to say in the interview |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Sources | What the corpus was, how big, how often it changed, who owned it | "About 3,000 articles, edited daily by the support team. They owned the content; I owned getting it into the index." |
| 2. Retrieval | Chunking, embedding model, dense or hybrid, reranking, prompt with citations. One choice you made and the option you rejected | "Dense-only was simpler, but it missed error codes and product names. I added keyword search after the eval showed that." |
| 3. Evaluation | The eval set (size, where questions came from, who labelled them), the metric, the baseline, the result with its error bar | "200 real tickets, labelled with two support leads. 85%, so about plus or minus 5 points." |
| 4. What broke | One production failure and the design change it caused | "We quoted an old price after the pricing article changed. That's why every chunk now carries its version." |

For the metric, name one for retrieval and one for answers. For retrieval, **hit@k** or **recall@k**: the share
of questions where a relevant passage appears in the top k results. For answers, a **groundedness** check: does
every claim in the answer come from the retrieved passages?

Give the error bar with the number. An eval set is a sample, so the score could move if you picked different
questions. With 50 questions at 85%, the 95% error bar is about **±10 points**; with 200 it is about **±5**. If
your before-and-after gap is smaller than that, say so and say how you handled it (a bigger set, or a paired
comparison on the same questions).

### Follow-up 1: stale documents

A **stale** answer comes from an old version of a document. Don't answer with "re-index more often"; that
shrinks the problem but doesn't let you see it. Make staleness observable at three levels:

1. **Index lag.** Store the source version and ingestion time on every chunk. Alert on the gap between a document
   changing and being re-indexed, and on documents deleted at the source but still in the index.
2. **Answer provenance.** Log the document id and version in every citation. Then "answered from an old version"
   is a query over logs.
3. **Canary questions.** When a key document changes, add a question whose answer changed, and run it against the
   live system on a schedule.

Say it like this: "Every answer logs the article versions it used, so finding stale answers is a query. Index lag
is a metric with an alert, and changed articles get a canary question."

### Follow-up 2: questions the documents don't cover

State the behaviour first: **abstain** (decline to answer), say what the documents do cover, and route to a human
or a search link. Then enforce it with an instruction to answer only from the retrieved passages, plus a
retrieval-score threshold below which the system abstains.

Then measure it. Put unanswerable questions in the eval set and track two rates: the **abstain rate** (it said "I
don't know" when it should have) and the **false-abstain rate** (it said "I don't know" when the answer was
there). Without those items you can't tell whether the abstain path works at all.

Say it like this: "Twenty of our eval questions have no answer in the help center. We track how often it declines
on those, and how often it wrongly declines on the rest."

### If your RAG experience is the site's lab

Say so. The <LabRef lab="rag" inline /> lab's measured results on two public BEIR test sets (with paired
intervals between versions) are honest material for the evaluation step. But a public benchmark has no stale
documents, so describe what you would measure in production instead.

## Small-team version

At a small company you probably built all of it: ingestion from Drive or a wiki, retrieval in Postgres with
pgvector (a Postgres extension for storing and searching embeddings) or a hosted store, the prompt, the eval and
the dashboard. Present that breadth as ownership.

The small-team answers to the follow-ups are cheap:

- a `source_version` and `indexed_at` column on the chunks table;
- a nightly job that compares them with the source;
- citations that carry the version;
- 20 to 30 unanswerable questions in the eval set.

Bring the cost per question and the monthly bill. With an assumed 2,000 questions a day, at the family
walkthrough's example of about $0.0064 per question on Claude Sonnet 5, the bill is about **$384 a month**.
Re-embedding everything is cheap too: about **$10** per full re-index of 1M chunks with text-embedding-3-small.
Revisit the managed-API default only if a data-residency rule or a much larger bill appears.

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the numbers (for a Staff-level follow-up)</summary>

- Per question: 1,000 uncached input tokens × $2 per million + 2,000 cached input × $0.20 per million + 400 output
  × $10 per million = $0.002 + $0.0004 + $0.004 = **$0.0064** (Claude Sonnet 5 list prices fetched 2026-10-11;
  token counts assumed).
- Monthly: 2,000 × $0.0064 × 30 ≈ **$384**.
- Re-index: 1M chunks × 500 tokens = 500M tokens; at $0.02 per million tokens (text-embedding-3-small) ≈ **$10**.

</details>

## Pitfalls

- **A pipeline tour with no eval number.** The interviewer can't tell whether it worked, so they assume it was
  tuned by feel.
- **A number with no sample size.** "85%" on 50 questions and on 1,000 questions mean very different things.
- **"We" for every decision.** The interviewer can't tell what you did.
- **"Re-index more often" as the stale-document answer.** It gives no way to detect the problem when it happens.
- **An abstain path that was never measured.** Without unanswerable eval questions, you don't know it works.

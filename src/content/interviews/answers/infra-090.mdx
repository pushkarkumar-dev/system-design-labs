## What they're really asking

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "This is a multi-tenant job platform whose jobs happen to be GPU training runs. Memory decides the hardware: a
> full fine-tune of an 8B model needs about 128 GB and a whole 8-GPU machine, while LoRA or QLoRA fits on one GPU.
> So the scheduler has two job shapes, small ones it can pack and big ones that need all their GPUs at once. Jobs
> checkpoint and resume under a lease, each tenant's data and weights are isolated and encrypted with their own
> keys, quotas are priced in GPU-hours before admission, and nothing deploys until it beats the base model on the
> tenant's eval set."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer.

The interviewer wants to see a solid backend platform design. The hard parts aren't the training math. They are
choosing hardware per method, isolating tenants' data and weights, quotas, jobs that survive failures, and a path
from a finished run to a served model.

### What makes this different from a normal job platform

You may have built a job runner: a queue, workers, retries, per-customer limits. This is that, with three twists.

1. **Jobs differ hugely in size.** For the same 8B model, one method needs about 4.5 GB and another 128 GB. Some fit
   on one GPU; others need a whole 8-GPU machine, and all 8 GPUs
   must start together. A scheduler that treats them alike wastes most of the fleet.
2. **Jobs run for hours and are expensive to retry.** A retry that starts from step 0 burns real money.
3. **The output is a model trained on a customer's private data.** The weights are derived from that data, so isolation
   covers datasets, checkpoints and the served model, not only the API.

### Words you need first

- **Fine-tuning**: continuing to train an existing open model on a customer's own examples. A **full** fine-tune
  updates every **parameter** (learned number) in the model.
- **LoRA** (low-rank adaptation): freeze the original weights and train a small add-on, called an **adapter**,
  usually well under 1% of the model's size.
- **QLoRA**: LoRA on a copy of the base model compressed to about 4 bits per weight, so it fits in much less memory.
- **Token**: a piece of a word, about ¾ of a word. Datasets and prices are measured in tokens.

The write-up asks for datasets, LoRA, QLoRA and full fine-tuning, distributed training with checkpointing,

## Clarify first

- **Base models?** "I'll assume 8B and 70B open models."
- **Methods?** "LoRA, QLoRA and full? Full fine-tuning of the 70B changes the hardware story."
- **Volume?** "Dataset sizes per job, say 10M to 1B tokens, and how many jobs per day?"
- **Isolation bar?** "May two tenants share a GPU, or does each job get dedicated machines?"
- **Delivery?** "Does each tenant get a dedicated endpoint, or adapters served on a shared base model?"

## Answer

### The flow, end to end

```
Tenant uploads dataset
   │  validate: schema, token count, personal-data scan, dedup
   ▼
Job spec ── base model, method, settings, pinned versions (stored, reproducible)
   │  quota check: estimated GPU-hours vs the tenant's budget
   ▼
Queue
   │
   ▼
Scheduler ── packs small jobs onto single GPUs; reserves whole machines for big ones
   │
   ▼
Isolated training container ── no network egress; tenant key mounted
   │  checkpoints every N steps to the tenant's encrypted storage prefix
   ▼
Eval step ── must beat the base model on the tenant's eval set
   │
   ▼
Artifact registry ──► Deploy (adapter on a shared base, or dedicated replicas)
```

**Validation** stops bad data before it costs GPU time: wrong schema, a token count far above the quota, or
personal data the tenant shouldn't be uploading. **The job spec** pins every version so a run can be repeated.
**The quota check** estimates GPU-hours before the job is admitted. **The scheduler** handles the two job shapes
described next. **The training container** runs one job and cannot reach the network. **The eval step** blocks
deployment of a model that is worse than the base. **The registry** records which tenant owns each artifact.

### Follow one tenant's job: hardware by method

Say a tenant wants to fine-tune the 8B model. Memory decides what hardware the job needs. Training needs about
16 bytes per trained parameter (weight, gradient, and the optimizer's bookkeeping), and only 2 bytes for a frozen
one.

| Method | Memory for model state | Hardware | What to say in the interview |
|---|---|---|---|
| Full fine-tune | about **128 GB** | at least 2 GPUs; in practice one 8-GPU machine, all GPUs started together | "This is a gang-scheduled job: all 8 GPUs or none." |
| LoRA (rank 16 on the attention layers) | about **16 GB** frozen + **0.2 GB** trained | one GPU | "Packable. Several can share a machine." |
| QLoRA | about **4.5 GB** base | one small GPU | "The QLoRA paper fine-tunes a 65B model on one 48 GB GPU." |

The working data for each batch comes on top of these numbers. Measure it per model and sequence length.

Say it like this: "The scheduler has two job shapes: single-GPU LoRA and QLoRA jobs it can pack, and multi-GPU full
fine-tunes that must be scheduled as a gang."

FSDP (fully sharded data parallel) is PyTorch's way of splitting model state across GPUs. The 13.6M adapter
parameters: 32 layers × rank 16 × (8,192 + 5,120 + 5,120 + 8,192) for the four attention projections.

### Jobs that survive failures

Each job runs under a **lease** with a **fencing token**: a number that increases each time the job is handed to a
new worker. Storage rejects writes carrying an old token, so a worker that lost its lease (and doesn't know it yet)
can't overwrite the final artifact. The job checkpoints every N steps, including its position in the data. Retries
resume from the latest checkpoint, and the number of retries is capped.

### Isolation

- One job per container. No GPU sharing across tenants unless the hardware's partitioning is approved for it.
- No network egress from training containers.
- Per-tenant encryption keys for datasets, checkpoints and weights. Deletion covers all three.
- The tenant ID travels with every artifact. The serving router refuses an adapter whose owner doesn't match the
  caller.

### Quotas and cost

Each tenant gets a GPU-hour budget and a cap on concurrent jobs. Estimate a job's cost before admission: training
work is about 6 × parameters × tokens operations for a full fine-tune, or about 4 × parameters × tokens for LoRA
(the frozen base skips part of the backward pass). Divide by the throughput you have measured on your hardware.

### Deploy

LoRA adapters are small, so many can be served on one shared copy of the base model (multi-adapter serving). Full
fine-tunes need their own replicas, which is the real cost difference between the two methods. Gate every
deployment on the tenant's eval set beating the base model.

Say it like this: "The training bill is small. The serving bill of a full fine-tune is the product decision: it
needs its own replicas forever."

## Small-team version

At a 50–500 person company you build the internal version: a job spec in git, a queue, one rented GPU machine and an
eval gate.

Managed fine-tuning is the default to buy. Together lists LoRA fine-tuning of Llama 3.1 8B at $0.34–$0.38 per 1M training tokens
(fetched 2026-10-12). For 150M training tokens (50M tokens of examples, 3 passes) that is about **$51–$57**.

Keep an exit path. OpenAI's pricing page says it is winding down its fine-tuning platform and no longer accepts new
users (fetched 2026-10-12). A vendor can remove the method you rely on.

## Pitfalls

- **One hardware shape for every method.** Single-GPU LoRA jobs on 8-GPU machines waste most of the fleet.
- **Retries that restart from step 0, or two workers writing the same artifact.** Use checkpoints and fencing tokens.
- **Isolation that stops at the API.** Checkpoints and served adapters carry tenant data too.
- **No eval gate between "training finished" and "deployed".** A finished run is not a better model.

## What they're really asking

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "First I'd contain it: route the high-risk questions to a person or a fixed answer while I investigate. Then I'd
> trace the bad answers and sort each one by cause: the knowledge wasn't available, it wasn't retrieved, the
> model had it and contradicted it, or it faithfully repeated an outdated source. For mitigation I wouldn't rely
> on the prompt. The bot answers only from cited sources, says 'I don't know' when the evidence is weak, shows the
> passage it used, and a human reviews the riskiest category. I'd set the 'I don't know' threshold with the
> product owner, from measured numbers."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer.

The interviewer wants two things: a disciplined investigation, and mitigations that work *even when the model
misbehaves*.

That second part is what makes this different from a normal production incident. When a backend service returns
wrong data, you fix the code and the bug is gone. A language model has no line of code to fix. It writes fluent,
certain-sounding text whatever its evidence, and it will be wrong on some share of questions no matter how good
the prompt is. In a high-risk setting, "we told the model to be careful" is not a safety argument. The protection
has to sit around the model.

### Words you need first

- **RAG** (retrieval-augmented generation): before the model answers, the system searches your own documents
  and pastes the best passages (**chunks**) into the prompt. Finding them is **retrieval**; writing the answer is
  **generation**.
- An answer is **faithful** when every claim is supported by the chunks it was given. It can be faithful and
  still wrong if the chunk is outdated.
- **Abstention** means the bot declines on purpose ("I don't know, let me connect you to a person").
- An **LLM judge** is a second model asked to grade answers, for example "is this claim supported by this
  passage?". It must be checked against human labels before you trust it.

An aggregator tags this question to Anthropic (ML engineer): "A deployed conversational model gives confident but
factually wrong answers in high-risk contexts", then asks how you would investigate and mitigate it
(<Cite id="s-d7a8e88bf0" />). The page doesn't say whether the model uses retrieval, so this card covers both.

## Clarify first

- **Which contexts count as high-risk** (medical, legal, financial, account actions), and who acts on the answer?
- **Does the system retrieve from documents**, or answer from the model's own knowledge?
- **How were the failures found, and at what rate?**
- **May the system decline and hand off?** *Assume yes.* "If it may, most of the risk becomes manageable."

## Answer

### Follow one case through it

Use one example throughout (invented for illustration). A health insurer's member assistant is asked: *"Is my
MRI covered without prior approval?"* It answers, confidently: *"Yes, MRIs no longer need prior approval."* Under
the current plan rules, they do. A member who trusts this could get a large bill.

| Step | What you do | For this case | What to say in the interview |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Contain | Route coverage questions to a person or a fixed answer; keep a kill switch | Coverage questions now get "Here is the policy page; a person will confirm" | "Before I know the cause, I stop the damage." |
| 2. Trace | Collect the question, retrieved context, prompt, answer, model version | The prompt held two plan documents: last year's and this year's | "Now I can see what the model saw." |
| 3. Classify | Absent from sources? Not retrieved? Retrieved but contradicted? Faithful to a stale source? | Faithful to last year's document | "Stale source. The model did what we asked; the data was wrong." |
| 4. Build an eval set | These cases plus "must decline" items, labeled by a domain expert | Add every coverage question from the reports | "I measure correctness, faithfulness and how often it declines." |

Without retrieval, step 3 asks a different question: did the model answer outside what it can know? A model
answering from its training data has no way to know this year's plan rules.

### The investigation, in full

1. **Contain first.** Before the root cause is known, route the high-risk intents to a human or a fixed response,
   and keep a kill switch.
2. **Collect and trace** the reported cases: question, retrieved context (if any), prompt, answer, model version.
3. **Classify each case.** Was the answer absent from the sources (a coverage gap), present but not retrieved,
   retrieved but contradicted (a generation failure), or faithful to a stale source?
4. **Build a high-risk eval set** from these cases plus "must decline" items, labeled by a domain expert. Measure
   answer correctness, faithfulness to the context, and how often the bot declines.

### Mitigate, from most to least structural

"Structural" here means the protection works whether or not the model behaves.

- **Scope.** Classify each question's intent and keep the riskiest out of free-form generation entirely.
- **Ground and abstain.** Answer only from retrieved, cited sources. Decline when no chunk clears a relevance
  threshold, or when a calibrated judge finds a claim with no support.
- **Show the evidence.** Quote the source passage next to the answer, so the user can check it.
- **Human review** for every answer in the highest-risk category, or a sample in lower ones.
- **Monitor** correctness on a sampled, judged stream of answers, not just error rates.

The cost of getting this wrong is concrete: a tribunal held Air Canada responsible for its chatbot's wrong fare
advice in Moffatt v. Air Canada, 2024 ([Law360 Canada](https://www.law360.ca/ca/articles/1804075)). Say: "The
company owns what its bot says. A wrong answer here is a liability, not a UX bug."

### Set the threshold with numbers

Declining more often makes the bot safer and less useful. Two numbers describe that trade. **Coverage** is the
share of questions the bot answers. **Risk** is the share of its answers that are wrong. Raising the "I don't
know" threshold lowers both.

In a hypothetical set of 200 high-risk questions, one threshold might answer 90% of them with **6.7%** wrong, and
a stricter one 70% with **2.1%** wrong. The product owner chooses the point on this curve; the engineer makes the
curve visible. Say: "How many members get 'I don't know' is a product and legal decision. My job is to show them
the curve."

With a few hundred questions, small differences in risk are within noise. The llm-eval lab (this site's
hands-on evaluation lab) measured how large a gap a paired test detects at n = 500
(<LabNumber lab="llm-eval" metric="Smallest true gap with 80% power, paired test, n=500" />).

## Senior vs Staff

<LevelContrast
  title="High-risk wrong answers"
  senior={["Contains the risk, classifies failures by stage, and ships abstention, citations and review with an eval that measures them."]}
  staff={["Owns the coverage-risk decision with product and legal, defines who reviews what, and sets the monitoring that would show the mitigation failing."]}
/>

In plain words: Senior stops the damage and fixes the cause with evidence. Staff makes the risk trade-off an
explicit decision with owners, and makes sure the team would notice if the protection stopped working.

## Small-team version

A small company has no safety team, so the AI owner decides whether this ships.

- **Build this week:** containment, abstention and citation checks. They are ordinary code.
- **The real cost is people.** A domain expert labeling 100–200 high-risk questions, and a person reviewing the
  riskiest answers.
- **Put a human in the loop for the riskiest category from day one.** It is cheaper than the incident.
- **Buy** the model and the tracing tool.

Rough numbers, all assumed: labeling 200 questions at 3 minutes each is **10 hours** (about **$930** at a loaded
$93 an hour). Reviewing an assumed 50 high-risk answers a day at 2 minutes each is about **1.7 hours a day**,
roughly **$4,650 per 30 days**.

Revisit the mix as the measured risk at your chosen coverage comes down, or when volume makes full review of the
riskiest category unaffordable. Then review a sample and route the rest to a fixed answer.

- Review: 50 × 2 min = 100 min ≈ 1.67 h a day; 1.67 h × $93 ≈ $155 a day; × 30 days ≈ $4,650.

## Pitfalls

- **"Add 'don't hallucinate' to the system prompt" as the mitigation.** It depends on the model behaving, which
  is the thing that failed.
- **Treating confident tone as information.** Fluent text sounds certain whether or not it is right.
- **Optimizing faithfulness while the sources are outdated.** A faithful answer to a stale document is still wrong.
- **Declining on everything and calling the error rate solved.** Measure coverage too, or "zero wrong answers"
  means nothing.

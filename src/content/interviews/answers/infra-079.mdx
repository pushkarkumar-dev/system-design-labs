## What they're really asking

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "I'll start from what users see, latency and errors per service, and what changed. Then I'll look for the
> feedback loops that turn load into an outage: CPU limits throttling pods while the autoscaler flips up and
> down, readiness checks pulling pods out so the rest get more load, and retries multiplying traffic. First
> I stop the bleeding: raise the autoscaler's minimum, shed load at the edge, turn off inner retries. Then I
> fix the cause: requests set from real usage, and autoscaling on a signal closer to users than CPU."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer.

The interviewer wants to see whether you can run an incident on a Kubernetes service. That means three
things: find which layer is overloaded, stop the user pain, and fix the cause.

What makes this different from a single slow server is that Kubernetes reacts to load on its own. It adds
pods, removes pods, and takes pods out of rotation. Those reactions are meant to help. Under the wrong
settings they make things worse, and they feed each other. So the core of a good answer is naming those
loops and breaking them, not just finding "the slow thing".

Confidence is low.)

## Clarify first

- **Is the whole cluster overloaded, or one service on it?**
- **What does the autoscaler scale on** (CPU, a custom metric), with what minimum, maximum and target?
- **Requests and limits on the hot pods.** Is a cluster autoscaler installed?
- **What changed?** A deploy, a traffic spike, a new tenant, a dependency slowing down?
- **Retries:** which layers retry, and how many times?

## Answer

### Words you need first

- A **pod** is one or more containers that run together on a machine, called a **node**.
- A pod declares a CPU **request** (what the scheduler reserves for it) and a CPU **limit** (the most it
  may use). When it hits the limit, the kernel **throttles** it: it stops running until the next short
  time slice.
- The **HPA** (Horizontal Pod Autoscaler) adds or removes copies of a pod to keep a metric, often average
  CPU, near a target. **Thrash** means it keeps flipping up and down.
- A **readiness probe** is the check that decides whether a pod receives traffic. A pod that fails it is
  taken out of the **Service**, the stable address that spreads traffic over ready pods.
- **RED** means rate, errors and duration per service: what users feel.

### Follow one incident through

Take one service whose p99 latency jumped and whose replica count keeps going up and down. Here is the
order to work in:

| Step | What you do | What it tells you | What to say in the interview |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | RED per service, then what changed | Which service users feel, and the likely trigger | "Most incidents follow a change, so I ask that first." |
| 2 | `kubectl describe hpa` next to p99 and throttling | Whether scaling and latency move together | "If replicas and latency swing in step, the autoscaler is part of the problem." |
| 3 | `kubectl get events`, pods not Running | Readiness failures, evictions, restarts | "Pods failing readiness under load push their traffic onto the rest." |
| 4 | Traces | Which hop holds the latency | "If the time is in the database, more pods won't help." |
| 5 | Mitigate | Stops the loop | "I stop the feedback loop first, then find which loop it was." |
| 6 | Fix | Removes the cause | "Then I change the defaults so it doesn't come back." |

### The commands

```bash
kubectl top nodes; kubectl top pods -A --sort-by=cpu
kubectl describe node <node>       # Conditions and Allocated resources
kubectl get events -A --sort-by=.lastTimestamp | tail -50
kubectl describe hpa <name>        # metric vs target, scaling history
```

Traces tell you which hop holds the latency. Put the HPA replica count, p99 and CPU throttling on one
timeline; the loops below show up as lines that move together.

### Guesses, one at a time

- **Throttle-scale loop.** Pods hit their CPU limits and latency rises. The HPA scales up. New pods take
  time to warm up. Load falls, the HPA scales down, and it starts again. Kubernetes' stabilization window
  exists "to restrict the flapping of replica count", with a 300 s default for scale-down
  ([HPA](https://kubernetes.io/docs/concepts/workloads/autoscaling/horizontal-pod-autoscale/), read
  2026-10-12).
- **Readiness cascade.** Overloaded pods fail readiness and leave the Service. The remaining pods get more
  load, and they fail too.
- **Retry amplification.** Up to three attempts at each of two layers can send the bottom layer up to
  **nine times** the traffic (3 × 3). Say it like this: "Retries at every layer turn a slowdown into an
  outage."
- **Overcommitted nodes.** Requests set far below real usage let the scheduler pack nodes that then fight
  over CPU and memory.
- **A slow dependency** (a database, a provider API) holding threads and connections.

### Mitigate now

- Raise the HPA minimum, so it stops removing pods and the oscillation ends.
- Shed or rate-limit load at the edge.
- Turn off retries in the inner layers.
- Cap how many requests each pod handles at once.

### Fix later

- Set requests from measured usage, so the scheduler stops overpacking nodes.
- Scale on a signal closer to users, such as requests in flight or queue depth, not CPU alone. A throttled
  or I/O-bound service has misleading CPU numbers.
- Keep liveness probes cheap, and make readiness probes independent of dependencies.
- Add a **retry budget**: a cap on retries as a share of total traffic.
- Run load tests that reproduce the thrash, so you can see the fix work.

In plain words: a Senior answer finds the overloaded layer and stops the pain before fixing it. A Staff
answer also explains how Kubernetes' own reactions made it worse, and changes the settings that let them.

## Small-team version

If you run an LLM-backed service on managed Kubernetes, the same loops apply, plus one more: the model
provider's **rate limit**, the cap on how many requests or tokens you may send per minute. Adding pods
doesn't raise your provider quota. So more pods can mean more rejected calls (HTTP 429), not more
throughput.

Three defaults cover it:

- Scale on requests in flight, not CPU. An LLM call spends most of its time waiting, so CPU stays low.
- Cap concurrency to the provider quota.
- Alert on the 429 rate.

If you have one or two services, ask whether a managed platform would remove the HPA problem entirely.
Running and tuning autoscaling is real work for a small team, and it only pays when you have many services
or steady, heavy load.

## Pitfalls

- **Adding nodes before knowing the bottleneck.** If requests, limits or a dependency are the problem, more
  nodes change nothing.
- **Autoscaling on CPU for a throttled or I/O-bound service.** CPU doesn't reflect the load such a service
  is under.
- **Readiness probes that call the database.** A slow database then takes every pod out of rotation at
  once.
- **Retrying at every layer.** The multiplication lands on the layer that is already slowest.

## What they're really asking

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "Over two months, seven customers complained that our AI assistant quoted the wrong version of our product docs.
> Each complaint was filed as its own ticket and patched with a per-customer filter. I grouped them, showed they all
> had one cause, which was that our documents had no version field, and proposed fixing that in the product. It
> pushed a planned integration back two sprints. Wrong-version answers dropped from about thirty a month to two,
> and we deleted the seven filters."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer, so you can build the same shape from your own work.
> (That story and its numbers are invented to show the level of detail. Use your own.)

The interviewer is asking whether you see patterns across customers and push for the lasting fix, or whether you
patch each case as it arrives.

### What makes this question different

Every engineer who has worked near customers has a "customer asked, I built it" story. That is not what this
question wants. It wants the step in between: noticing that several separate complaints are one problem, and
getting the product changed so the problem stops for everyone.

Two words carry the idea:

- A **symptom** is what one customer sees, fixed locally: a setting for that customer, a tweak for that account.
- A **product gap** is the missing capability underneath many symptoms. Fixing it removes the symptoms for every
  customer at once.

The story has to travel from the field (one customer's ticket) to the core product (a change everyone gets), and it
has to say what you displaced to make room.

This prompt comes from a Cohere **forward-deployed engineer** (**FDE**) loop. An FDE works inside customers'
organizations, so they see the same complaint arrive from many directions. The candidate who reported this VP round
says the interviewers wanted you to "identify recurring customer pain, separate local symptoms from product gaps",
that "They're not looking for stories about workarounds", and that the follow-ups were "how did I get buy-in? How
did I prioritize it against other work? What was the outcome?" (<Cite id="s-88b41bdbd6" />).

## Clarify first

- **Where the feedback came from.** "Should this be feedback I gathered directly, or feedback that reached me
  through support or sales?" Either works; say which.
- **Which half.** "Do you want the product change itself or the process of getting it prioritized?" They will want
  both, so confirm you can cover both briefly.
- **Your role.** "Contributed to" allows a story where another team built the fix and you made the case for it.

## Answer

### Words you need first

The example is an AI assistant that answers customers' questions from product documentation.

- **Retrieval** is the step where the assistant searches the documentation for passages relevant to the question
  and pastes them into the text sent to the AI model. The model then answers from those passages.
- **Metadata** is the extra labels stored with each document, such as its product, date or version. Retrieval can
  filter on it.
- A **trace** is the recorded path of one request through the system: what was searched, which passages came back,
  and what the model answered. Reading traces is how you see why an answer was wrong.

### The story's shape

```
The signal ──────── how many customers, over what period, how it reached you
   ▼
The pattern ─────── what you did to see they were one problem
   ▼
Symptom vs gap ──── the local fix, and the missing capability underneath
   ▼
Buy-in ──────────── who owned the roadmap, and what evidence moved them
   ▼
Prioritization ──── what got pushed down the list, and why that was right
   ▼
Outcome ─────────── a before-and-after number
   ▼
What changed ────── how field signal now reaches the roadmap
```

### One story, followed through

| Step | What happens in the story | What to say in the interview |
|---|---|---|
| **1. The signal** | Seven customers, two months, about thirty complaints a month, each filed as unrelated. | "None of them looked related, because each was filed by a different account team." |
| **2. The pattern** | I read the traces for twenty complaints. In every one, retrieval had returned passages from an older product version. | "The traces showed the same thing every time." |
| **3. Symptom vs gap** | The local fix was a per-customer filter. The gap: documents had no version field, so retrieval couldn't tell versions apart. | "The filters treated the symptom. The system had no idea what a version was." |
| **4. Buy-in** | The product lead's roadmap was full. I brought the complaint count, about forty support hours a month, and one renewal that mentioned it. | "I brought numbers the product lead already cared about." |
| **5. Prioritization** | Adding version metadata to the document pipeline displaced a planned integration for two sprints. | "Something real moved down the list, and I said what." |
| **6. Outcome** | Wrong-version answers dropped from about thirty a month to two. The seven filters were deleted. | "Thirty a month to two, and seven special cases gone." |
| **7. What changed** | A monthly review where field engineers bring their top recurring issue. | "Now patterns reach the roadmap on a schedule, not by luck." |

**Step 5 is where most stories fail.** Without a displaced item, it isn't prioritization; it is a wish list. Say it
like this: "To make room, we pushed the integration back two sprints. I told its sponsor myself."

**Step 6 should remove things.** The best proof that you fixed a gap, not a symptom, is the workarounds you got to
delete.

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the numbers (for a Staff-level follow-up)</summary>

- **The drop.** About 30 complaints a month falling to 2 is a drop of 28, about 93%.
- **The case for prioritizing it.** At an assumed 40 support hours a month, two sprints (about four weeks) of one
  engineer's time pays back within a few months of support time saved, before counting the renewal. Every input
  here is an invented value for the story; in a real story, use your own and say which are estimates.
- **Sampling the traces.** Reading 20 of about 60 complaints was enough to see one cause every time. If even a few
  had shown a different cause, you would read more before proposing a single fix.

</details>

## Senior vs Staff

<LevelContrast
  title="Customer feedback into product"
  senior={["Spots a repeated issue, builds or specifies the fix, and measures the drop."]}
  staff={["Builds the channel that turns field signal into roadmap input, and makes the prioritization trade explicit with the product owner."]}
/>

In the example, the Senior version ends with "thirty a month to two". The Staff version adds the monthly review that
brings the next pattern to the roadmap, and the open conversation with the integration's sponsor about what slipped.

## Small-team version

At a 50–500 person company, the AI owner often sits next to support and sees every complaint about the AI feature
directly. The risk is the opposite of a big company's: so much direct feedback that you patch endlessly.

Each patch is often a small edit to the instructions sent to the model for one customer. Those edits pile up into
production changes nobody reviewed.

A strong small-team story shows three actions:

- **You grouped complaints** before fixing any of them.
- **You fixed the cause once,** in the product.
- **You stopped the per-customer edits** and deleted the ones you had.

## Pitfalls

- **One customer's request, built as asked.** That is a delivery story, not a pattern story.
- **A workaround presented as a product improvement.** The interviewers reportedly said they aren't looking for
  workaround stories.
- **No answer to "what did you push down the list?"** Without a cost, the interviewer can't tell whether you
  prioritized at all.
- **An outcome with no number.** "Customers were happier" can't be checked; "thirty a month to two" can.

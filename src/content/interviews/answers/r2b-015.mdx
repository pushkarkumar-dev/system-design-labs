## What they're really asking

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "A GRPO step has three phases: generate several answers per prompt, score them against each other, then train.
> Pipeline parallelism, which splits the model's layers into stages like an assembly line, works well for the
> training phase if I cut the batch into enough micro-batches: with 4 stages and 16 micro-batches, about 19% of the
> time is idle. It works badly for generation, because each new token must pass through every stage before the next
> one can start, so most stages sit idle. So I'd generate with a separate inference engine, copy weights into it
> after each update, and tag every answer with the model version that wrote it."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer.

The interviewer wants to know whether you understand how a GRPO training step actually runs on hardware, and why
one splitting technique suits one half of it and not the other.

### What makes this different from ordinary training

Ordinary training reads a fixed dataset. Every batch has a known size, and the work per batch is predictable.

GRPO (group relative policy optimization) is a kind of **reinforcement learning**: the model is trained on scores
for its own answers rather than on fixed examples. The model writes its own training data first, and only then trains on it.
Writing data means generating text one token at a time, which is slow, sequential and uneven in length. Training is
fast, parallel and regular. One system has to do both, and the two halves want different hardware layouts.

### Words you need first

- **Token**: a piece of a word, about ¾ of a word. Models read and write tokens.
- **Policy**: the model being trained, in reinforcement-learning language. Each update creates a new **policy
  version**.
- **Pipeline parallelism (PP)**: putting different layers on different GPUs, so data flows from stage to stage. The
  idle time at the start and end of each step is the **bubble**.
- **Micro-batch**: a slice of the step's batch. Cutting the batch into many micro-batches keeps more stages busy.

- **Overlap?** "Do we generate, then train, in turns? Or do generation and training overlap?"

## Answer

### GRPO in plain words

GRPO comes from the DeepSeekMath paper ([Shao et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.03300)). Older methods
train a second model, a **value model**, to predict how good an answer will be. GRPO "foregoes the value model,
instead estimating the baseline from group scores".

In practice: for each prompt, sample G answers and score them. Then compare each answer with its own group. Subtract
the group's average score and divide by the group's spread (standard deviation). The result is the answer's
**advantage**: how much better or worse it was than its siblings. Training then makes above-average answers more
likely and below-average ones less likely. A penalty for drifting from a frozen reference copy of the model (a
distance measure called **KL divergence**) is added to the loss rather than to the reward.

### A step has three phases

1. **Generate** G answers per prompt. This is slow, token-by-token work, and answers vary widely in length.
2. **Score**: compute rewards, and run forward passes of the current and reference models to get each token's
   probability.
3. **Train**: forward and backward passes on the sampled tokens, weighted by the advantages, with clipping that
   limits how far one update can move the model.

### Pipeline parallelism in the training phase: works as usual

Split the layers into p stages and the batch into m micro-batches. Run the standard **1F1B** schedule (one forward,
one backward, alternating). The idle share is about (p − 1) ÷ m ([Narayanan et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.04473)).
With **4 stages and 16 micro-batches, about 19%** of the time is idle.

Two GRPO details matter here.

- **A group must be complete before it can train.** The advantage needs all G scores in the group. So a micro-batch
  can start only when all of its groups have been generated and scored.
- **Answer lengths vary.** If micro-batches are filled by number of answers, one with a very long answer becomes a
  straggler, and its stage holds up the line. Pack micro-batches by token count instead.

Say it like this: "In the training phase GRPO is ordinary pipeline training, except that groups must be complete and
micro-batches should be packed by tokens, not by answers."

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the numbers (for a Staff-level follow-up)</summary>

Bubble = (p − 1) ÷ m = (4 − 1) ÷ 16 = 3 ÷ 16 ≈ 19%. Doubling m to 32 halves it to about 9%; adding stages raises it.

</details>

### Pipeline parallelism in the generation phase: works badly

Follow one answer being generated on a 4-stage pipeline:

| Moment | Stage 1 | Stage 2 | Stage 3 | Stage 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| token 1, part 1 | busy | idle | idle | idle |
| token 1, part 2 | idle | busy | idle | idle |
| token 1, part 3 | idle | idle | busy | idle |
| token 1, part 4 | idle | idle | idle | busy |
| token 2, part 1 | busy | idle | idle | idle |

Each new token depends on the one before it, so it must pass through all 4 stages before the next token can start.
With one sequence in flight, only one stage works at a time: three-quarters of the GPUs idle. You need at least p
independent batches of sequences in flight to fill the line. And generation is limited by memory reads, not math,
anyway.

So most designs generate with a **separate inference engine** that splits each layer's math across GPUs instead
(**tensor parallelism**). After each training update they copy the new weights into it, rearranging them from the
training layout to the inference layout (**resharding**).

Say it like this: "Decoding under a pipeline starves the stages, because each token waits for the previous one to
go all the way through. I generate with a separate engine and copy weights in after each update."

### Policy versions and staleness

If generation overlaps training, answers come from policy version v while the learner is already at v + 1 or later.
Tag every answer with its version, and store each token's probability as generated. The clipped objective already
corrects for mild staleness. Drop answers beyond a lag limit.

## Small-team version

Small teams rarely run GRPO. If you try reinforcement learning with automatically checkable rewards (for example,
"did the code pass its tests?") on a small open model:

- Use a library that already separates generation from training.
- Keep it on one machine.
- Measure whether it beats ordinary supervised fine-tuning on your own eval before investing further.

## Pitfalls

- **Treating generation like training micro-batches.** Token-by-token decoding leaves most stages idle.
- **Computing advantages before the whole group finishes.** The group average needs every score.
- **Ignoring length variance across micro-batches.** One long answer becomes the straggler.
- **Not tracking which policy produced which answer.** You can't bound or correct staleness without it.

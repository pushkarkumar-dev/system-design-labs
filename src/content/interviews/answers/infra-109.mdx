## What they're really asking

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "I'd talk to each team to find the problem under their request, because several requests usually share one root
> cause. I'd size each by how many people are blocked and what deadline is at risk, then say clearly what ships now,
> what ships later with a date, and what we won't do and why. For general changes I'd fix upstream rather than keep
> a private fork. And once we've tried internally for a fixed time, I'd go to the maintainers or the vendor with a
> minimal reproduction and the measured impact, so their time is spent on the problem."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer, so you can say it with understanding.

The interviewer is testing three things: how you triage when demand exceeds what you can deliver, how you
communicate a "no", and whether you know when and how to get outside help. It is a working-with-others question,
not a technical one, but the technical setting matters.

### Why this is not a normal feature backlog

You have probably managed a backlog where product managers rank requests. Here the setting is different. The
requests are for changes to **PyTorch**, the most widely used open-source library for building, training and
running AI models. Your team maintains the company's PyTorch setup, and the "users" are other engineering teams
whose work stops when something is missing.

That changes two things. First, you don't own the code: PyTorch is maintained by an outside open-source project,
so any change you make privately becomes your problem on every new release. Second, "immediately" is often real: a
missing feature can block a training run that costs money every hour it waits.

### Words you need first

- **Upstream**: the original open-source project. "Fixing upstream" means getting your change accepted there, so
  every future release includes it.
- **Fork**: your own modified copy of the project. A fork is a maintenance cost you pay on every release, because
  you must re-apply your changes each time.
- **Operator**: one of PyTorch's built-in math building blocks. A missing or slow operator is a common root cause
  behind several different-sounding requests.
- **Minimal reproduction**: the smallest piece of code that shows the problem. It is what makes an outside expert's
  time cheap.

The questions were reported from an NVIDIA Senior MLOps Architect onsite round on working with others (MLOps is
running machine-learning systems in production): "What do you do when lots of users all need conflicting PyTorch
features immediately?" and "Who do you tap once you've exhausted internal resources?" (<Cite id="s-79598dd069" />,
about 2025).

## Clarify first

- **Who are the users?** Internal teams, external customers, or both?
- **Do the requests truly conflict, or only compete?** Two incompatible behaviours need a design decision. Requests
  that just compete for your team's time need an order.
- **What does "immediately" mean?** A blocked launch, or a preference?

## Answer

### The example we will follow

Assume four teams ask in the same week (all invented):

| Request | What they asked for | Need underneath | Decision | What to say |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Team A | "Support our new model layer" | A missing operator | **Fix now, upstream** | "This blocks two teams, so it goes first." |
| Team B | "Training is 30% slower since the upgrade" | The same missing operator falls back to a slow path | **Fixed by A's fix** | "Same root cause as A; one fix unblocks both." |
| Team C | "Change the default behaviour of X" | Conflicts with Team D's workflow | **Make it a configuration option** | "Both behaviours are valid, so it becomes a setting." |
| Team D | "A custom feature only we need" | One team's convenience | **Decline, with a workaround** | "Only one team needs it, and it would be a fork we maintain forever." |

That table is the whole answer in miniature. The steps below explain each column.

### Triage

1. **Find the need under each request.** Talk to the requesters. Several requests often share one root cause, such
   as a missing operator, a version pin (a team stuck on an older version) or a performance regression.
2. **Size impact and urgency.** How many users are blocked, which deadlines and revenue are at risk, and whether a
   workaround exists.
3. **Separate true conflicts from competition for time.** True conflicts, two incompatible behaviours, need a design
   decision: often a configuration option or a separate code path. Competition just needs an order.
4. **Prefer upstream.** For a general change, contributing upstream avoids a private fork you must rebase on every
   release.

Say it like this: "I'd group the requests by the problem underneath them. Usually two or three share a root cause,
and fixing that once unblocks several teams."

### Decide and communicate

Say what ships now, what ships later with a date, and what you won't do and why. Write the decision down where all
requesters can see it, with the reason for the order. Offer workarounds for the deferred ones. A "no" with a reason
and a workaround is far easier to accept than silence.

Say it like this: "Everyone gets an answer and a date, even if the answer is no."

### Escalating outside

Once internal options are exhausted, go to whoever owns the code or knows it best:

- **Upstream maintainers**, through the project's forums or issue tracker.
- **A vendor's support or solutions team**, if you pay for one (for example, the GPU vendor).
- **A paid expert** for a narrow, hard problem.

Do it after a timeboxed internal attempt, not after the deadline. Prepare a minimal reproduction, the versions
involved, and the measured impact ("this blocks two teams and slows training by 30%"), so the expert's time goes on
the problem rather than on understanding it. Going too early wastes goodwill; going too late misses the deadline.

If you have a real example, close with it: the competing requests, how you grouped them, the one you declined, and
the outcome.

## Small-team version

On a small team you are often both the platform and its only maintainer. The "users" are a few product engineers
and a founder. Keep a short, visible request list with the reason for each one's place in the order.

Your outside experts are usually the AI provider's support, the open-source project's issue tracker, and a paid
contractor for a narrow problem. Budget for them, because your own time is not free: at a loaded cost (salary plus
benefits and taxes) of **about $194K a year**, derived in the family walkthrough from US Bureau of Labor
Statistics data, one week of your time costs **about $3,700**, before counting what you didn't ship that week.

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the numbers (for a Staff-level follow-up)</summary>

- **Loaded cost:** BLS May 2025 median software developer wage $135,980 ÷ 0.70 (wages as a share of total
  compensation, June 2026) ≈ $194K a year.
- **One week:** 194,000 ÷ 52 ≈ **$3,700**.

</details>

Default: provider support and the upstream issue tracker first, with a minimal reproduction. Pay for outside help
after a timeboxed attempt, when a week of a contractor costs less than another week of you being stuck.

Say it like this: "A week of my time is worth nearly four thousand dollars, so after a fixed attempt I'd rather pay
an expert for a day than stay stuck."

## Pitfalls

- **Serving whoever asked loudest or most recently.** It rewards pressure, not impact.
- **Forking a framework for one team's request.** You then own that fork on every release, forever.
- **Escalating without a reproduction.** The expert spends their time understanding your setup instead of fixing
  the problem.
- **Saying yes to everything with no dates.** Nobody can plan around it, and trust drops when the dates slip.

## What they're really asking

> **If you only remember one thing, say this in the first minute:**
> "I drove the decision to put all our AI model calls behind one shared gateway, instead of letting each team add
> its own retries. The team that owned our main user path pushed back, and their worry was fair: a new hop on their
> most important request. We agreed a two-week trial where the gateway only watched copies of real traffic, with a
> pass mark set before we started. It passed, provider-caused incidents dropped from about six a quarter to one,
> and one team still hasn't moved its batch jobs."
>
> The rest of this page explains each part of that answer, so you can build the same shape from your own work.
> (That story and its numbers are invented to show the level of detail. Use your own.)

The interviewer is asking two things. Did you, personally, make a technical call that mattered? And did you bring
people with you, rather than going around them?

### What makes this round different

You have probably done behavioral rounds before: "tell me about a time" questions, answered with stories from
your own work. At most companies they are the softer part of the loop, and a tidy, rehearsed story does fine.

This one is different in three ways:

1. **It is partly technical.** The interviewer wants the mechanics of the decision: the architecture before and
   after, the numbers, what could break. A leadership story with no system in it gives them little to score.
2. **They hunt for your part.** Expect repeated questions about what *you* did and how big the team was.
3. **The follow-ups carry the signal.** The opening has to be short enough to leave time for them.

This prompt was reported for an Anthropic Senior/Staff inference loop, in the **hiring-manager round** (the
behavioral round run by the person you would report to). The interviewer "kept drilling on what I personally did,
how big the team was, how I got buy-in on a technical decision", and said she wanted technical detail rather than
"generic leadership stories" (<Cite id="s-4e04a1689c" />).

## Clarify first

This is a behavioral prompt, so you clarify what kind of story they want, not system requirements. Spend 15
seconds, not a minute.

- **Scope.** "Would you like a decision inside my team, or one that crossed teams?" Default to cross-team if you
  are interviewing for Staff.
- **Depth.** "Should I go into the architecture, or stay at the decision level first?" Offer the technical layer;
  the reported interviewer wanted it (<Cite id="s-4e04a1689c" />).
- **How many.** If time is tight, ask whether they want one story in depth or two briefly. Usually it is one.

## Answer

### Words you need first

- **Buy-in** means the people who must live with a decision actually agree to it and act on it, not just that
  they stopped objecting.
- A **shadow trial** sends a copy of real traffic to a new system. The new system does all its work, but its
  results are thrown away, so users are never affected. You compare it with the old path and decide on evidence.
- A **rollback trigger** is a number agreed in advance that, if crossed, means you undo the change. Agreeing it
  first is what makes a risky step safe to try.

### The shape of the answer

Open with the decision in one sentence, then stop after about 90 seconds.

```
Headline ─────────── what you decided, against what, and the result
   ▼
Stakes ───────────── what was at risk, with a number
   ▼
Alternatives ─────── two or three real options, and who wanted each
   ▼
Why this one ─────── the evidence or principle that decided it
   ▼
Buy-in ───────────── the objection at its strongest, and how you agreed
   ▼
Outcome and cost ─── before and after, plus what didn't go to plan
   ▼
What changed ─────── the pattern the team now reuses
   ▼
STOP ─────────────── let the interviewer pick the follow-up
```

The headline is the part most candidates skip. Put the decision, the rejected option and the result in one
sentence, so that if you were cut off there, the interviewer would still know what the story is about.

### One story, followed through

Here is the invented gateway story told in that shape. In the story, a **gateway** is one internal service that
every other service calls instead of calling the AI model provider directly, so retries, spending limits and a
backup provider live in one place.

| Part | What happens in the story | What to say in the interview |
|---|---|---|
| **Headline** | Shared gateway chosen over per-team retries; two product teams adopted it in a quarter. | "I drove the decision to put our model calls behind one shared gateway instead of each team adding retries." |
| **Stakes** | About six incidents a quarter came from provider errors, and spend on model calls was untracked. | "We were losing about six incidents a quarter to one provider's errors." |
| **Alternatives** | Per-team retries (cheapest); a vendor gateway (fast, but customers' questions go through another company); a thin internal gateway. | "Per-team retries was the cheapest option, and a sensible person would have picked it." |
| **Why this one** | One place for retries, a budget and a **fallback provider** (a second AI company's API to switch to when the first fails), without new data-handling approvals. | "I chose it because it put retries, budget and fallback in one place." |
| **Buy-in** | The user-path team feared added delay and a single point of failure. A two-week shadow trial, with a pass mark agreed first: under 20 ms of added delay at the **p99** (the delay 99 of every 100 requests stay under). | "Their worry was right, so we agreed what number would make it safe and measured it on their traffic." |
| **Outcome and cost** | It came in at 8 ms. Incidents fell from about six a quarter to one. It took most of my quarter; one team's batch jobs still bypass it. | "It worked, and it cost more than I planned." |
| **What changed** | Every new model integration starts on the gateway. Shadow trials became how the team tests infra changes. | "The trial pattern outlived the project." |

Told in full sentences, the opening takes about **a minute and a half** (worked out in the box below). That leaves most of the round for the
follow-ups, which is where the decision is made.

<details>
<summary>The arithmetic behind the numbers (for a Staff-level follow-up)</summary>

- **Ninety seconds.** A full opening script for this story runs about 225 words. At an assumed speaking pace of
  150 words a minute, 225 ÷ 150 = 1.5 minutes.
- **The outcome.** Six incidents a quarter falling to one is a drop of five, about 83%. In a real story, say
  whether you counted incidents or minutes of user impact.
- **The pass mark.** 8 ms measured against a 20 ms limit leaves 12 ms of headroom. Saying the limit before the
  result is what makes the number persuasive.

</details>

### The mechanism is the answer

"I explained it and they agreed" is not a mechanism. The credible ones are:

- **A written proposal** with the alternatives laid out, so people argue about options rather than about you.
- **A shadow trial with an exit test** agreed before anyone sees data.
- **A reversible first step** with a rollback trigger.
- **A scoped compromise** that meets the objection.

Say it like this: "We agreed the pass mark before we saw any data, so the result settled it, not me."

### Layers you keep in reserve

Prepare three deeper layers and don't volunteer them:

- **Technical.** The architecture before and after, and the failure modes. For the gateway: what happened when
  the gateway itself was down (services called the provider directly).
- **People.** The words you used: "Your worry is right, so let's not argue about it. Let's agree what number would
  make it safe."
- **Counterfactual.** What result would have made you drop the proposal: "Over 20 ms, and I'd have let that team
  keep its own retries."

### The reported follow-ups

The reported follow-ups were "How did you make sure everyone was aligned?" and "How do you receive feedback and
resolve conflict?" (<Cite id="s-4e04a1689c" />).

For alignment, name a concrete check you made after the decision: "A month later I looked at which services still
called the provider directly, and two batch jobs did." For feedback and conflict, name one time feedback changed
your plan: "The user-path team's objection is why the gateway has a direct-call fallback."

The same candidate wrote "I know I was too wordy and slower to land the point" about this round. They didn't get
an offer, but they don't name this as the reason, so treat it as their hindsight, not feedback from the company.
It is still the easiest mistake to avoid.

In the gateway story, the Senior version ends at "it came in at 8 ms and incidents dropped". The Staff version
adds that the user-path team's objection shaped the design, and that shadow trials became the team's default.

## Small-team version

At a 50–500 person company you may be the only person who owns the company's use of AI. Your best decision
stories often have non-engineers on the other side: the CEO, a sales lead, the support lead.

Show three things:

- **You wrote the decision down even though nobody required it.** One page: the options, the choice, the
  evidence. It takes about an hour.
- **You named the number that would prove you wrong.** For example, "if cost per resolved ticket rises above
  [X] for two weeks, we switch it off".
- **You set a reversal trigger.** Small teams can't afford a long argument about money already spent.

Scope at a small company is measured by consequence, not by how many teams were involved. A decision that changed
how the whole company uses an AI provider is a Staff-scope story with three people in it.

## Pitfalls

- **Context before the decision.** Five minutes of background means the interviewer is still waiting to hear what
  you decided when time runs out.
- **"We" throughout.** If you never say "I", the interviewer can't find your part to score it.
- **A leadership parable.** A story with no architecture or numbers fails at a company that asked for technical
  detail.
- **An opponent who was simply wrong.** It tells the interviewer you can't see why reasonable people disagree.
